How to Use This Pocket Door Rough Opening Calculator

Enter the size of the door slab itself (not a pre-hung unit), pick its thickness, and choose single or double doors. Then select your wall's stud size and drywall thickness. Press Calculate and you get the rough opening width and height, the pocket cavity width, a typical header length, the minimum straight wall run, and a check of whether the wall is deep enough around that door thickness.

The two allowance fields are planning defaults, not a manufacturer's spec. If your pocket frame kit publishes its own rough opening, type those allowances in here or simply use the kit's numbers. Results can be shown in fractions, decimals, or millimeters, and the copy button gives you a text summary to send to your framer.

Why This Matters

A pocket door saves the 10 or so square feet of floor a swinging door sweeps through. That makes it popular for small bathrooms, laundry closets and tight hallways. It is also one of the least forgiving things to frame. A hinged door can be planed or shimmed. A pocket door lives inside the wall, so a rough opening that is 3/4″ too narrow or a pocket that is out of plumb means opening the drywall back up.

The numbers matter most before the walls are closed in. A 32″ door needs about 65″ of framed width, and a pair of 30″ panels needs about 122″. Those are long spans for a header, and they decide where studs, outlets and ducts can go. Plumbing, wiring and ducts can't run through the pocket zone. A contractor planning a bathroom remodel, a homeowner moving a wall, or a designer drawing a plan can use the figures to confirm the wall is long enough and deep enough before ordering the frame. The thickness check also catches a common surprise: many standard frames are built around 1-3/8″ doors, and a 1-3/4″ door in a 2×4 wall may need a different kit.

How It's Calculated

The formulas are common planning rules, not code requirements. No building code publishes pocket door framing dimensions, so the manufacturer's installation sheet always wins. Each result below uses the inputs shown, with no hidden terms:

Pocket cavity (each) = door width + width allowance
RO width, single = 2 × door width + width allowance
RO width, double = 4 × door width + 2 × width allowance
RO height = door height + height allowance
Header length = RO width + 3″ (1-1/2″ trimmer each end)
Minimum wall run = RO width + 6″ (king + trimmer each end)
Finished wall = stud depth + 2 × drywall
Space beside slab = (stud depth − door thickness) ÷ 2

The pocket is the half of the opening where the slab disappears, so its width is the slab width plus the allowance for the hardware stop and bumper. The other half is the clear opening, equal to one slab width. The cavity width plus the opening width gives the rough opening width.

The wall-depth check compares what is left inside the stud cavity once the slab is in place. Below 3/4″ per side the tool shows a Stop result, because split studs, frame faces and clearance need room there. That 3/4″ figure is a conservative planning minimum, not a published standard. Slabs over 1-3/4″ thick, and 1-3/4″ doors in general, always get a Caution to confirm the kit's rating.

Every result is rounded in the conservative direction: dimensions up to the next 1/16″ (or 1 mm), and the remaining space down. The header length is the framing length only. Header sizing depends on load, species and span, so it is not calculated here. In a load-bearing wall, size the header from the tables in IRC R602.7 (or your local amendment), or have an engineer do it. Local codes vary, and this tool is not a permit, engineering or inspection approval.

Worked Example

Take a 32″ × 80″ × 1-3/8″ interior slab, one pocket, a 2×4 wall with 1/2″ drywall, and the default allowances of 1″ width and 2-1/2″ height.

  1. Pocket cavity = 32 + 1 = 33″.
  2. Rough opening width = 2 × 32 + 1 = 65″.
  3. Rough opening height = 80 + 2.5 = 82-1/2″.
  4. Header length = 65 + 3 = 68″. Minimum wall run = 65 + 6 = 71″.
  5. Finished wall = 3.5 + 2 × 0.5 = 4-1/2″.
  6. Space beside the slab = (3.5 − 1.375) ÷ 2 = 1-1/16″ per side, so the fit check passes.

Switch to a 1-3/4″ slab and the space beside it drops to (3.5 − 1.75) ÷ 2 = 7/8″. That is still above the planning minimum, but the tool flags a Caution to confirm the kit is rated for that thickness. With two 30″ panels and the same allowances, the opening becomes 4 × 30 + 2 × 1 = 122″, each pocket is 31″, the header is 125″ and the wall run is 128″.

Tips & Common Mistakes

  • Start with the kit, not the calculator. Buy or download the frame's instruction sheet first. Its published rough opening replaces the default allowances here.
  • Use slab size, not the finished passage. Clear walk-through width is smaller than the slab, often by about 1-1/2″ for the edge pull and stop. If you need a 32″ clear opening for accessibility, you will usually need a wider slab.
  • Add the finished floor. If tile or hardwood goes in after framing, add its thickness to the height allowance or the door will drag.
  • Keep utilities out of the pocket. Run wires, pipes and ducts around the cavity and mark it on the plan. Screws and nails driven through the drywall can hit the door.
  • Don't skip the header. A pocket door opening is almost twice the door width. In a bearing wall it needs a properly sized header and framing, and a 2×4 pocket frame's split studs are not automatically structural.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the rough opening for a 32×80 pocket door?

With this tool's default allowances, a 32″ × 80″ slab needs a rough opening of 65″ wide × 82-1/2″ tall, with a 33″ pocket. Manufacturers differ by a fraction of an inch, so confirm against your kit's instructions before framing.

Can I put a pocket door in a load-bearing wall?

It is possible, but the wide opening needs a correctly sized header and full-height support at each end. Size it from IRC R602.7 or your local tables, or have an engineer do it. Do not assume the split studs in the frame carry load.

Do I need a 2×6 wall for a pocket door?

Not usually. Most 1-3/8″ interior doors fit frames made for 2×4 walls. Thicker or heavier doors often need a heavy-duty frame, and some are easier to fit in a 2×6 wall. Always choose a frame made for your actual wall thickness.

How do I figure out a double pocket door rough opening?

Each panel slides into its own pocket, so the width is 4 × panel width plus the allowance for both pockets. Two 30″ panels with a 1″ allowance each give 122″. Select "Double doors" and the tool does this for you.

Can I run electrical or plumbing in a pocket door wall?

No, not through the pocket zone. The cavity is taken up by the door and frame, and fasteners through the wall can hit the door. Route wiring and pipes in the adjacent walls and plan outlet locations before framing.

How much taller than the door should the rough opening be?

Commonly about 2 to 2-1/2″ above the slab for the track, header and floor clearance. This tool defaults to 2-1/2″. Increase it for thick flooring, and use your kit's own figure if it publishes one.
