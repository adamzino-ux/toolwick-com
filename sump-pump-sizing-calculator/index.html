How to Use This Sump Pump Sizing Calculator

Enter your pit shape and dimensions, the distance your float switch travels between pump-on and pump-off, the water rise rate you measured with the pump unplugged, and your total discharge head. Pick a safety factor (+50% is the default) and press Calculate. You get the minimum flow in GPM and GPH, a recommended horsepower class, a comparison of five pump classes at your head, and an estimate of pump run time and discharge pipe size.

Why This Matters

A sump pump rarely fails on a dry day. It fails in the one storm that sends water into the pit faster than the pump can move it. A 6 GPM pump facing a 9 GPM inflow loses ground every minute, and the pit overflows within a few minutes. Finished basements often carry $20,000–$50,000 of drywall, flooring and belongings, so sizing the pump correctly is cheap insurance.

Many people shop by the headline GPH rating on the box. That number is measured at zero feet of head, where the pump lifts nothing. At 10–15 feet a "3,600 GPH" pump may deliver 2,000–2,500 GPH. Oversizing has its own cost: a pump far larger than the pit can empty it in seconds and short-cycle, which wears out the float switch and motor.

The tool suits homeowners replacing a failed pump, buyers checking whether an existing pump is adequate, and builders planning a new pit. Typical residential storm inflow ranges from 2–10 GPM, but a high water table can push it past 30 GPM.

How It's Calculated

The calculator follows common industry practice and generic manufacturer-style performance curves. It does not follow a code table. Plumbing codes (for example the sump and ejector provisions of IRC chapter 30) and the NEC cover installation, check valves, discharge location and the electrical circuit, and those rules vary by location.

Area (in²) = π × (diameter ÷ 2)² or length × width
Gallons per inch = Area ÷ 231
Peak inflow (GPM) = Gallons per inch × rise rate (in/min)
Required flow (GPM) = Peak inflow × safety factor
Specified flow = Required flow rounded UP to a whole GPM
GPH = Specified GPM × 60

One US gallon is 231 cubic inches. The safety factor is applied to the flow, not to pump efficiency. Efficiency affects electrical power draw, not how many gallons a pump must move to keep up with the inflow.

Head matters. The calculator rounds your head up to the next whole foot. It reads each pump class's typical flow at that head from a performance curve (for example a 1/3 HP class falls from about 43 GPM at 10 ft to 32 GPM at 15 ft and 19 GPM at 20 ft). The recommended pump is the smallest class whose flow at your head is at least 110% of the specified flow. The extra 10% allows for generic curves, wear and clogged intake screens. If no class qualifies, the tool says so instead of guessing.

Run time per cycle = stroke volume ÷ (pump flow at head − inflow). The stroke volume is gallons per inch × your float range. The discharge pipe is the smallest of 1.5", 2" or 3" PVC (Schedule 40 inside diameters) that keeps velocity at or below 8 ft/s.

These pump curves are typical, not data for any specific model. Always check the manufacturer's published curve at your head before buying.

Worked Example

Take a 24-inch round pit, 30 inches deep, with an 8-inch float range. During a storm you measure an 8 in/min rise with the pump unplugged. Total head is 14 ft, and you choose the +50% safety factor.

  • Area = π × 12² = 452.4 in², so gallons per inch = 452.4 ÷ 231 = 1.958 gal/in.
  • Peak inflow = 1.958 × 8 = 15.67 GPM.
  • Required flow = 15.67 × 1.5 = 23.50 GPM, rounded up to 24 GPM (1,440 GPH).
  • At 14 ft, the 1/4 HP class gives about 15.6 GPM, which is too small. The 1/3 HP class gives about 34.2 GPM, above the 26.4 GPM needed (24 × 1.1), so the answer is 1/3 HP.
  • Stroke volume = 1.958 × 8 = 15.67 gal. Fill time between cycles is 1.0 min, and run time is 15.67 ÷ (34.2 − 15.67) = 0.85 min, or about 51 seconds.
  • Pipe: at 34.2 GPM, 1.5" PVC runs at about 5.4 ft/s, under the 8 ft/s limit, so 1.5" is enough.

Tips & Common Mistakes

  • Measure the rise rate in a real storm. Unplug the pump, mark the pit wall and time the rise. A dry-day measurement can understate peak inflow several times over.
  • Count the full head. Add the vertical lift to the outlet, then a conservative allowance of about 1 ft per 10 ft of horizontal pipe and around 2 ft for the check valve and fittings. Underestimating head is the most common sizing error.
  • Don't shop by the box GPH. Compare the manufacturer's curve at your head to the GPM shown in your results.
  • Add a backup. Storms often take out the power. A battery or water-powered backup pump, plus a high-water alarm, covers the failure mode that floods most basements.
  • Keep the outlet circuit code-compliant. Check the NEC and local rules for the receptacle, and don't discharge where local rules prohibit it, such as into the sanitary sewer.

Frequently Asked Questions

What size sump pump do I need for my basement?

Most homes are well served by a 1/3 HP pump, which delivers roughly 40 GPM at 10 ft of head. Homes with high water tables, large drainage areas or long discharge runs often need 1/2 HP or more. The calculator gives a figure for your own pit and head.

How do I measure my sump pit's water rise rate?

During or right after a heavy rain, unplug the pump and time how far the water rises. Mark the pit wall with tape, time two or three minutes, and divide inches by minutes. Take the measurement during your worst storm and do not average it with dry days.

What is total head and how do I calculate it?

Total head is the vertical distance from the water in the pit to the discharge outlet, plus friction losses in the pipe and fittings. A quick conservative estimate is vertical lift + 1 ft per 10 ft of horizontal run + about 2 ft for the check valve and fittings.

Should I choose a 1/3 HP or 1/2 HP sump pump?

Choose by the pump curve rather than the horsepower label. If the 1/3 HP curve meets your required GPM at your head with a margin, it is enough and costs less to run. If it only barely meets it, or your storms are severe, step up to 1/2 HP.

Why is the GPH result lower than the rating on pump boxes?

Box ratings are measured at zero head. Your result is the flow the pump must deliver at your actual head, which is the number that counts when you compare pump curves.

Do I need a backup sump pump?

If your basement is finished, or the pit runs heavily in storms, yes. Power outages and pump failures tend to happen together with flooding. A backup pump sized for the same flow gives you redundancy, and a high-water alarm tells you when something is wrong.
