Press Calculate to get the concrete to order in cubic yards or cubic meters, bag counts, gravel, rebar and form lumber, plus a thickness comparison table. Open the optional section to add the filled tub weight for a load check, and local prices for a cost estimate.

Why This Matters

A hot tub is one of the heaviest things you will ever put on a patio. A 7-foot square tub holding water and six adults can top 5,000 lb, concentrated on about 49 square feet. Set that on bare soil or a thin, unreinforced slab and you invite settling, cracking and a tilted shell, which can strain plumbing and void the manufacturer's warranty. A properly sized pad spreads the load and stays level for years.

Ordering the right amount matters too. Ready-mix is sold by the cubic yard, often $150 to $200 or more once delivery is included, and many suppliers add a short-load fee on small orders. Run short mid-pour and you get a cold joint, a weak seam right under the heaviest part of the tub. Over-order by a full yard and you pay for concrete you have to dump. DIYers pouring a weekend slab, deck builders pricing a spa pad and homeowners comparing 4-inch and 6-inch slabs all need the same number. For an 8 ft × 8 ft pad, going from 4 to 6 inches adds about 0.4 cubic yards.

How It's Calculated

The pad is the tub footprint plus the border on every side. Volume is the pad area times thickness, increased by your extra percentage.

Pad length = tub length + 2 × border
Rectangle: V = pad length × pad width × thickness
Round: V = π/4 × pad diameter² × thickness
Order = V × (1 + extra%) ÷ 27 = cubic yards

Cubic feet divide by 27 to give cubic yards, and multiply by 0.0283168 for cubic meters. The order quantity is rounded up to the next quarter cubic yard (or 0.1 m³ in metric). Bag counts use typical yields of 0.60 ft³ for an 80-lb bag, 0.45 ft³ for 60 lb and 0.30 ft³ for 40 lb, rounded up to whole bags. Poured concrete is taken as 150 lb per cubic foot.

Gravel is area × depth plus 15% for compaction, at about 1.4 tons per cubic yard. Rebar assumes bars in both directions at your spacing, 3 inches of cover from each edge and 10% extra for laps and cut waste. Round pads are estimated as their bounding square, which runs slightly high. If you enter the filled tub weight, tub load is that weight divided by the tub footprint, and soil pressure is tub plus slab weight divided by pad area.

These figures follow common manufacturer guidance (a reinforced slab of at least 4 inches over about 4 inches of compacted crushed stone) and a rule-of-thumb comparison to the 1,500 psf lowest presumptive soil bearing value in IRC Table R401.4.1. They are planning estimates, not a building code, an engineering design, or permit or inspection approval. Local codes, frost depth and soil conditions vary, so confirm with your tub manufacturer and local building department.

Worked Example

A 7 ft × 7 ft tub, 6-inch border, 6-inch slab, 4 inches of gravel, 10% extra and a 12-inch rebar grid (the calculator's defaults):

  1. Pad size: 7 + 2 × 0.5 = 8 ft each way, so the area is 64 ft².
  2. Volume: 64 × 0.5 ft = 32 ft³, or 32 ÷ 27 = 1.19 yd³.
  3. With 10% extra: 32 × 1.10 = 35.2 ft³ = 1.30 yd³.
  4. Rounded up to the next quarter yard: order 1.50 yd³.
  5. Bagged alternative: 35.2 ÷ 0.60 = 58.7, so 59 bags of 80 lb (about 4,720 lb of bags).
  6. Gravel: 64 × (4 ÷ 12) = 21.3 ft³, × 1.15 = 24.5 ft³ = 0.91 yd³, about 1.27 tons.
  7. Rebar: 7.5 ft effective each way gives 8 bars per direction, 2 × 8 × 7.5 = 120 ft; plus 10% is 132 ft, or 14 bars of 10 ft.

Tips & Common Mistakes

  • Read the tub manual first. Thickness, concrete strength, reinforcement and pad size come from the manufacturer. If the manual asks for more than this tool's defaults, follow the manual.
  • Plan electrical before the pour. Set conduit sleeves in the slab or trench where your electrician says. Spa wiring is covered by NEC Article 680, and disconnect and GFCI rules are a licensed electrician's job.
  • Prepare the subgrade. Strip topsoil and organic material, compact the soil, then add and compact the gravel. A soft or uneven base eats concrete and causes cracking.
  • Don't cut the extra to zero. Forms bow, subgrades dip and some concrete stays in the truck chute. Keep 5 to 10%, and more for a rough excavation.
  • Support the rebar mid-slab. Rebar lying on the gravel does little. Use chairs or dobies so it sits in the middle of the thickness, and keep it off the edges.
  • Cure properly. Keep the slab damp and covered for several days, and check how long your manufacturer wants you to wait before setting the tub.

Frequently Asked Questions

How thick should a concrete pad for a hot tub be?

Most manufacturers call for at least 4 inches of reinforced concrete. Many installers pour 5 to 6 inches for heavier tubs, soft soil or freeze-thaw climates. This calculator defaults to 6 inches and won't go below 4.

How much concrete do I need for a 7x7 hot tub?

With a 6-inch border the pad is 8 ft × 8 ft. At 6 inches thick that is 32 cubic feet, about 1.19 cubic yards, and with 10% extra you would order 1.5 cubic yards. At 4 inches the same pad needs about 0.79 cubic yards before the extra.

How many bags of concrete for a hot tub pad?

An 80-lb bag yields about 0.60 cubic feet, so one cubic yard takes about 45 bags. The 8 × 8 × 6-inch example needs 59 bags with 10% extra. Above roughly one cubic yard, ready-mix is usually easier, because you must mix and place everything before it sets.

Do I need rebar or wire mesh under a hot tub?

Most tub manufacturers require reinforcement, typically #3 rebar on a 12-inch grid or welded wire mesh held mid-slab. It keeps cracks tight rather than adding load capacity. Check your installation manual for the exact requirement.

How big should the concrete pad be compared with the hot tub?

The pad should be at least as large as the tub footprint. Most installers extend it 6 inches or more past the tub on every side, which also gives room for the cabinet and service access. Use a larger border if you want a landing or steps.

How long before I can put a hot tub on a new concrete pad?

Many manufacturers say to wait at least 7 days, though concrete only reaches its full design strength at about 28 days. A filled tub is a heavy, concentrated load, so follow your manual and err on the longer side in cold weather.
