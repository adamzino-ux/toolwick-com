How to Use This Generator Transfer Switch Load Calculator

Tap the quick-add buttons for the appliances you want running during an outage, or enter a custom item with its running watts, starting watts and quantity. Choose a safety margin and your system voltage, then press Calculate. You get the minimum generator size in kilowatts, the next standard size up, and the transfer switch amperage, with tables showing how each number was reached.

Why This Matters

A generator that is too small stalls the moment your well pump or air conditioner tries to start, and repeated overloads can damage both the generator and your appliances. One that is far too large costs thousands more to buy and install. A 22 kW standby unit often runs $10,000 or more installed, while a 7.5–10 kW unit backing up a furnace, refrigerator, sump pump and lights can cost about half that.

People looking for a generator size calculator for house backup usually face one of three situations: a portable generator with a manual transfer switch on essential circuits, an automatic standby unit for the whole home, or a storm-prone area where a three-day outage means spoiled food and a flooded basement. The question is always the same: how many watts run at once, and how big is the largest motor-start spike? Transfer switch sizing matters just as much. It is the device that isolates your house from the utility so you cannot back-feed power onto lines that line crews believe are dead. A switch rated too low can overheat under continuous load, so this tool always rounds up.

How It's Calculated

Every step below is exactly what the calculator does:

  1. Total running watts = sum of (running watts × quantity) for every appliance.
  2. Largest single-motor surge = the biggest value of (starting watts − running watts) for one unit. Quantity is not applied here, because identical motors start one at a time. Three window ACs add to your running load, but you only need to cover one extra starting spike.
  3. Peak load = total running watts + largest single-motor surge.
  4. Required capacity = peak load × (1 + safety margin). Minimum kW = required capacity ÷ 1,000, rounded up to the next 0.01 kW.
  5. Recommended generator = the next standard size at or above the minimum: 3.5, 5, 7.5, 10, 12.5, 15, 17.5, 20, 22, 25, 30, 35, 40, 45 or 50 kW. Above 50 kW the tool tells you the load needs a commercial-class design.
  6. Transfer switch amperage is the larger of two currents: the running load × 1.25 ÷ voltage (the 125% continuous-load convention), and the recommended generator's rated output (kW × 1,000 ÷ voltage). The result is rounded up to a standard size: 30, 40, 50, 60, 100, 125, 150, 200, 225 or 400 A. Above 400 A the tool flags an engineered design.
Required W = (Running W + largest single-unit surge) × (1 + margin)
Switch amps = max(Running W × 1.25 ÷ V, Generator W ÷ V)

Starting surge is deliberately left out of the transfer switch step. It lasts only one to three seconds and is already covered by the generator's size and the margin, while the switch must carry the sustained current. Also make sure the switch is never rated below the generator's output breaker, and if you use a service-entrance-rated whole-house switch, size it to your main breaker as your electrician directs.

Tips & Common Mistakes

  • Use nameplate data when you can. Starting watts for a motor are roughly locked-rotor amps × voltage. Typical values are 2–3× running watts for pumps and fridges and 3× or more for central air conditioners.
  • Check the fuel rating. Natural gas typically delivers about 10–15% less rated output than propane from the same engine, and altitude can cost roughly 3–4% per 1,000 ft. Use the manufacturer's rating for your fuel, or raise the margin. Cold weather mainly hurts battery starting and propane vaporization in a low tank, so keep the battery charged and the tank well above empty.
  • Don't forget hidden loads. Well pumps, garage door openers and furnace igniters are easy to miss. Electric ranges, dryers, water heaters and electric heat each draw 3,000–5,000 W or more, so leave them off the backed-up circuits unless you have sized for them.
  • Stagger motor starts. Identical motors on timers can try to start together when power returns. A soft-start kit on your air conditioner and a load-management module greatly reduce the surge you must cover.
  • Never back-feed through a wall outlet. Use a code-compliant transfer switch or interlock. Run portable generators outdoors, well away from windows and doors, and install carbon monoxide alarms inside.

Frequently Asked Questions

What size generator do I need for a 2,000 sq ft house?
Square footage is a weak predictor, because the appliances you run matter far more. Backing up essential circuits (furnace, refrigerator, sump pump, lights, a few outlets) usually takes 7.5–12.5 kW. Adding central air commonly pushes that to 15–22 kW, and a whole-house setup with electric cooking and drying can need 22–30 kW or more. List your actual loads in the calculator for a real number.
What is the difference between running watts and starting watts?
Running watts are the continuous power an appliance draws. Starting (surge) watts are the brief spike a motor needs to get moving, typically 2–3 times higher and lasting only a second or two. Your generator must handle the total of all running loads plus the spike from the largest motor starting.
Do I need a transfer switch for a generator?
If you power hardwired circuits, yes. NEC 702.5 requires transfer equipment that prevents your home from back-feeding the utility, and most inspectors require a permit for the installation. Running extension cords from a portable generator to appliances does not use a switch, but it cannot safely power a furnace, well pump or sump pump.
What size transfer switch do I need for a 7,500-watt generator?
At 240 V, 7,500 W is 31.25 amps, which is just over 30 A, so this calculator rounds up to a 40-amp switch. Some manufacturers pair 7,500 W units with a 30-amp inlet and breaker, so follow the generator and switch instructions and your electrician's judgment.
Why isn't starting surge used to size the transfer switch?
The surge lasts one to three seconds and is handled by the generator's capacity and its breaker. The switch contacts must carry the sustained current, so it is sized to the continuous load and the generator's rated output. Sizing it to the surge would often oversize it without any safety benefit.
How much safety margin should I use?
20% is a sensible default. Use 25–30% if you run lots of motors, live at high altitude, burn natural gas, or expect to add appliances later. A generator running near 100% of its rating for days wears out faster and has no room for error.
