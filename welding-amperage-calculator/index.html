How to Use This Welding Amperage Calculator

Choose your welding process and base metal, then enter the material thickness in inches or millimeters, or drag the slider. Optionally pick a specific wire, rod or tungsten size and your welding position; leave the size on Auto and the tool selects one that suits the thickness. Press Calculate to see a starting amperage, a working range, an estimated arc voltage and a conservative breaker and wire size for the supply. The table underneath compares amperage for common sheet gauges and plate sizes.

Why This Matters

Amperage is the setting that decides whether a weld actually fuses. Too low and the bead sits on top of the plate with cold lap and no penetration. Too high and you blow holes in sheet metal, undercut the edges and warp the part. Fabricators, farm and ranch repair welders, auto hobbyists and students all hit the same problem: the chart on the machine covers one wire, and the part in front of you is a different thickness.

Consider a 16-gauge (0.060 in.) steel exhaust hanger and a 3/8 in. trailer tongue. The hanger needs roughly 60 amps with 0.023 in. wire. The tongue needs 250 amps or more, a beveled joint and several passes. Starting from a sensible number saves wire, rod and gas, and it breaks the "turn it up until it works" habit that leads to burn-through. The tool also checks your electrical supply. A welder that draws 28 amps from a 120-volt outlet will trip a 15- or 20-amp breaker, and knowing that before you buy a machine or run a circuit saves real money.

How It's Calculated

For MIG, flux-core and TIG the calculator uses the long-standing shop rule of about one amp per 0.001 in. of material thickness:

Amps = thickness (in) × 1,000 × metal factor × position factor

The metal factor is 1.0 for mild steel, 0.9 for stainless (it conducts heat poorly, so it needs less current) and 1.2 for aluminum (it sheds heat quickly). Position factors are 1.0 flat, 0.95 horizontal, 0.9 vertical-up and 0.85 overhead, because a smaller puddle is easier to control against gravity. The result is capped at the top of the chosen wire or tungsten range, and the tool tells you when multiple passes are needed.

Stick welding works differently. Amperage is limited by the electrode, so the tool interpolates inside typical manufacturer ranges for E6010/E6011, E6013 and E7018 rods in 3/32, 1/8, 5/32 and 3/16 in. sizes, then applies the position factor. Always confirm against the range printed on your rod box.

The circuit check is a conservative approximation, not the NEC Article 630 method. Article 630 sizes the supply conductors and overcurrent protection from the welder's nameplate primary current and duty cycle, and this tool has no nameplate to work from. Instead it estimates the arc voltage with NEMA-style load-voltage formulas (stick 20 + 0.04 × A, MIG and flux-core 14 + 0.05 × A, TIG 10 + 0.04 × A), then the input current:

Input amps = arc volts × output amps ÷ (0.75 efficiency × supply volts)

The breaker suggestion is 125% of that input current, rounded up to the next standard size (NEC 240.6). The copper conductor is the smallest wire whose NEC 310.16 60 °C ampacity meets or exceeds that breaker. On 120 V, the tool warns whenever the 125% figure is more than 20 A. Local codes vary, and your welder's nameplate and manual always override these numbers. Have a licensed electrician verify any installation.

Worked Example

You are MIG welding 1/8 in. (0.125 in., 3.18 mm) mild steel flat, on a 240 V circuit, with Auto wire size.

  • Amps: 0.125 × 1,000 × 1.0 × 1.0 = 125 A.
  • Wire: 125 A falls under the 140 A ceiling for 0.030 in. wire, so Auto picks 0.030 in. (typical range 40–190 A).
  • Working range: 90% to 110% of 125 A, or 113–138 A.
  • Arc voltage: 14 + 0.05 × 125 = 20.3 V.
  • Input current: 20.25 × 125 ÷ (0.75 × 240) = 14.1 A.
  • Circuit: 14.1 × 1.25 = 17.6 A, rounded up to a 20 A breaker with 12 AWG copper.

On 120 V the same weld draws about 28 A. Times 1.25 that is 35 A, which rounds up to a 40 A breaker. The tool warns you that this is more than a standard 20 A household circuit can supply.

Tips & Common Mistakes

  • Treat the number as a starting point. Run a bead on scrap of the same thickness, then adjust 5–10 amps at a time based on bead shape and penetration.
  • Match wire size to thickness. Thin wire on thick plate lacks the current range; thick wire on sheet burns through. Let Auto pick if unsure.
  • Gauge is not thickness. Steel and aluminum gauges differ. Measure with calipers and enter the decimal value.
  • Do not skip joint prep on thick plate. Above about 3/8 in., bevel the edges and use multiple passes; maximum amps alone will not give full penetration.
  • Respect duty cycle. A machine rated 140 A at 20% duty can weld only two minutes in ten at full output. Heavy plate often needs a larger machine.
  • Never oversize a breaker to stop it tripping. A tripping breaker means the circuit is too small for the load; fix the circuit.

Frequently Asked Questions

How many amps do I need to weld 1/8 inch steel?

For MIG, flux-core or TIG, about 125 amps flat (the one-amp-per-thousandth rule). For stick with a 1/8 in. E7018 rod, the tool suggests about 100 A within the rod's 90–150 A range. Use the working range as your tuning window.

How many amps per inch of thickness?

Roughly 1,000 amps per inch, or 1 amp per 0.001 in., for mild steel. Stainless needs about 10% less and aluminum about 20% more. Very thick material is welded in several passes rather than one very hot pass.

Can I weld on a 120V outlet?

Yes, for thin material. With the tool's conservative sizing (125% of estimated input must stay within 20 A), a 120 V machine tops out at about 80 A for MIG or flux-core, about 100 A for TIG and about 65 A for stick. For MIG that suits steel up to roughly 5/64 in. (2 mm). The calculator shows a warning whenever the result goes beyond that.

What happens if my amperage is too high or too low?

Too high causes burn-through, undercut, spatter and distortion. Too low causes a tall, ropey bead, poor fusion and a stuttering arc. If the bead sits on top of the metal rather than wetting into it, raise the amps slightly.

Why does the calculator suggest a larger breaker than my welder's manual?

The tool assumes continuous operation and a conservative 75% efficiency, applies a 125% factor and rounds up. NEC Article 630 instead sizes the supply from your welder's nameplate current and duty cycle, so the nameplate and manual take priority. Follow them and have a licensed electrician verify the installation.

Does welding position change the amperage?

Yes. Vertical and overhead welds use a smaller, cooler puddle so it does not sag, so this calculator reduces amperage by 10–15% for those positions. Flat welding uses the highest current.
