Why This Matters

Getting the roof pitch right isn't just a geometry exercise — it has real consequences for cost, materials, and code compliance. A 6/12 pitch means for every 12 inches of horizontal run, the roof rises 6 inches vertically, creating a 26.6° angle. That's considered a standard residential pitch across most of North America.

Consider a 24-foot-wide, 40-foot-long house with a 6/12 pitch. Each common rafter measures about 13.4 feet before any overhang. Add a 12-inch eave overhang and the rafter grows to roughly 14 feet 6 inches (14.53 ft) — multiply that across 80+ rafters on a typical house and material costs add up fast if you miscalculate even a few inches.

Pitch also drives water runoff, snow load capacity, attic livability, and which roofing material is even allowed. Low-slope roofs under 3/12 typically require different underlayment and materials than standard-slope roofs, while anything above 9/12 usually calls for a safety harness during installation. Knowing your exact numbers before ordering lumber or shingles can save hundreds of dollars and prevent surprises on the job site.

How It's Calculated

The math behind roof pitch is straightforward trigonometry. Here are the core formulas:

  • Pitch ratio: Rise ÷ Run × 12 (expressed as x/12)
  • Angle: θ = arctan(Rise ÷ Run), converted to degrees
  • Rafter factor: √(12² + Pitch²) ÷ 12 — the rafter length produced by every foot of run
  • Rafter length (no overhang): Run × Rafter Factor
  • Rafter length (with overhang): (Run + Overhang in feet) × Rafter Factor
  • Roof area (one side, no overhang): Rafter length × Building length
  • Total roof area: 2 × Rafter length × Building length

The rafter factor is simply the Pythagorean theorem applied per foot of run: a triangle with a 12-inch base and a "pitch"-inch height has a hypotenuse of √(12²+pitch²) inches, which becomes the sloped length of rafter needed for every foot of horizontal run.

Worked Example

Let's calculate a real roof: a house with a 24-foot span, 6/12 pitch, 40-foot building length, and a 12-inch eave overhang — entered on the Span & Pitch tab above.

  • Step 1 — Run: Span ÷ 2 = 24 ÷ 2 = 12 ft
  • Step 2 — Rise: Run × (Pitch ÷ 12) = 12 × (6 ÷ 12) = 6 ft
  • Step 3 — Angle: arctan(6 ÷ 12) = 26.57°
  • Step 4 — Rafter factor: √(12² + 6²) ÷ 12 = √180 ÷ 12 = 1.1180
  • Step 5 — Rafter length (no overhang): 12 × 1.1180 = 13.42 ft (about 13 ft 5 in)
  • Step 6 — Rafter length (with 12-in overhang): (12 + 1) × 1.1180 = 14.53 ft (about 14 ft 6.4 in)
  • Step 7 — Roof area: One side = 13.42 × 40 = 536.6 sq ft. Both sides with overhang = 2 × 14.53 × 40 ≈ 1,162.8 sq ft
  • Step 8 — Roofing squares: 1,162.8 ÷ 100 × 1.10 waste = 12.79, rounded up to 13 squares

Plug those same numbers into the calculator above and you'll see an identical result — pitch 6.00/12, angle 26.57°, and a rafter length (with overhang) of 14 ft 6.4 in.

Tips & Common Mistakes

  • Span vs. Run: The run is half the span for a symmetrical gable roof. Confusing the two will double your rise calculation.
  • Add waste factor: Order 10–15% extra roofing material to account for cuts, waste, and ridge/hip details.
  • Check local building codes: Many jurisdictions have minimum pitch requirements for specific roofing materials — asphalt shingles typically need at least 2/12 or 3/12.
  • Rafter length is structural length: This calculator gives the rafter's sloped length. Add bird's mouth cut depth and any fascia tail to determine actual lumber stock length needed.
  • Vertical rise isn't total roof height: The "Vertical Rise" figure runs from the top of the wall plate to the ridge — it doesn't include wall height, so it's not the same as overall building height above grade.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a good roof pitch for a house?

Most residential homes use pitches between 4/12 and 9/12 (18.4° to 36.9°). A 6/12 pitch is extremely common — it sheds water well, is safe to work on with care, and offers usable attic space. Pitches below 3/12 are considered low-slope and above 12/12 are considered steep-slope, each requiring different roofing materials and installation methods.

What's the difference between pitch and slope?

In common usage they're interchangeable, but technically "pitch" is the ratio of rise to total span, while "slope" is rise over run expressed as x/12. The roofing industry almost universally uses the x/12 slope format and calls it pitch — so 6/12 means 6 inches of rise per 12 inches of run. This calculator uses that industry-standard convention.

How do I measure pitch on an existing roof?

Use a level and a tape measure. Hold a 12-inch level horizontally against the roof surface, then at the 12-inch mark measure straight down to the roof — that vertical measurement is your pitch. For example, 6 inches down means a 6/12 pitch. You can also use a digital angle finder and convert with pitch = 12 × tan(angle).

Does this calculator work for hip roofs?

This tool calculates common rafter lengths for standard gable roofs. Hip roofs need separate hip and valley rafter calculations because those rafters run diagonally in plan as well as in slope. As a rough guide, the hip-to-common rafter ratio is √2 only on a flat roof; on a 6/12 pitch it's closer to 1.34 and it keeps shrinking as pitch increases, so use a dedicated hip rafter calculator for precise cuts.

What roof pitch is too steep to walk on safely?

Most roofers consider anything above 6/12 to require extra caution, and above 9/12 usually requires fall-protection equipment like a harness and roof anchors. Steeper pitches also need slip-resistant footwear and roof jacks or toe boards for safe footing during installation or repair.

How much does eave overhang add to rafter length?

Overhang adds proportionally to rafter length based on the rafter factor, not a flat amount. For a 6/12 pitch, a 12-inch (1 ft) overhang adds about 1.12 ft of rafter length; for a steeper 12/12 pitch, that same 1 ft of overhang adds about 1.41 ft, since the steeper slope stretches every foot of horizontal run further.
