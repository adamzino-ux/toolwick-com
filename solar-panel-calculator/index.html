How to Use This Solar Panel Calculator

Enter your electricity use from a utility bill, set the peak sun hours for your location with the slider, and type the wattage printed on the panels you are considering. The calculator returns the number of panels (always rounded up), the installed system size in kW, estimated yearly production, roof area, inverter size, and a minimum breaker. Cost and payback appear if you fill in the optional price fields. Change any input and the results update.

Why This Matters

Going solar is one of the largest purchases a homeowner makes, often $15,000 to $30,000 before incentives, so sizing it correctly pays off. Too small, and you keep buying power from the utility that you meant to replace. Too large, and you pay for capacity you cannot use, because many utilities credit exported power at less than the retail rate.

Location changes the answer a lot. A home using 900 kWh a month needs about 7.4 kW of panels at five peak sun hours, but about 10.6 kW at Seattle's roughly 3.5 hours. That is the difference between 19 panels and 27. Planning an electric car (about 3,000 to 4,000 kWh a year) or a heat pump can add several more panels.

Use this tool to sanity-check installer quotes, find out whether your roof has enough space before you book site visits, or compare 350 W and 500 W panels. It models a grid-tied system with no batteries.

How It's Calculated

The calculator converts your target into the panel count that delivers it, using sun hours and a real-world efficiency factor:

Annual kWh = monthly kWh × 12 (if entered monthly) Target kWh = Annual kWh × Offset % Required kW = Target kWh ÷ (Sun hours × 365 × Efficiency %) Panels = ROUNDUP(Required kW × 1,000 ÷ Panel watts) System kW = Panels × Panel watts ÷ 1,000 Production = System kW × Sun hours × 365 × Efficiency %

Peak sun hours are the number of hours per day at a standard 1,000 W/m² intensity. They are not daylight hours. System efficiency (default 80%) covers inverter loss, wiring, heat, dust, and shading. Newer systems in clear locations may reach 85%, while shaded or hot-climate roofs can fall below 75%.

Roof area assumes about 19 W of panel per square foot (a 400 W panel covers roughly 21 sq ft), plus 25% for fire-code pathways, setbacks and spacing. Inverter size uses a DC-to-AC ratio of 1.2, rounded up to the next 0.5 kW. The AC current assumes 240 V single-phase. The minimum breaker is the inverter's AC amps multiplied by 125% for continuous output (NEC 690.8 and 705, 2020/2023 editions), then rounded up to the next standard breaker size. Roof setbacks follow the general intent of IRC R324. Local adoption and amendments vary, and your utility and inspector have the final say.

Worked Example

A household uses 900 kWh per month, has 5 peak sun hours, 400 W panels, 80% efficiency, a 100% offset target, $2.75 per watt installed, and $0.16 per kWh.

  • Annual use: 900 × 12 = 10,800 kWh.
  • Daily-to-yearly factor: 5 × 365 × 0.80 = 1,460 kWh per kW installed.
  • Required size: 10,800 ÷ 1,460 = 7.40 kW.
  • Panels: 7,397 W ÷ 400 W = 18.49, rounded up to 19 panels.
  • Installed size: 19 × 400 W = 7.60 kW, producing 7.6 × 1,460 = 11,096 kWh a year (103% of use).
  • Roof area: 19 × 400 ÷ 19 = 400 sq ft of panels, or 500 sq ft with the 25% allowance.
  • Inverter: 7.6 ÷ 1.2 = 6.33 kW, rounded up to 6.5 kW. That is 27.1 A at 240 V, ×1.25 = 33.9 A, so a 35 A breaker minimum.
  • Cost and payback: 7,600 W × $2.75 = $20,900. Savings are 10,800 × $0.16 = $1,728 a year, so payback is about 12.1 years before incentives.

Tips & Common Mistakes

  • Use 12 months of bills, not one. A single summer or winter bill can be 40% off your true annual average.
  • Do not confuse sun hours with daylight. Ten hours of daylight might equal only 4 to 5 peak sun hours. Use a local figure from NREL's PVWatts or your installer.
  • Account for shade, tilt and direction. A roof facing east or west, or shaded by trees after 2 p.m., can cut output 10 to 25%. Lower the efficiency input to model it.
  • Plan for future loads. An EV, heat pump or pool can add thousands of kWh. It is cheaper to add panels during the first install than later.
  • Check panel limits. Your main panel's busbar rating limits how large a breaker you can backfeed (the NEC 705.12 "120% rule"). Roof structure and wind or snow loads also need checking. Have a licensed electrician or engineer verify both.

Frequently Asked Questions

How many solar panels do I need to power a house?

Most US homes need 15 to 25 panels of 400 W, depending on usage and sun hours. A house using 900 kWh a month at 5 sun hours needs about 19 panels. Enter your own numbers above for a tailored count.

How many panels do I need for 1,000 kWh per month?

At 5 peak sun hours and 80% efficiency, 12,000 kWh a year needs about 8.2 kW, or 21 panels of 400 W. In a 4-hour location it rises to about 26 panels. The sun hours slider shows the effect.

How much power does one 400 W panel produce per day?

With 5 peak sun hours and 80% efficiency, a 400 W panel yields about 1.6 kWh per day, or roughly 584 kWh per year. Cloudy regions may see less than half of that in winter.

What are peak sun hours?

Peak sun hours measure how much solar energy your location receives, expressed as hours of full-intensity sunlight (1,000 W/m²) per day. Most of the US falls between 3.5 and 6.5. It is a standard figure for sizing a solar system.

Should I offset 100% of my electricity use?

Not always. If your utility pays little for exported power, sizing for 80 to 90% can give a better return. If you plan to add an EV or heat pump, sizing above 100% makes sense. Use the offset slider to compare.

Can this replace a professional solar quote?

No. It is a planning estimate. A licensed installer must assess your roof, shading, structure, electrical panel and local code requirements, and a permit and inspection are normally required before you connect to the grid.
