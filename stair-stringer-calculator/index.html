How to Use This Stair Calculator

Enter the total rise (the vertical distance from the lower finished floor to the upper finished floor), the number of risers, and the tread depth you want. Add your tread thickness and stringer board width, then press Calculate. You get the unit rise, total run, cut angle, throat, stringer length, a cut schedule, and a comparison of nearby riser counts. Warnings flag anything outside common IRC limits.

Why This Matters

Stairs are one of the few parts of a house where a quarter inch matters. People learn the height of a step in their legs, so a riser that is 3/8″ taller than its neighbours is a classic trip-and-fall spot. That is why the IRC limits the variation between the tallest and shortest riser in a flight to 3/8″.

Take a deck that sits 49″ above grade. Seven risers give exactly 7″ each, which is comfortable and compliant. Six risers would give 8.17″ each, over the 7¾″ residential maximum. Eight risers give 6.125″ each, legal but longer, which means more run and more lumber. A calculator lets you compare those options in seconds, before you buy a single 2×12.

The same numbers decide your lumber order, the saw angle, the footprint on the ground, and whether the stringer leaves enough solid wood at its thinnest point. Homeowners building a deck or basement stair, carpenters checking a layout, and inspectors sanity-checking a plan all need these figures. Get them wrong and you cut an expensive board that cannot be used.

How It's Calculated

A flight with N risers has N − 1 treads, because the top riser meets the upper floor or deck. All formulas below use inches.

Unit rise = Total rise ÷ N
Total run = Tread depth × (N − 1)
Stringer length = √(Total rise² + Total run²)
Cut angle = arctan(Unit rise ÷ Tread depth)
Throat = Board width − (Unit rise × Tread depth) ÷ √(Unit rise² + Tread depth²)
Bottom riser cut = Unit rise − Tread thickness

The cut angle is the angle of one step, not of the overall hypotenuse. This is the angle you set on a saw or framing square. The throat is the perpendicular distance from the inside corner of a notch to the bottom edge of the board. It is the thinnest strip of wood carrying the load. The bottom riser is cut shorter by one tread thickness, so the first finished step is the same height as the rest once treads are on.

The stock length adds at least 12″ (or one board width, if larger) to the stringer length for the plumb and seat cuts, then rounds up to the next standard length (8, 10, 12, 14, 16, 18, 20 or 24 ft). Stringer length is rounded up and throat is rounded down, so the displayed numbers err on the safe side.

Checks follow IRC 2021 R311.7 for one- and two-family homes: maximum riser 7¾″ (R311.7.5.1), minimum tread 10″ (R311.7.5.2), nosing required when treads are under 11″ (R311.7.5.3), and maximum 12 ft 7 in between landings (R311.7.3). The IBC (commercial) is stricter: 4″ to 7″ risers and 11″ treads. The 3½″ minimum throat is a widely used rule of thumb, not a code number. Local codes vary, so confirm with your building department.

Worked Example

A deck is 49″ above a concrete pad. We want 7 risers, 11″ treads, 1.5″ thick treads, and 2×12 stringers (11.25″).

  1. Unit rise = 49 ÷ 7 = 7.000″.
  2. Treads = 7 − 1 = 6, so total run = 6 × 11 = 66″.
  3. Stringer length = √(49² + 66²) = √6,757 = 82.20″. The tool displays 82.21″ because it rounds up.
  4. Cut angle = arctan(7 ÷ 11) = 32.5° from horizontal (57.5° from vertical).
  5. Step diagonal = √(7² + 11²) = 13.04″. Throat = 11.25 − (77 ÷ 13.04) = 11.25 − 5.91 = 5.34″, well above 3½″.
  6. Bottom riser cut = 7.0 − 1.5 = 5.5″.
  7. Stock needed = 82.20 + 12 = 94.20″ = 7.85 ft, so buy 8 ft boards.
  8. Checks: 7″ ≤ 7¾″, 11″ ≥ 10″, and rise + run = 18″. No warnings.

Tips & Common Mistakes

  • Do not round the unit rise and step it off. A rise of 7.714″ marked as 7 11/16″ is 0.026″ short per step, which adds up to more than 1/4″ over 10 steps. Use the cumulative heights in the cut schedule or a stair gauge set once.
  • Measure total rise to finished surfaces. Include the thickness of the upper decking or flooring and of the landing pad. A rise measured to the framing will be wrong by a full tread thickness.
  • Remember the bottom riser adjustment. Without it, the first step is a tread thickness taller than the others, which is a common trip point.
  • Check the throat before you cut. A big rise and a short tread leave little wood under the notch. Use wider stock, an engineered stringer, or add another riser.
  • Mind the unsupported span and spacing. Long runs often need a mid-landing or support post. Stringers are usually spaced 16″ on center or closer for 5/4 decking, and a 36″ wide stair normally needs three. Check span tables for your lumber species and grade.
  • Land the bottom on a firm pad. Wood stringers should not sit in soil. Use a concrete pad or approved footing, and treat all stringer cuts with preservative if the lumber is pressure-treated.

Frequently Asked Questions

How many stringers do I need for a staircase?

For a 36″ wide stair, three stringers (two outside, one in the middle) is the usual practice. Space them no more than 16″ on center with 5/4 decking, and closer if your treads are thinner or the load is heavy. Wider stairs need more, and your local code or span tables have the final word.

What is the ideal rise and run for comfortable stairs?

Most people find stairs comfortable when rise plus run is 17 to 18 inches, or when two times the rise plus the run is 24 to 25 inches. A 7″ rise with an 11″ run fits both rules. Steeper stairs save floor space but are harder to climb and descend.

Why is total run one tread less than the number of risers?

The top riser lands on the upper floor or deck, so it has no tread of its own. A stair with 7 risers therefore has 6 treads, and the total run is 6 times the tread depth. Counting 7 treads would make the stair and the stringer too long.

What is a stringer throat, and how much do I need?

The throat is the thinnest section of wood left under a notch, measured perpendicular to the board's edge. Many builders want at least 3½″ on a 2×12. That is a rule of thumb rather than a code figure, and it depends on lumber grade, spacing and span.

Why is the bottom riser cut shorter than the others?

The first tread sits on top of the stringer's bottom notch, while the stringer itself rests on the pad. Cutting the bottom riser shorter by one tread thickness makes the first finished step equal the others. The top of the stringer is usually laid out the same way against the deck framing.

What angle do I set on my saw or framing square?

The cut angle result is measured from horizontal for the seat cut. The plumb cut is 90° minus that angle. On a framing square, use the unit rise on one leg and the tread depth on the other, and mark along the outside edges.
