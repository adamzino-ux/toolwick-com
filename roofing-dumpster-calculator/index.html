How to Use This Roofing Dumpster Size Calculator

Enter your roof area in square feet or in roofing squares (one square is 100 sq ft). If your number came from a satellite report or a ground-level measurement of the building outline, choose "Footprint" and pick the pitch so the calculator can add the extra surface that slope creates. Then select the material you are removing, the number of layers, and how much of the decking you expect to replace. Press the button to see the recommended dumpster, a fill gauge, a debris estimate, a table comparing every common size, and a quick look at what happens if you find extra layers.

Why This Matters

Roofing debris is heavy, and that is what surprises most homeowners and small contractors. A 24-square architectural shingle roof can produce about 5 tons of waste, even though it only fills roughly 12 cubic yards. Order a 10-yard container because the volume looks small and you will hit the weight limit before the bin is half full, which means overweight fees or a second haul, often $300 to $600 each time. Order a 40-yard container "just in case" and you pay more for the rental, may not fit on the driveway, and may need a street permit.

Getting the size right matters most for tear-offs that go beyond the basics: a 2,800 sq ft ranch with two layers of 3-tab shingles, a cedar shake roof with rotted decking, or a clay tile roof that weighs 700+ lb per square. Roofers use estimates like this to quote jobs, and homeowners use them to avoid paying for a dumpster twice. Scheduling matters as well, since most rentals are priced by the week, and a swap-out mid-job can stall a crew for a day.

How It's Calculated

The calculator converts your area to roofing squares, multiplies by layers and material factors, adds decking, and applies a safety margin. If you chose footprint, the slope multiplier is applied first:

Surface sq ft = footprint sq ft × √(1 + (pitch ÷ 12)²) Squares = surface sq ft ÷ 100 Volume (yd³) = (squares × layers × V + deck squares × 0.20) × 1.10 Weight (lb) = (squares × layers × (W + 15) + deck squares × 180) × 1.10 × (1.15 if wet)

W and V come from the table below. The extra 15 lb per square covers underlayment, nails, drip edge and flashing. Decking is assumed at 1.8 lb per sq ft (about 5/8" plywood or OSB), and loose debris from decking at 0.20 yd³ per square replaced. The 10% margin covers ridge caps, hidden damage and estimating error. For each dumpster size, the tool calculates how many are needed by volume and by weight, takes the larger number, and recommends the smallest size that works in a single container. Volumes are rounded up to 0.1 yd³ and weights up to 0.1 ton, so every result leans toward the larger, safer side.

Worked Example

A homeowner has a 2,400 sq ft roof surface of architectural shingles, one layer, with about 10% of the decking expected to need replacement and dry weather.

  • Squares: 2,400 ÷ 100 = 24.0 squares. Decking to replace: 24 × 10% = 2.4 squares.
  • Volume: (24 × 1 × 0.45 + 2.4 × 0.20) × 1.10 = (10.8 + 0.48) × 1.10 = 12.408, shown as 12.5 yd³.
  • Weight: (24 × (350 + 15) + 2.4 × 180) × 1.10 = (8,760 + 432) × 1.10 = 10,111 lb, or about 5.1 tons.
  • By size: a 10-yard needs 3 containers (weight), a 15-yard needs 2, a 20-yard needs 2 (5.06 tons exceeds its 4-ton limit), and a 30-yard needs 1 (6-ton limit).
  • Result: a 30-yard dumpster. Volume alone would fit a 15-yard, but weight pushes the recommendation up.

A 20-yard with an overage fee for the extra 1.1 tons could also work. This tool recommends the size that stays within typical limits, so confirm your hauler's actual per-ton overage rate before choosing the cheaper option.

Tips & Common Mistakes

  • Don't include shingle waste. The 10-15% extra material you order for cuts and hips is not tear-off debris. Use the actual roof surface area.
  • Check weight limits, not just yards. Asphalt and especially tile, slate and concrete are weight-limited long before the container is full. Ask whether the hauler offers heavy-debris or roofing containers.
  • Never fill above the rim. Haulers can refuse to haul overfilled containers, and loose debris is a road hazard. Round up to the next size if you are close.
  • Count your layers before ordering. Check an exposed edge or the attic for a second layer. Each extra layer nearly doubles the debris, and local code often limits roofs to two layers.
  • Test older roofs. Roofing felts, cements and some shingles installed before the 1980s may contain asbestos. Have material tested before tear-off, since disposal rules differ.
  • Plan for rain. Wet shingles and decking can weigh noticeably more. Tick the rain option, or tarp the container.

Frequently Asked Questions

What size dumpster do I need for a roof replacement?

Most single-layer residential jobs of 15 to 30 squares land in the 20 to 30 yard range. A 10-yard works only for small roofs of roughly 8 to 9 squares of architectural shingles. Use the calculator above to account for material, layers and decking.

How many squares of shingles fit in a 20-yard dumpster?

By this tool's assumptions and a typical 4-ton limit, a 20-yard holds roughly 19 squares of single-layer architectural shingles with 10% decking replaced, or about 25 squares of 3-tab. Weight, not volume, is the limit. By volume alone it would hold nearly twice as much.

How much does a square of shingles weigh?

A square of 3-tab asphalt typically weighs 200 to 250 lb, and architectural shingles 300 to 400 lb. Old shingles saturated with water or granules can weigh more. This tool uses 250 and 350 lb per square plus 15 lb for underlayment and metal.

Do I need a permit for a roofing dumpster?

A dumpster on your own driveway usually needs no permit, but one placed on a public street often does. Many HOAs and cities have their own rules about placement and duration, so ask your hauler and local office before delivery.

Can I put roofing debris in a regular dumpster?

Often yes, but tell the hauler up front. Roofing waste is heavy, and some haulers use dedicated roofing or heavy-debris containers with different weight limits and recycling options. Mixing in household trash, paint or hazardous items can lead to extra fees or rejection.

Should I measure the footprint or the roof surface?

Either works. Surface area is better, as it is what roofing reports and shingle orders use. If you only know the footprint of the house (including overhangs), choose the footprint option and enter the pitch so the calculator multiplies by the slope factor. At 6:12 that factor is about 1.118.
