Why This Matters

A sidewalk looks like the easiest concrete job there is, but it is also where small math errors get expensive. Ready-mix trucks charge by the cubic yard, many suppliers add a short-load fee below roughly 3–4 yards, and concrete starts to stiffen within about 90 minutes of batching. You cannot drive back for another half yard once the forms are full.

Thickness is the biggest lever. A 30 × 4 ft walk at 4 inches thick needs about 1.48 cubic yards before waste. Go to 5 inches and it is 1.85, and 6 inches takes it to 2.22. That is 50% more concrete for two extra inches. Homeowners use a calculator like this to decide between a truck and bagged mix (one cubic yard is about 45 bags of 80-lb mix). Landscapers use it to bid walkway jobs. Property managers use it to budget trip-hazard repairs. The gravel base matters just as much. A well-compacted 4-inch base is what keeps panels from settling and cracking, and it has to be ordered too.

How It's Calculated

Everything starts with the slab volume, which is the surface area multiplied by the thickness. Thickness is converted from inches to feet (or centimetres to metres) so the units match.

Volume (ft³) = Length × Width × (Thickness in ÷ 12) × Sections
Cubic yards = ft³ ÷ 27
Metric: m³ = Length m × Width m × (Thickness cm ÷ 100) × Sections
Total = Net volume × (1 + waste %)

The order quantity is the total rounded up to the next ¼ cubic yard (or 0.1 m³), because suppliers batch in increments and running short is the costly mistake. Bag counts divide the total cubic feet by the yield of each bag (0.60 ft³ for 80 lb, 0.45 ft³ for 60 lb, 0.30 ft³ for 40 lb) and round up to whole bags. Gravel base volume is area × base depth, plus 15% to allow for compaction, converted at about 1.5 tons per cubic yard of loose crushed stone, and rounded up. Control joints are counted as the length divided by your spacing, rounded up, minus one, per section. Joint depth is at least one quarter of the slab thickness.

Joint spacing, reconciled. Two rules of thumb apply and the tighter one governs. For slabs in general, spacing in feet should not exceed about 2 to 3 times the thickness in inches, which allows 8–12 ft for a 4-inch slab. But a sidewalk is narrow, and a panel much longer than it is wide tends to crack across its width, so panel length should stay near the width, up to about 1.5 times. For a 4 ft walk that gives 4–6 ft, which is tighter than the slab rule, so the calculator defaults to 5 ft. It shows a notice if your spacing exceeds either limit.

These are common rules of thumb (4-inch minimum walkway thickness, 5–6 inches where vehicles cross, joints cut to ¼ depth). They are not tied to one code edition, and local codes and municipal standards vary. Results are planning estimates, not engineering, permit or inspection approval. Confirm requirements with your building department.

Worked Example

Suppose you are pouring a 30 ft long, 4 ft wide walkway, 4 inches thick, one section, 10% waste, a 4-inch gravel base and joints every 5 ft at $165 per yard.

  • Area: 30 × 4 = 120 ft².
  • Net volume: 120 × (4 ÷ 12) = 40 ft³, which is 40 ÷ 27 = 1.48 yd³.
  • With 10% waste: 40 × 1.10 = 44 ft³ = 1.63 yd³.
  • Order quantity: 1.63 rounded up to the next ¼ = 1.75 yd³.
  • Bags: 44 ÷ 0.60 = 73.3, so 74 bags of 80 lb (or 98 of 60 lb, 147 of 40 lb).
  • Gravel base: 120 × (4 ÷ 12) ÷ 27 × 1.15 = 1.704 yd³. The calculator rounds this up to 1.71 yd³, which is about 2.6 tons (1.704 × 1.5 = 2.56, rounded up to 2.6).
  • Joints: 30 ÷ 5 = 6 panels, so 5 cuts, each at least 1 inch deep.
  • Cost: 1.75 × $165 = $288.75, shown as $289 (concrete only).

Tips & Common Mistakes

  • Never skip the waste factor. Subgrade that is a half-inch low, spilled concrete and uneven forms easily eat 5–10%. Order the rounded-up quantity, not the net volume.
  • Use a thicker slab where vehicles cross. Driveway aprons and mower or truck crossings are usually 6 inches or more. Enter that section separately.
  • Mind width and slope for accessibility. The 2010 ADA Standards call for at least 36 inches of clear width and a cross slope no steeper than 1:48 (about 2%) on accessible routes. Many residential walks are 4 ft wide for comfort.
  • Cut control joints early. Cut or tool them to at least ¼ of the slab depth within the first 6–12 hours so cracking happens in the joint, not across the panel. When in doubt, space them closer, not farther.
  • Do not rely on bags for big jobs. Past about 1 cubic yard (45 bags of 80 lb), mixing by hand is slow, and inconsistent batches create weak or mismatched panels.
  • In freeze-thaw climates, ask for air-entrained concrete and compact the base well. Cheaper shortcuts tend to cost more later.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much concrete do I need for a 4×20 sidewalk?

At 4 inches thick, 4 × 20 × (4 ÷ 12) = 26.67 ft³, or 0.99 cubic yards. With a 10% waste allowance that becomes about 1.09 yards, so you would order 1.25 cubic yards.

How thick should a concrete sidewalk be?

Four inches is the common minimum for foot traffic, and 5–6 inches is typical where vehicles cross, such as a driveway apron. Local codes and municipal standards vary, so check with your building department before you pour.

How many bags of concrete do I need for a sidewalk?

An 80-lb bag yields about 0.60 ft³, so divide your total cubic feet by 0.60 and round up. A 4 × 20 ft walk at 4 inches (26.67 ft³) needs about 45 bags, or 49 with a 10% waste allowance.

How wide should a sidewalk be?

For accessible routes, the ADA minimum clear width is 36 inches. Residential walks are commonly 4 ft wide, and 5 ft lets two people pass or walk side by side comfortably.

How far apart should sidewalk control joints be?

Two limits apply. A slab guideline is spacing in feet of about 2 to 3 times the thickness in inches (8–12 ft for 4 inches), and a narrow walk should have panels roughly as long as they are wide, up to about 1.5 times. The tighter limit wins, which is why 4–5 ft is typical for a 4 ft wide sidewalk.

Should I order extra concrete for a sidewalk?

Yes. Plan for 5–10% extra to cover uneven subgrade, spillage and form movement. Running short mid-pour leaves a cold joint, which is far more expensive than a little leftover concrete.
