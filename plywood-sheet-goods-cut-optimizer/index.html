How to Use This Plywood & Sheet Goods Cut Optimizer

Pick a standard sheet size or enter your own, set the saw kerf, then list each part by width, height and quantity. Press Optimize Cuts to see how many sheets the layout needs, the waste percentage, a per-sheet breakdown and a scaled drawing of every sheet.

Switch units any time: all your entered numbers are converted automatically. Turn off 90° rotation if grain direction matters. The spare allowance rounds your purchase up so you do not come up short.

Why This Matters

Sheet goods are one of the largest line items on any cabinet, shop-fixture, shed or subfloor job. A sheet of ¾-inch birch cabinet plywood commonly costs $80–$130, and a full kitchen of cabinet boxes can use 12–18 sheets, so one wasteful layout can burn $150 or more. Hobbyists feel it too: a workbench with a top, shelves and drawer parts might look like "about one sheet" on paper, but kerf, awkward part sizes and the need to avoid damaged edges often push it to two.

Contractors use cut plans for subfloor and sheathing, where a 4×8 panel covers 32 square feet and every offcut that cannot be reused is landfill. Planning before you buy tells you whether you need 5 sheets or 6, whether a half-sheet will do, and which offcuts are worth saving. It also gives you a cut list you can hand to a lumber yard, many of which will run the panel saw for a small per-cut fee.

How It's Calculated

The layout uses a guillotine rectangle-packing heuristic, a practical rule of thumb rather than a code formula. Every cut runs edge to edge, like a table saw or track saw. The tool tries eight combinations (four sort orders and two split styles), then keeps the result with the fewest sheets.

  • Kerf: each neighbouring piece needs the blade width between them, so n pieces across = n × width + (n − 1) × kerf must not exceed the sheet width.
  • Best short-side fit: each piece goes in the free rectangle where it leaves the smallest gap, in both orientations if rotation is allowed.
  • Waste %: (sheets × sheet area − total piece area) ÷ (sheets × sheet area) × 100. Kerf and offcuts count as waste.
  • Suggested purchase: ceil(sheets × (1 + spare % ÷ 100)). It always rounds up, never down.

The method is a heuristic, so on complex lists a hand-tuned layout may occasionally save one sheet. Parts that cannot fit the sheet in any allowed orientation are rejected with an error instead of being silently dropped.

Worked Example

Sheet: 48"×96" (4,608 sq in), kerf ⅛", 10% spare, and a cost of $65 per sheet. Parts: four 23.5"×47.5" panels and six 10"×20" shelf parts. These are the tool's default piece sizes; the cost rows only appear after you enter $65 in the cost field.

  • Panel area: 23.5 × 47.5 = 1,116.25 sq in each, ×4 = 4,465 sq in. All four fit on sheet 1. A 2×2 grid works (two across need 47.125", two down need 95.125"). With rotation on, the tool usually draws them rotated to 47.5"×23.5" and stacked in one column instead: 4 × 23.5 + 3 × 0.125 = 94.375" of the 96" length. Either arrangement uses the same area, so the totals below do not change.
  • Shelf area: 10 × 20 = 200 sq in each, ×6 = 1,200 sq in. What remains on sheet 1 is only thin strips (roughly 1.5" along the end and under ½" along the side), far too narrow for a 10"×20" part. All six shelf parts go on sheet 2.
  • Total parts: 5,665 sq in. Two sheets give 9,216 sq in, so waste = 3,551 ÷ 9,216 = 38.5%.

Tips & Common Mistakes

  • Never set kerf to zero: ignoring the blade width is the classic reason parts come out short. Ten cuts can eat over an inch.
  • Measure the real sheet: many panels run a little under nominal, and factory edges may be damaged. Enter the measured size, or the layout may be a hair too optimistic.
  • Turn rotation off for grain-critical parts: face frames, visible veneer and tabletops need grain running the right way, and rotating a part can cost an extra sheet.
  • Cut oversize, then trim: for tight joinery, add 1/16"–⅛" to critical parts and trim to final size.
  • Keep the spare allowance: the suggested purchase rounds up on purpose. A second trip for one sheet from a different dye lot costs more than the spare.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is saw kerf and why does it matter?

Kerf is the width of material the blade turns into sawdust, about ⅛" (0.125") per cut for a typical circular saw blade. It is why two 24" pieces will not quite fit across a 48" sheet: 24" + ⅛" + 24" is 48⅛". Over ten cuts the lost material adds up to 1¼".

Can I use this for MDF, OSB, drywall or acrylic?

Yes. Any rectangular sheet material works, including MDF, melamine, OSB, cement board, drywall, acrylic and foam board. Enter the sheet size and your parts, and choose the matching units.

Why did the optimizer use more sheets than I expected?

Area alone is not enough, because kerf and part shapes leave unusable strips. Finding the perfect layout is a hard problem, so the tool uses a fast heuristic that is usually very close. Try changing quantities or allowing rotation to see if a sheet can be saved.

How much extra plywood should I buy?

A 10% spare allowance is a common rule of thumb, and it is the default here. Use 15% or more for figured veneer or complex parts. The tool always rounds up.

Does rotation affect grain direction?

Yes. With rotation on, a part may be turned 90° to fit better, which changes grain direction. The layout marks rotated parts with ↻, and you can turn rotation off entirely.

Can I use this layout for structural sheathing?

It only plans material quantity and cuts. Structural panels must meet their span rating, edge-support and fastening requirements in your local adopted code, and local codes vary. Confirm with your building department or a licensed professional.
