How to Use This Chain Link Fence Calculator

Enter the total length of your fence line including gate openings, pick the fence height, and choose whether the fence is a closed loop or an open run. Add your corner count and any walk or drive gates, then set post spacing, waste allowance and your local frost depth. The calculator returns total posts, fabric rolls, top rail, fittings, concrete bags and a spacing comparison, and you can copy the whole list for the store.

Why This Matters

Chain link is the workhorse of yard, kennel, school, pool-area and commercial fencing because it is cheap per foot and goes up fast. That speed is also why people under-order. A simple 200 ft backyard perimeter in 4 ft fabric with four corners and one walk gate needs about 25 posts, five rolls of fabric, 15 lengths of top rail and roughly 40 bags of 80 lb concrete. Miss any of those by a few pieces and you are making a second trip mid-job, with concrete already curing and a crew waiting.

Over-ordering costs money too. A single 6 ft roll of fabric can run well over $100, and fabric from different production lots sometimes varies slightly in finish, so topping up later is not always seamless. Contractors use a takeoff like this to quote jobs, landlords use it to budget a lot fence, and homeowners use it to decide whether a DIY install makes sense before comparing it with a contractor bid. If the fence encloses a pool, remember that barrier rules for height, mesh size and gate latches are stricter than for a normal yard, so check with your building department before you buy anything.

How It's Calculated

The calculator works from the fence run, which is the total length minus the gate openings:

Run = total length − (walk gates × walk width) − (drive gates × drive opening)

Line posts = ROUNDUP(Run ÷ spacing) − 1

Terminal posts are the heavy posts that carry the fabric tension: two end posts for an open run (none for a closed loop), one per corner, and two per walk gate. Each double drive gate adds two larger gate posts. The fabric is cut into segments between terminal posts, and every segment end gets a tension bar, tension bands, a truss-rod brace and rail ends.

Fabric = Run × (1 + waste) ÷ 50 ft per roll, rounded up. Top rail = (Run + brace rails) × (1 + waste) ÷ 21 ft, rounded up.

Embed depth starts at 24 in for 3–4 ft fences, 30 in for 5–6 ft, 36 in for 8 ft and 42 in for 10 ft. Terminal posts go 6 in deeper and drive-gate posts 12 in deeper. If your frost depth is greater, that value wins, and every depth is rounded up to the next 6 in. Post length is height + embed + 2 in, rounded up to a whole foot.

Concrete volume per hole is π × (diameter ÷ 2)² × depth, using 8 in holes for line posts, 10 in for terminal posts and 12 in for gate posts. The total gets a 10% allowance and is divided by 0.60 cu ft per 80 lb bag. The line-post count is a conservative upper bound: it treats the run as one continuous span, so it may be a few posts high on layouts with many short segments. Rules of thumb follow common ASTM F567 installation guidance (line posts no more than 10 ft apart) and ASTM F900 gate-post sizing. Local codes vary, and this is a planning estimate, not engineering, permit or inspection approval.

Worked Example

Take a 50 ft × 50 ft yard: 200 ft of 4 ft fence, closed perimeter, 4 corners, one 4 ft walk gate, 10 ft spacing, 5% waste and no frost. These are the tool's default inputs.

  • Run: 200 − 4 = 196 ft.
  • Line posts: 196 ÷ 10 = 19.6, rounded up to 20, minus 1 = 19.
  • Terminal posts: 0 ends + 4 corners + 2 gate posts = 6. Total posts: 25.
  • Fabric: 196 × 1.05 = 205.8 ft ÷ 50 = 4.1, so 5 rolls.
  • Top rail: there are 5 segments (4 corners + 1 gate) and 10 segment ends, so brace rails add 10 × 10 = 100 ft. (196 + 100) × 1.05 = 310.8 ft ÷ 21 = 14.8, so 15 lengths.
  • Concrete: line holes (8 in × 24 in) take about 0.70 cu ft each, or 13.3 cu ft for 19. Terminal holes (10 in × 30 in) take about 1.36 cu ft each, or 8.2 cu ft for 6. With 10% extra over 0.60 cu ft per bag, that is 25 + 15 = 40 bags.
  • Hardware: 10 tension bars, 42 tension bands, 20 brace bands, 20 rail ends, 10 truss rods and 183 tie wires.

Tips & Common Mistakes

  • Call 811 before digging. Underground utilities are the most expensive thing to hit, and a locate is free in most areas.
  • Let concrete cure 24–48 hours before stretching fabric. Pulling fabric tight against green concrete leans terminal posts out of plumb, and they rarely come back.
  • Measure the real line, not the plan. Run a string along the fence line, mark every corner and gate, and add up the straight segments. Fence lines on sloped lots are longer than they look on a survey.
  • Do not skimp on gate posts. A drive gate leaf is heavy and swings off one post. Use the larger post size the tool shows and check your gate manufacturer's specification.
  • Verify the boundary and the rules. Confirm your property line, setbacks, HOA rules and permit requirements before you dig. Fences over 6 ft almost always need a permit.

Frequently Asked Questions

How many posts do I need for 100 feet of chain link fence?

For a straight open run with two ends and 10 ft spacing, you need 9 line posts plus 2 end posts, which is 11 in total. Each corner or gate adds terminal posts, so a fence with the same 100 ft of length and a gate will need a few more.

How far apart should chain link fence posts be?

Line posts should be no more than 10 ft apart, center to center, which is the commonly cited ASTM F567 limit. Many installers go closer, around 8 ft, in windy areas or for taller fences. This calculator's slider stops at 10 ft for that reason.

How deep should chain link fence posts be set?

A common rule is at least 24 in for 3–4 ft fences and 30–36 in or more for taller fences, and always below your local frost line. This tool uses those minimums, adds depth to terminal and gate posts, and rounds up to the next 6 in.

How much concrete does each fence post need?

An 8 in hole that is 24 in deep holds about 0.70 cu ft, or just over one 80 lb bag. A 10 in by 30 in terminal hole holds about 1.36 cu ft, or about 2.3 bags. The calculator adds 10% for spillage and uneven holes.

How many rolls of chain link fabric do I need?

Fabric comes in 50 ft rolls in most heights. Divide your fence run (without gates) by 50 after adding a waste allowance, then round up. A 196 ft run with 5% waste works out to 5 rolls.

Do I need a permit for a chain link fence?

Often yes, especially above 6 ft or in front yards, and always check for pool-barrier and HOA rules. Rules differ by city and county, so confirm with your local building department before you order materials.
