How to Use This Wallpaper Calculator

Choose feet or metres, then enter the room width, room length and wall height. Pick a roll size preset or type the width and length printed on your roll's label. Enter the pattern repeat (0 for plain paper), a trim allowance, a waste percentage and any doors or windows. The calculator returns the number of rolls to buy, the strips each roll yields, a wall-by-wall breakdown and a comparison of different pattern repeats.

Why This Matters

Wallpaper is one of the few finishes where guessing can really hurt. Rolls are printed in batches, and a roll from a later batch can differ in shade enough to show as a stripe on the wall. If you come up one roll short in a 12 × 14 ft room, you may wait weeks for the same batch, or never find it. Overbuying hurts too: designer papers often run $80–$200 per double roll, so two spare rolls can add several hundred dollars.

Pattern makes a bigger difference than most people expect. A plain paper in a 12 × 12 ft room with 8 ft walls needs about 12 double rolls with the default settings here. The same room with 9 ft walls and a 24 in drop-match pattern needs about 17, because each strip is cut longer and only two fit on a 33 ft roll. Doors and windows help only partly. A strip over a doorway still has to be cut above the door, so you can't just subtract the opening's area. DIYers, landlords refreshing a rental and designers quoting a client all need a “buy this many” number, not a square-foot guess.

How It's Calculated

This follows the drop-by-drop method used by paperhangers and retailers, with conservative rounding. All working is done in feet, and the steps are:

  1. Strip drop = wall height + pattern repeat + trim allowance.
  2. Strips per roll = floor(roll length ÷ strip drop).
  3. Strips per wall = ceil(wall length ÷ roll width), counted for each of the four walls separately. Most hangers start a fresh strip at every corner.
  4. Opening credit. An opening of width w always fully covers at least ceil(w ÷ roll width) − 1 strips, wherever it sits. Each of those full-height strips is removed and replaced by short pieces: one above a door, or one above and one below a window (the remaining height split in half), each plus repeat and trim. The short pieces are packed into strip-length slots, largest first. An opening narrower than one strip earns no credit.
  5. Rolls = ceil(strip slots ÷ strips per roll), then ceil(rolls × (1 + waste)).
  6. Area cross-check = ceil(net wall area × (1 + waste) ÷ roll area). If this is ever higher than the strip-based answer, the higher number is shown.

Worked Example

Room 12 × 14 ft, 8 ft walls. Roll 20.5 in × 33 ft, 12 in pattern repeat, 4 in trim, 5% waste. One door (3 × 6.8 ft) and one window (3 × 4 ft).

  1. Roll width 20.5 in = 1.708 ft. Strip drop = 8 + 1 + 0.333 = 9.333 ft.
  2. Strips per roll = floor(33 ÷ 9.333) = floor(3.54) = 3.
  3. Walls: 12 ÷ 1.708 = 7.02 → 8 strips; 14 ÷ 1.708 = 8.20 → 9 strips. Total 8 + 9 + 8 + 9 = 34.
  4. Door: ceil(3 ÷ 1.708) − 1 = 1 strip removed. Window: also 1. That leaves 32 full strips.
  5. Short pieces: above the door 1.2 + 1 + 0.333 = 2.53 ft. Above and below the window 2 + 1 + 0.333 = 3.33 ft each. Together they total 9.2 ft, which fits in one 9.333 ft slot, so 33 slots in all.
  6. Rolls = ceil(33 ÷ 3) = 11. With 5% waste, 11 × 1.05 = 11.55 → 12 double rolls.

The area check (383.6 sq ft net × 1.05 ÷ 56.4 sq ft per roll) gives only 8, so the strip method governs. Ignoring the openings would have given 13 rolls, so they save one roll here.

Tips & Common Mistakes

  • Buy every roll from one batch. Check the batch or lot number on each roll's label. Shade differences between batches show clearly once the paper is on the wall.
  • Enter the roll as sold. US paper is usually a 33 ft double roll, though listings are often priced per 16.5 ft single roll. Put the real continuous length in the roll-length field.
  • Use the full pattern repeat. Check the label for the repeat. For half-drop or large drop-match patterns, enter the full repeat to stay safe.
  • Don't subtract door and window area by hand. A narrow window earns no strip credit, and the paper above and below it still has to be cut from the roll.
  • Measure the tallest point. Stairwells, sloped ceilings and tall alcoves need the highest wall height. Add 10–15% waste for awkward rooms.
  • Ceilings are separate. This calculator covers four walls only. Paper a ceiling by running a separate calculation and adding the roll counts.

Frequently Asked Questions

How many rolls of wallpaper do I need for a 12×12 bedroom?

With 8 ft walls, 20.5 in × 33 ft double rolls, no pattern repeat, 4 in trim and 5% waste, this calculator gives 12 double rolls (base 11). Each 12 ft wall needs 8 strips, and a 33 ft roll yields only 3 strips of 8 ft 4 in. In US listings priced per single roll, that is about 24 single rolls.

What is the difference between a single roll and a double roll?

A US single roll is about 16.5 ft long (roughly 28 sq ft) and a double roll is about 33 ft (roughly 56 sq ft). Many papers are sold in double-roll bolts but priced per single roll. Always enter the length of the continuous roll you are actually buying.

Why does pattern repeat change the number of rolls?

Every strip must start at the same point in the pattern, so up to one full repeat is cut off each strip. On 9 ft walls with a 33 ft roll, a plain paper gives 3 strips per roll. Adding a 24 in repeat makes each strip 11 ft 4 in, so only 2 fit, which means 50% more rolls.

Does the calculator subtract doors and windows?

Yes, but conservatively. It removes only the full strips an opening is guaranteed to cover, then adds back the short pieces needed above doors and above and below windows. Openings narrower than one roll width give no credit.

How much extra wallpaper should I order?

The strip method already includes trim and roll-end offcuts. The waste slider covers cutting mistakes and future repairs. 5% suits plain papers, and 10–15% suits large patterns or awkward rooms.

Does this include the ceiling?

No, it covers the four walls only. For a ceiling, run the calculation again for that surface and add the roll counts together.
