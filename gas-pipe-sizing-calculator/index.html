How to Use This Gas Pipe Sizing Calculator

Pick your gas type, pipe material and supply pressure, then enter the input BTU/h rating from each appliance nameplate. Enter the longest run from the meter (or second-stage regulator) to the farthest appliance, and set a fitting allowance with the slider. The calculator returns the smallest nominal pipe size that carries your total load within the allowable pressure drop, plus a capacity table for every size so you can compare.

Why This Matters

Undersized gas piping is one of the most common hidden defects in remodels and new builds. A pipe that is too small works fine until every appliance runs at once. Then the furnace, water heater and range compete for gas, pressure at the appliance regulators sags, and burners starve. Symptoms include yellow flames, nuisance lockouts, and a furnace that short-cycles on the coldest night of the year.

Typical cases: adding a 75,000 BTU/h tankless water heater to a house plumbed with 1/2-inch lines; running a new 60-foot line to a detached garage heater or outdoor kitchen; swapping a 40,000 BTU/h tank heater for a 199,000 BTU/h tankless unit; or converting a home from oil to gas. A tankless heater alone can pull more than 190 cubic feet of natural gas per hour, which a 3/4-inch line over 80 feet cannot deliver at a 0.5 in. w.c. drop.

Homeowners use the result to sanity-check a quote. Contractors use it for a quick take-off before laying out the system. Inspectors and licensed gas fitters will still size the system against the adopted code tables, but an early estimate avoids surprises on the plan review.

How It's Calculated

The calculator uses the equations behind the IFGC (2021) Section 402.4 and NFPA 54 (National Fuel Gas Code) sizing tables, applied with the longest length method. Flow is in cubic feet per hour (cfh), found by dividing total BTU/h by the gas heating value (about 1,000 BTU/ft³ for natural gas, 2,500 BTU/ft³ for propane).

Low pressure (under 1.5 psi):
D = Q^0.381 / ( 19.17 × (ΔH / (Cr × L))^0.206 )

Elevated pressure (1.5 psi and above):
D = Q^0.381 / ( 18.93 × ((P1² − P2²) × Y / (Cr × L))^0.206 )

D is inside diameter in inches, Q is flow in cfh, ΔH is allowable drop in inches of water column, L is length in feet, and P1 and P2 are inlet and outlet absolute pressures in psia. For natural gas Cr = 0.6094 and Y = 0.9992; for propane Cr = 1.2462 and Y = 0.9910.

The tool rearranges the same equation to find each pipe's capacity. Effective length is your run times (1 + fitting allowance), then rounded up to the next standard table length (10, 20, 30 ft, and so on). The result is the smallest nominal size whose actual inside diameter gives enough capacity. The fitting allowance is a rule-of-thumb stand-in for elbow and tee equivalent lengths, not a code value. Local codes vary, and this tool is an estimate, not engineering, permit or inspection approval.

Worked Example

A house has a 80,000 BTU/h furnace, a 40,000 BTU/h water heater, a 65,000 BTU/h range and a 22,000 BTU/h dryer on natural gas at 7 in. w.c., with a 0.3 in. w.c. allowable drop. The longest run is 50 feet and the fitting allowance is 25%.

  1. Total load = 80,000 + 40,000 + 65,000 + 22,000 = 207,000 BTU/h.
  2. Flow Q = 207,000 ÷ 1,000 = 207 cfh.
  3. Effective length = 50 × 1.25 = 62.5 ft, rounded up to the 70 ft table length.
  4. Minimum inside diameter: D = 207^0.381 ÷ (19.17 × (0.3 ÷ (0.6094 × 70))^0.206) ≈ 7.63 ÷ 6.90 ≈ 1.10 in.
  5. 1-inch Schedule 40 (ID 1.049 in.) carries only about 181 cfh, which is too small. 1-1/4-inch (ID 1.380 in.) carries about 371 cfh.
  6. Result: 1-1/4-inch Schedule 40 pipe, about 56% utilized, with an estimated drop near 0.10 in. w.c.

Tips & Common Mistakes

  • Use input ratings, not output. A 96% efficient furnace labelled 80,000 BTU/h output may have a different input. Size on the input BTU/h printed on the nameplate.
  • Measure to the farthest appliance. The longest length method uses the full length from the meter to the most distant outlet for every section, which keeps the answer on the safe side.
  • Do not forget future appliances. Adding a generator, pool heater or tankless unit later can force a full re-pipe. Add the expected load to the "Other" field now.
  • Match the pressure drop to your supply. Natural gas at 7 in. w.c. is normally sized for 0.3 in. w.c. drop; check the drop your utility or regulator maker allows.
  • CSST needs manufacturer tables. Corrugated stainless tubing is sized by EHD number from the maker's data. Do not substitute steel results. Check also that copper is permitted locally, since many jurisdictions restrict it for fuel gas.

Frequently Asked Questions

What size gas pipe do I need for a tankless water heater?

A 199,000 BTU/h tankless unit draws about 199 cfh of natural gas. Over a 50 ft run at 0.5 in. w.c. drop, that usually calls for 1-inch pipe or larger, and the total includes every other appliance on the same line. Run the calculator with all loads for a reliable answer.

How do I convert BTU/h to cubic feet per hour?

Divide the BTU/h rating by the heating value of the gas. Natural gas is about 1,000 BTU per cubic foot, so 100,000 BTU/h is 100 cfh. Propane is about 2,500 BTU per cubic foot, so the same load is 40 cfh of vapor.

Why does propane need smaller pipe than natural gas?

Propane carries about 2.5 times the energy per cubic foot, so the same BTU load needs far less volume. Propane is also typically supplied at 11 in. w.c. rather than 7, which allows a larger pressure drop. Both factors reduce the required diameter.

What is the longest length method?

It sizes every pipe section using the total length from the meter to the farthest appliance, even for short branches. Each section carries only the load it serves. The method is simple and conservative, which is why it is widely used for residential layouts.

Does this calculator size CSST or polyethylene tubing?

No. CSST and PE tubing are sized from manufacturer-specific capacity tables, because their flow resistance differs from rigid pipe. This tool covers Schedule 40 steel and Type L copper only.

Is the result enough to get a permit?

No. It is an estimate based on the IFGC / NFPA 54 formulas, and local amendments, utility rules and inspector judgment all apply. Have a licensed gas fitter confirm the design and obtain the required permit and pressure test before any gas is turned on.
