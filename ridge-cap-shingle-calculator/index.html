How to Use This Ridge Cap Shingle Calculator

Measure the total length of every ridge and every hip on your roof and enter them above. Pick the type of ridge cap you plan to buy, or type the linear-foot coverage printed on the bundle wrapper, then set a waste allowance. The calculator returns the number of bundles to buy (always rounded up), the number of cap pieces, nails, and a cost estimate if you add a price.

Why This Matters

Ridge caps are the most exposed shingles on the roof. They sit at the peak, take the hardest sun and wind, and seal the joint where two slopes meet. Running short mid-job is costly: a roofer standing on a half-finished ridge can't wait for another delivery, and a later purchase may come from a different dye lot with a visibly different color along the roofline.

Ridge cap is sold by linear coverage, not by roof area, so square-based shingle estimates don't help. A 2,400 sq ft hip roof might have 40 ft of ridge and 84 ft of hips, so 124 linear feet of cap, about five or six bundles. A simple 30-ft gable has only a 30-ft ridge, which is just two bundles at 20 to 25 lf each. Homeowners pricing a reroof, roofers writing bids and contractors ordering materials all need this number before the truck arrives.

Complex roofs add up fast. Every dormer, cross-gable and hip adds cap length, and cutting angled ends at hip-and-ridge intersections wastes material. That's why a waste allowance of 10% to 15% is standard, and why this tool shows how the bundle count changes at different waste levels.

How It's Calculated

The calculator adds ridge and hip lengths, applies your waste percentage, and divides by the coverage of one bundle:

Total LF = Ridge + Hips
LF with waste = Total LF × (1 + waste %)
Bundles = ROUNDUP(LF with waste ÷ coverage per bundle)
Cap pieces = ROUNDUP(LF with waste × 12 ÷ exposure in inches)
Nails = Cap pieces × nails per piece

If you don't know the hip length, a regular hip roof gives it as: hip = √(2 × run² + rise²), where run is the horizontal distance from the eave to the ridge line and rise is the vertical height. Metric entries are converted at 1 m = 3.28084 ft. The default of 2 nails per cap piece reflects common manufacturer instructions; the 4-nail option is for high-wind areas or where the manufacturer requires it. Asphalt shingle fastening falls under the International Residential Code (R905.2), which defers to the manufacturer's installation instructions. Local codes vary, so check yours. This tool is an estimating aid, not a permit, engineering or inspection approval.

Worked Example

A 28 ft × 40 ft hip roof at 6/12 pitch. Each hip's horizontal run is 14 ft on each side and the rise is 7 ft (6/12 × 14 ft).

  • Hip length: √(2 × 14² + 7²) = √441 = 21 ft each; four hips = 84 ft.
  • Ridge length: 40 ft − 28 ft = 12 ft.
  • Total: 12 + 84 = 96 linear feet.
  • With 10% waste: 96 × 1.10 = 105.6 ft.
  • Bundles at 25 lf each: 105.6 ÷ 25 = 4.22, rounded up to 5 bundles.
  • Cap pieces at 5" exposure: 105.6 × 12 ÷ 5 = 253.4, rounded up to 254 pieces, so 508 nails at 2 per piece.

To check, enter 12 for ridge, 84 for hips, 25 for coverage and 10% waste. You'll get the same result.

Tips & Common Mistakes

  • Measure hips along the slope. Using the horizontal run understates the length of a 6/12 hip by roughly 10% and more on steeper roofs.
  • Don't forget small ridges. Dormers, porch roofs and cross-gables each add ridge and hip footage that's easy to overlook.
  • Check the wrapper coverage. Thicker, high-profile caps cover less per bundle (often around 20 lf) than thin economy caps. Don't assume one figure fits all brands.
  • Round up, and buy matching lots. One extra bundle is cheap insurance against color mismatch and cutting waste. Keep leftovers for repairs.
  • Follow the nailing pattern. Use the nail length and count on the wrapper. Ridge caps are thick, and a nail that's too short can back out. In high-wind regions choose the 4-nail option or whatever the manufacturer specifies.

Frequently Asked Questions

How many bundles of ridge cap shingles do I need?

Divide your total ridge and hip length, plus 10% waste, by the linear coverage printed on the bundle (usually 20 to 35 lf), then round up. A 100 ft run with 10% waste needs 5 bundles at 25 lf each.

How many linear feet does one bundle of ridge cap cover?

Most purpose-made architectural ridge caps cover roughly 20 to 33 linear feet per bundle, depending on thickness and exposure. Always use the figure on the wrapper of the product you're buying.

Can I cut 3-tab shingles into ridge caps?

Yes, a 12" × 36" 3-tab shingle can be cut into three 12" pieces. At 5" exposure that's about 15 inches of ridge per shingle. Purpose-made caps are thicker and usually look and hold up better, so many manufacturers recommend them.

How much waste should I add for ridge cap shingles?

Use 10% for simple roofs with a few hips and 15% to 20% for complex roofs with many intersections and dormers. Angled cuts at hip-to-ridge joints use more material than straight runs.

Do hips and ridges use the same shingle?

Yes, the same ridge cap product is used on both hips and ridges, which is why the calculator adds them together. Valleys are different. They're normally woven or cut with field shingles and are not capped.

How many nails do ridge cap shingles need?

Two nails per cap piece is the usual minimum, placed where the manufacturer marks, and many high-wind installations use four. Follow the wrapper instructions and local code, which may require more than a general estimate.
