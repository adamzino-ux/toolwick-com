How to Use This Soffit & Fascia Calculator

Measure your eaves and rakes separately and enter the totals (or use Multi-Side Mode if overhangs differ from side to side). Then set the soffit panel width, fascia height and board width, board lengths, prices and waste. Press Calculate to see whole soffit panels and fascia boards to buy, trim and fastener counts, an edge-by-edge table and an estimated cost.

Why This Matters

Soffit and fascia are the trim pieces people most often get wrong, because the roof edge looks simple. The quantities add up fast. A 40 × 28 ft gable house, with the gable ends on the 28 ft sides, has about 80 ft of eaves and about 63 ft of rakes. With a 16-inch eave overhang that is roughly 107 sq ft of soffit coverage. But a 16-inch run needs two 12-inch panel rows, so you actually buy about 160 linear feet of panel before waste.

Come up short and you make a second trip mid-project, with the risk of a mismatched dye lot on vinyl or aluminum. Over-order and you pay for leftovers: at $2.50 per sq ft, an extra 40 sq ft of soffit is $100 sitting in the garage. Accurate counts also help you budget a re-roof, since fascia and soffit are cheapest to replace while the roof edge is already open. Contractors use this to price bids, DIYers to write a shopping list, and buyers to sanity-check a quote.

Rakes get their own input because they usually need fascia but not soffit. Lumping them in with eaves would count soffit you may not actually install, so enter a rake overhang only when your gable ends are boxed in.

How It's Calculated

  • Rake length (if you only know the run) = run × √(1 + (pitch ÷ 12)²). For a 6:12 pitch the multiplier is about 1.118.
  • Soffit rows per edge = ⌈ overhang ÷ panel width ⌉. A 24 in overhang with 12 in panels is 2 rows. A 16 in overhang is also 2 rows.
  • Panel run (lin ft) = edge length × rows. Panels to buy = ⌈ panel run × (1 + waste %) ÷ panel length ⌉.
  • Net soffit area (sq ft) = edge length × overhang ÷ 12. This is the coverage figure, shown for reference.
  • Fascia courses = ⌈ height to cover ÷ board width ⌉. Boards = ⌈ length × courses × (1 + waste %) ÷ board length ⌉, counted separately for eaves and rakes because rake boards are cut on a slope.
  • Channel (J- or F-channel, 12.5 ft pieces) = ⌈ 2 × soffit edge length × (1 + waste %) ÷ 12.5 ⌉, one run at the wall and one at the fascia.
  • Fasteners: soffit one per 16 in of panel run. Fascia two per 16 in, per course. Both include the waste allowance.
  • Cost = pieces to buy × piece size × your unit price, so you see what you will actually pay at the counter. Soffit is priced on the full panel area you buy, which is larger than the net coverage area.

Rounding is always up, never down. Fastener spacing and the 12.5 ft channel length are common rules of thumb, not a code requirement. Your panel manufacturer's installation instructions and local code (for example IRC R703 for exterior coverings and R806 for roof ventilation) take priority, and local codes vary. Soffit also usually supplies attic intake ventilation. A common IRC baseline is 1 sq ft of net free vent area per 150 sq ft of attic floor, or 1:300 under certain conditions, so check that solid panels don't block your intake vents.

Worked Example

A gable house is 40 ft long and 28 ft wide, with gable ends on the 28 ft sides and a 6:12 roof pitch. The two eaves are 40 ft each, so enter 80 ft. Each gable end has two rakes of 14 × 1.118 ≈ 15.65 ft, so the four rakes total about 62.6 ft. Eave overhang is 16 in with no rake soffit. Panels are 12 in × 12 ft, fascia is 7.25 in boards (1×8) covering a 6 in face, 12 ft boards, 10% waste, soffit at $2.50 per sq ft and fascia at $2.80 per lin ft.

  • Soffit rows = ⌈16 ÷ 12⌉ = 2. Panel run = 80 × 2 = 160 lin ft. With waste: 176 lin ft ÷ 12 = 14.67, so 15 panels. Net coverage is 80 × 16 ÷ 12 = 106.7 sq ft. Buying 15 × 12 sq ft = 180 sq ft costs $450.00.
  • Fascia courses = ⌈6 ÷ 7.25⌉ = 1. Eaves: 88 lin ft ÷ 12 = 7.33, so 8 boards. Rakes: 68.9 lin ft ÷ 12 = 5.74, so 6 boards. 14 boards × 12 ft = 168 lin ft × $2.80 = $470.40.
  • Channel: 2 × 80 × 1.1 = 176 ÷ 12.5 = 14.08, so 15 pieces. Fasteners: soffit 132, fascia 236.
  • Estimated material cost: $450.00 + $470.40 = $920.40.

Tips & Common Mistakes

  • Measure overhang horizontally from the wall to the back of the fascia, not along the rafter slope.
  • Measure rakes along the slope. A 28 ft gable end at 6:12 pitch has two rakes totaling about 31 ft, not 28.
  • Don't assume rakes have soffit. Enter a rake overhang only if your design has a boxed rake.
  • Order extra from one batch so colors and profiles match, and keep a few spare pieces for future repairs.
  • Keep vents in the plan. Use vented soffit panels where your attic needs intake air, and use stainless or hot-dip galvanized fasteners, which resist staining and corrosion.

Frequently Asked Questions

What's the difference between soffit and fascia?

Fascia is the vertical board along the roof edge that caps the rafter tails and carries the gutter. Soffit is the horizontal panel that closes the underside of the overhang between the fascia and the wall. Together they keep weather, pests and moisture out of the rafters and attic.

How do I measure the perimeter for a hip roof?

Add the lengths of all four eaves at the bottom edge of the roof. A true hip roof has no rakes, so enter 0 for rake length. On a gable roof, enter the two long eaves as eave length and the sloped gable edges as rake length.

How many soffit panels do I need for a 16-inch overhang?

With standard 12-inch panels you need two rows along every eave, because 16 ÷ 12 rounds up to 2. The calculator does this per edge, so a 24-inch overhang uses about twice as many panels as a 12-inch one.

Do rakes need soffit and fascia?

Rakes almost always get fascia (a rake or barge board), but most have little or no soffit. Enter a rake overhang only if your gable ends are boxed in. Rake boards are cut on a slope, so the calculator counts them separately from eave boards.

How much waste should I allow?

Ten percent works for simple gable roofs with long, straight runs. Use 15% or more for hip roofs, dormers, and lots of corners or vent cuts. Because the tool also rounds up to whole pieces, your real overage will usually be a bit higher than the percentage you enter.

Should I choose vinyl, aluminum, wood or fiber cement?

Vinyl is the lowest-cost and easiest to cut. Aluminum holds paint and suits harsh climates, wood looks best but needs regular painting, and fiber cement is the most durable but heavy and pricier to install. The sample prices are placeholders, so enter your own quotes.
