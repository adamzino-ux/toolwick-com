How to Use This Knee Wall Height Calculator

Enter your roof pitch, the building span measured outside to outside, and how long the knee walls run. Then set the knee wall height with the slider or number box, measured from the finished attic floor to the finished sloped ceiling at the room-side face of the wall. The calculator returns stud cut lengths (long and short point) with the top bevel angle, the floor depth and area behind the walls for storage, the room width between the walls, and a headroom check. A comparison table lists 36 to 60 in walls side by side.

Why This Matters

A knee wall is the short wall that holds up the sloped part of a finished attic or half-story ceiling. Its height decides three things at once: how much floor you keep as living space, how deep the storage is behind the wall, and how much lumber you cut. Homeowners converting a Cape Cod attic, builders framing a bonus room, and anyone planning attic storage all face the same trade-off: a taller wall gives deeper storage behind it but takes floor space from the room.

Pitch matters more than most people expect. The floor behind a wall runs from the point where the finished slope meets the floor out to the wall. In feet, that is the wall height in inches divided by the pitch number. A 48 in wall on a 12:12 roof has about 4 ft behind it, while the same wall on a 6:12 roof has about 8 ft. But that shallower roof may never reach 7 ft of headroom: a 30 ft wide 6:12 house is only about 6.75 ft tall inside at the ridge. Checking the numbers before you buy lumber prevents miscut studs and expensive reframing.

How It's Calculated

The geometry is plain trigonometry, not a code formula. Let m = pitch ÷ 12. All heights are measured from the finished attic floor.

  1. Finished roof surface. The roof plane is assumed to start at the outside face of the exterior wall at floor level. The finished ceiling sits below it by the rafter depth plus finish, t, measured square to the slope. The vertical drop is d = t × √(1 + m²). The finished slope meets the floor d ÷ m inside each exterior wall.
  2. Finished floor width. W = Span − 2 × (d ÷ m) ÷ 12 (ft, with d in inches). Finished ridge height is W ÷ 2 × m.
  3. Wall position. The slope reaches height H at H ÷ pitch feet from where it meets the floor. That is the room-side face of the wall.
  4. Storage depth behind the wall. H ÷ pitch − stud depth ÷ 12. With a clearance requirement c: (H − c) ÷ pitch − stud depth ÷ 12. Area is depth × 2 sides × run length.
  5. Studs. Long point = H − 1.5 in per plate. Short point = long point − stud depth × m. The top cut angle from square is arctan(m).
  6. Headroom. Width with at least h ft of headroom is W − 2 × 12h ÷ pitch, limited by the flat ceiling or ridge. For the 5 ft measure, the wall face is used if the wall is taller than 5 ft.

Rounding is conservative: lengths and areas are rounded down, and stud and plate counts are rounded up. The headroom check follows the commonly cited IRC R305.1 sloped-ceiling rule (7 ft over at least 50% of the room, no area counted below 5 ft). The storage-load note refers to IRC Table R301.5. Editions and local amendments vary, so confirm with your building department. This tool is a planning estimate, not engineering, permit or inspection approval.

Worked Example

Take the default inputs: a 26 ft wide house, 12:12 pitch, knee walls running 32 ft, 48 in wall height, 2×4 studs at 16 in on center, a double top plate, 8 in rafter-plus-finish, an 8 ft flat ceiling and an 18 in storage clearance.

  1. Floor width: m = 1, so d = 8 × √2 = 11.31 in. The slope meets the floor 0.943 ft inside each wall, so W = 26 − 1.886 = 24.11 ft.
  2. Wall position: 48 ÷ 12 = 4.00 ft from where the slope meets the floor.
  3. Studs: three plates × 1.5 = 4.5 in, so the long point is 43 1/2 in. The short point is 43.5 − 3.5 × 1 = 40 in, with a 45.0° top cut.
  4. Behind the wall: 4 − 3.5 ÷ 12 = 3.708 ft (shown as 3.70 ft). With 18 in clearance: (48 − 18) ÷ 12 − 0.292 = 2.208 ft (shown as 2.20 ft).
  5. Areas: 2 × 3.708 × 32 = 237.3 sq ft of floor behind both walls. Usable: 2 × 2.208 × 32 = 141.3 sq ft, or 18.3% of the 771.6 sq ft finished floor.
  6. Room: 24.11 − 8 = 16.11 ft between the walls. The 8 ft flat ceiling is 24.11 − 16 = 8.11 ft wide. The finished ridge is 12.05 ft.
  7. Headroom: 14.11 ft of floor has 5 ft or more, and 10.11 ft has 7 ft or more. That is 71.6%, above the 50% rule of thumb.
  8. Lumber: 384 in ÷ 16 = 24 spaces + 1 = 25 studs per wall. Order 27 per wall (54 total) and 212 lin ft of plate stock including 10% waste.

Tips & Common Mistakes

  • Measure to the finished surface. Rafter depth, insulation and drywall lower the real ceiling. Measure on site and enter the actual rafter-plus-finish thickness.
  • Cut one test stud first. Floors and rafters are rarely perfect. Check the top bevel with a bevel gauge before cutting the whole batch.
  • Don't confuse storage depth with usable depth. The strip near the eave is too low for boxes. Keep ventilation channels and baffles clear, because insulation and baffles shrink the space further.
  • Check the floor under the storage. Attic ceiling joists are often sized for about 10 psf, while IRC Table R301.5 lists higher loads for attics with storage. Knee walls should also bear on framing below. Have a qualified person verify both.
  • Don't forget the plates. Stud length is wall height minus every plate. Skipping a 1.5 in plate leaves studs too long.
  • Plan access. Frame at least one access door per knee wall run, or the storage cannot be used.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a standard knee wall height?

Most residential knee walls are 36 to 60 inches tall, and 48 inches is a common choice. Taller walls give deeper, more usable storage behind them but narrow the room between the walls.

Does roof pitch affect how much storage I get?

Yes, and steeper pitch gives less depth behind a given wall. The floor behind a wall is roughly the wall height in inches divided by the pitch number, in feet. A 48 in wall leaves about 12 ft at 4:12, 6 ft at 8:12 and 4 ft at 12:12. Steeper roofs do give more headroom in the room itself.

How long should knee wall studs be?

Start with the wall height and subtract all plates, 1.5 inches each. That is the long point. The short point is shorter by the stud depth times pitch ÷ 12, because the top is cut to follow the roof slope.

What angle do I cut the top of a knee wall stud?

The cut angle from square is arctan(pitch ÷ 12). That is 45.0° for 12:12 and about 26.6° for 6:12. The calculator shows this for your pitch.

How much headroom does a finished attic room need?

A commonly cited IRC rule requires 7 ft of ceiling height over at least half the room, with no floor counted below 5 ft. Local codes differ, so ask your building department before you finish the space.

Can knee walls be framed at 24 inches on center?

Sometimes, but it depends on what the wall carries. Many builders use 16 inches on center because knee walls often support rafters or collar loads. Check your local code or an engineer for your specific roof.
