Why This Matters

Bond choice changes the bill more than most people expect. On a 10 × 4 ft wall built with standard 8 × 2.25 in brick and 3/8 in joints, running bond needs about 263 bricks. English bond needs about 395, because every second course is a header course that shows only the 3.75 in end of each brick instead of the 8 in side. That is roughly 50% more brick, plus extra cutting and labor.

Homeowners use a brick bond pattern calculator to price a garden wall or veneer before collecting quotes. Contractors use it when a client wants a Flemish look and needs to know what it costs against plain running bond. Landscapers planning a 30 ft seat wall need the same thing: a count that does not run out halfway. Running short is expensive because brick lots vary in color, and a second batch rarely matches. Ordering 5–10% above the net count is normal practice, and complex bonds with many cuts deserve the high end.

Bond also affects behavior. Running bond overlaps vertical joints so loads and cracks spread across the wall. Stack bond lines every joint up, which looks crisp but is usually treated as decorative and needs reinforcement.

How It's Calculated

Everything is converted to inches. A brick plus one mortar joint forms a unit. A stretcher shows the long face, so it is one length plus a joint wide. A header shows the short end, so it is one depth plus a joint wide. A soldier stands on end: it is one height plus a joint wide and one length plus a joint tall.

Courses = Wall height ÷ (Brick height + joint)
(soldier: Brick length + joint)
Stretcher course = Wall width ÷ (Length + joint)
Header course = Wall width ÷ (Depth + joint)
Flemish course = 2 × Wall width ÷ (Length + Depth + 2 × joint)
Net bricks = sum of all courses (top partial course counted by fraction), rounded up once
Order quantity = Net bricks × (1 + Waste ÷ 100), rounded up

Rounding happens once at the end, not per course, so large walls are not overstated. The course table therefore shows fractional counts: 14.3 means 14 whole bricks plus a cut piece. English bond alternates stretcher and header courses, so its two course types have very different counts. Flemish alternates header and stretcher inside every course.

For solid walls, stretcher courses are doubled for the hidden wythe, and each Flemish header and stretcher pair counts one header, one stretcher and one backing brick. Mortar is the joint volume around each brick, (unit width × unit height × depth) − brick volume, plus 15% waste, divided by about 0.6 cu ft per 80 lb bag and rounded up. Bag yields vary, so check your label.

Worked Example

A 10 × 4 ft wall (120 × 48 in), standard 8 × 2.25 × 3.75 in brick, 3/8 in joint, running bond, face only, 5% waste:

  1. Brick unit: 8 + 0.375 = 8.375 in wide and 2.25 + 0.375 = 2.625 in tall.
  2. Courses: 48 ÷ 2.625 = 18.29, so 19 courses are laid and the top one is cut to about 29% height.
  3. Bricks per course: 120 ÷ 8.375 = 14.33.
  4. Net bricks: 14.33 × 18.29 = 262.0, rounded up to 263 (6.55 bricks per sq ft, or 144 ÷ 21.98).
  5. With waste: 263 × 1.05 = 276.15, rounded up to 277 bricks.
  6. Mortar: (8.375 × 2.625 × 3.75) − (8 × 2.25 × 3.75) = 14.94 cu in per brick. Times 262.0 gives 3,915 cu in, or 2.27 cu ft. Adding 15% gives 2.61 cu ft, divided by 0.6 is 4.3, so 5 bags.

Switch the same wall to English bond. The 18 full courses split into 9 stretcher courses at 14.33 bricks and 9 header courses at 120 ÷ 4.125 = 29.09 bricks, plus the partial stretcher course. The total is 395 net bricks, about 51% more than running bond. Flemish lands in between at 352.

Tips & Common Mistakes

  • Enter actual brick sizes, not nominal. Nominal sizes already include the joint, so adding a joint again undercounts. Use the manufacturer's spec sheet.
  • Deduct openings by area. Each sq ft of window or door removes about 6.5 standard bricks, but add bricks back for soldier courses, sills and the extra cutting around openings.
  • Use the high end of waste for Flemish, English and corner-heavy walls. Closers, bats and cut bricks at ends and openings are not free; 8–10% is realistic, 5% suits simple running bond.
  • Check the joint that fits your height. The breakdown shows the joint thickness that gives whole courses. Keep joints uniform rather than squeezing the top course.
  • Do not treat stack bond as structural without reinforcement. It is commonly detailed with horizontal joint reinforcement; confirm spacing and amount with your local code and designer.
  • "Face only" is not a solid wall. A one-brick-thick English or Flemish wall has a hidden back wythe, so choose the solid option.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the most common brick bond pattern?

Running bond, also called stretcher bond, is the most common pattern in modern construction. Each course is offset by half a brick, which spreads loads and needs little cutting. Most residential veneer and garden walls use it.

How many bricks do I need per square foot?

With standard 8 × 2.25 in brick and 3/8 in joints you need about 6.55 bricks per sq ft, or roughly 655 per 100 sq ft before waste (144 ÷ 8.375 ÷ 2.625). Modular brick with 3/8 in joints is closer to 6.86 per sq ft. The calculator uses your exact dimensions.

Why does English bond need so many more bricks than running bond?

A header course shows only the short end of each brick, so it takes about twice as many bricks per course as a stretcher course. With half the courses as headers, the face count rises by about 50%. In a solid wall those headers also tie the two wythes together.

Is Flemish bond stronger than running bond?

Not meaningfully for a single-wythe wall; Flemish is chosen mainly for its look. English bond, with full header courses, is the traditional choice for thick solid walls. Modern strength depends more on ties, mortar and reinforcement than on the bond name.

Can I use this for concrete block (CMU)?

Yes. Choose the 8×8×16 preset, which uses the actual 15.625 × 7.625 × 7.625 in size, and keep a 3/8 in joint. Running and stack bond are the realistic choices; English and Flemish are rarely used with block.

How much waste should I add?

Five percent suits a simple running bond wall. Use 8–10% for Flemish, English, soldier courses or walls with many corners and openings. When unsure, round up, because matching a second brick lot is difficult.
