Why This Matters

A pergola looks light, but the structure carries real loads: its own weight, snow, wind uplift and the occasional person hanging a swing or a planter from it. Most failures come from the same few mistakes: beams that span too far between posts, posts that are too slim for their height, and footings that are too shallow or too small. Getting the numbers right before you shop saves money and prevents a rebuild.

Consider a typical 12 by 16 foot pergola over a patio. Builders often drop in four posts because it looks clean, but a double 2x10 beam can only span about 9 feet under a standard 50 psf load, so a 16 foot side needs a third post in the middle. That is six posts, six footings and roughly 39 bags of concrete rather than four posts and 26 bags. Knowing that up front lets you budget correctly, order the right lengths of lumber and avoid mid-project trips to the yard. It also helps when you talk to a contractor, because you can check their quote against a clear materials list.

How It's Calculated

The calculator uses rule-of-thumb spans derived from the International Residential Code deck tables: R507.5 for beams, R507.6 for joists (used here for rafters) and R507.4 for post height. Those tables assume Southern Pine No. 2 under 40 psf live plus 10 psf dead load. A pergola is not a deck, so this is a conservative approximation, not a code compliance check. Local codes vary.

  • Design load: q = max(snow, 20) + 5 psf. If q is above 50 psf, spans are scaled by √(50 / q). Hem-fir and cedar are scaled down a further 10 to 15 percent.
  • Beam span: tributary width is T = W / 2 + overhang. The max span comes from the next-higher tabulated width, then is rounded down to 0.1 ft.
  • Posts: spans = ceil(L / max span), then posts = 2 × (spans + 1). Posts are 6x6 if they are over 8 ft tall, the beam is a double 2x10 or larger, snow is above 40 psf, or tributary area exceeds 64 sq ft.
  • Rafters: if your width exceeds the rafter's max span, the tool upsizes the rafter. Count is ceil(total length / spacing) + 1.
  • Footings: depth is the greater of 30 in or your frost depth, rounded up to the next 6 in. Diameter is √(4P / (π × bearing)) with P the post load, rounded up to an even inch with a 12 in minimum. Concrete includes 10 percent waste, at 0.6 cu ft per 80 lb bag.
  • Lumber lengths: every piece is rounded up to the next stock length (8, 10, 12, 14, 16 or 20 ft).

Worked Example

Take a 16 by 12 ft pergola, 8 ft post height, 12 in overhang, 2x10 rafters at 16 in, double 2x10 beams, 2x2 slats at 12 in, Southern Pine, 30 psf snow, 24 in frost depth and 1,500 psf soil.

  1. Tributary width is 12 ÷ 2 + 1 = 7 ft, so the table gives a 9.0 ft max beam span. 16 ÷ 9 rounds up to 2 spans of 8 ft, so 6 posts.
  2. 2x10 rafters at 16 in can span 14 ft, and your width is 12 ft, so they pass. Rafters are 12 + 2 = 14 ft long. (216 in ÷ 16 in) rounds up to 14, plus 1, gives 15 rafters.
  3. Posts are 6x6 because the beam is a double 2x10. Buried length is 8 + 2.5 = 10.5 ft, so buy 12 ft stock.
  4. Footing depth is 30 in. Post load is 8 × 7 × 35 psf = 1,960 lb, which needs 1.31 sq ft at 1,500 psf, or a 15.5 in circle. That rounds up to 16 in diameter. Six footings use about 23 cu ft with waste, so 39 bags.
  5. Lumber is about 800 board feet, or roughly $1,399 at $1.75 per board foot. Shade from rafters plus slats is about 21 percent.

Tips & Common Mistakes

  • Don't copy a 4-post layout for a long side. The beam, not the posts, usually decides how many posts you need.
  • Use through-bolts and post caps. Wind lifts a pergola as easily as snow pushes it down, so toenailed connections are not enough. Hurricane ties at every rafter are cheap insurance.
  • Buy ground-contact lumber for buried posts. Standard treated lumber rots quickly in soil. Cedar and redwood need to be heartwood grades for contact.
  • Check for utilities before you dig. Call 811 in the US at least a few days before digging footings.
  • Keep overhangs modest. A cantilever longer than a quarter of the supported span invites sagging, so keep overhangs short on long spans.

Frequently Asked Questions

How many posts do I need for a 12x16 pergola?

Usually six: three along each 16 ft side. A double 2x10 beam spans about 9 ft under a standard load, so one span of 16 ft is too long. Run the calculator with your beam size, since a double 2x12 can sometimes reach farther.

How far can a pergola beam span?

For a double 2x10 supporting a 12 ft wide pergola with a 1 ft overhang, about 9 ft. A double 2x12 reaches roughly 10 ft 6 in. Heavy snow or weaker lumber such as cedar shortens those spans.

What size posts should I use for a pergola?

4x4 posts only suit small, low pergolas up to 8 ft tall. Most pergolas use 6x6 posts, which resist bending and give you room to attach beams and brackets. The tool picks 6x6 whenever height, beam size, snow or load area calls for it.

How deep should pergola post footings be?

At least as deep as your local frost line, and this tool never goes shallower than 30 in so there is resistance to wind uplift. In cold climates the frost depth can be 36 to 60 in. Your building department can tell you the exact number.

Do I need a permit to build a pergola?

Often yes, especially if it is attached to your house, taller than a set height or larger than a set area, commonly 120 to 200 square feet. Rules differ widely by city and county. Call your local building office before buying materials.

How far apart should pergola rafters be?

For shade and a classic look, 12 to 16 in on center is common. 24 in works for a lighter look or when you add slats on top. Wider spacing also reduces the span a rafter can safely cover, so the calculator checks this for you.
