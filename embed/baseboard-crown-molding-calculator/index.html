Why This Matters

Trim is the last material most people order and the first one they run short on. A 12×14 ft bedroom has 52 linear feet of wall, and a first floor with a living room, kitchen, hall and dining room can pass 200 feet. At that scale, a wrong guess means a second trip to the store or a stack of offcuts you cannot return.

The real trap is that trim is cut wall by wall. A 14 ft wall with 12 ft boards needs one full board plus a short scarfed piece of about 2 ft. That short piece is only reused if another cut is small enough to share the board. Dividing total feet by board length hides this, so jobs ordered that way often come up a board or two short. Matching lots also matter for painted or stained trim, because a later batch can differ slightly in colour and profile. Homeowners pricing a DIY room, remodelers writing quotes and anyone comparing 8, 12 and 16 ft stock can use this tool to plan before buying.

How It's Calculated

No building code sets how much molding to buy. The method follows common trade rules of thumb: cut each wall separately, join long runs with scarf joints, and add 10% or more for waste. Local codes vary for other trim-related items such as fire-rated assemblies. This is a materials estimate, not engineering, permit or inspection approval, and every rounding step goes up, never down.

  1. Walls. A rectangular room has two walls as long as the width and two as long as the length.
  2. Openings. Baseboard deducts each opening from its own wall. Crown deducts nothing by default, because it runs over doorways, and only deducts if you tick the option.
  3. Pieces. A wall with n openings is split into n+1 equal pieces, since the exact door position is unknown.
  4. Long runs. A run longer than the board is cut as full boards plus a remainder. Each scarf joint overlaps by 0.25 ft (3 in). If the remainder would be under 2 ft, the run is split into equal pieces instead.
  5. Packing. Pieces are placed largest first into boards. Each cut uses a 0.125 ft (1.5 in) allowance for baseboard or 0.25 ft (3 in) for crown, because coped crown joints consume more length.
  6. Waste floor. Net run × (1 + waste %) is divided by board length and rounded up.

Boards = max( boards from cut layout , ⌈ Net run × (1 + Waste%) ÷ Board length ⌉ )

Cost = Boards × Price per board

The result is a minimum practical count for a rectangular room, and a typical room should be close to it. Your actual joints depend on where you cut, so check wall lengths on site.

Worked Example

A 12 × 14 ft room has a 3 ft doorway on the bottom wall. Baseboard uses 12 ft boards at $14.00, crown uses 16 ft boards at $38.00, waste is 10%, and the crown deduction is off.

  • Perimeter: 2 × 12 + 2 × 14 = 52 ft.
  • Baseboard net run: 52 − 3 = 49 ft. The top wall is one 12 ft piece. The bottom wall is 9 ft, split into two 4.50 ft pieces. The left and right walls are each 14 ft, cut as a 12 ft piece plus a 2.25 ft piece (the extra 0.25 ft is the joint overlap).
  • Baseboard layout: The three 12 ft pieces take three boards. The two 4.50 ft pieces and one 2.25 ft piece share a fourth board, and the last 2.25 ft piece needs a fifth. The layout gives 5 boards. The waste check gives 49 × 1.10 = 53.9 ft ÷ 12 = 4.49, rounded up to 5. Boards to buy: 5, costing 5 × $14.00 = $70.00.
  • Crown run: the full 52 ft, because crown runs over the door. The pieces are 12, 12, 14 and 14 ft, and no two fit in one 16 ft board. The layout gives 4 boards. The waste check gives 52 × 1.10 = 57.2 ft ÷ 16 = 3.58, rounded up to 4. Boards to buy: 4, costing 4 × $38.00 = $152.00.
  • Total: 9 boards and $222.00 in material.

Tips & Common Mistakes

  • Measure every wall, not just two. Old rooms are rarely square. If walls differ by more than an inch or two, use the longest of each pair.
  • Raise the waste to 15% or more for crown, first-time installers, or rooms with bay windows, diagonals or many outside corners.
  • Only deduct from crown where it really stops. Crown normally runs across doorways and above windows. Tick the crown option for walls where it ends, such as a built-in or a cased opening that rises to the ceiling.
  • Don't deduct windows. Baseboard runs under windows and crown runs above them.
  • Buy all trim in one trip from the same lot, and keep the receipt for returning whole uncut boards.
  • Use the longest stock you can carry. Fewer joints look better, but a board longer than the wall by a few inches is easier to fit than one exactly the wall's length.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I deduct doorways from crown molding?

Usually not. Crown runs across the top of a doorway, so this calculator uses the full wall length for crown by default. Tick the crown deduction box only where the molding genuinely stops.

How much extra baseboard should I buy?

Ten percent is the usual minimum for simple rectangular rooms. Use 15 to 20% if you are new to coping and mitering or the room has many corners. The calculator also lays out each wall's cuts, so it can suggest more boards than the waste figure alone.

Should I buy 8 ft, 12 ft or 16 ft boards?

Pick the length that leaves the fewest joints on your longest walls. A 12 ft board covers a 12 ft wall in one piece, while a 14 ft wall needs a joint with any stock under 14 ft. Try different lengths in the calculator and compare the boards and cost.

Why does it suggest more boards than total feet divided by board length?

Every wall is cut separately and short remainders can only be shared where they fit on a board. For example, two 14 ft walls with 12 ft stock leave two 2.25 ft pieces that need board space of their own. Total length alone ignores that.

Can I use it for L-shaped rooms or bump-outs?

For an L-shaped room with one notched corner, the perimeter equals the bounding rectangle's perimeter, so you can enter the full width and length. The wall-by-wall layout is then approximate, so raise the waste to 15%. For more complex shapes, add a few spare boards.

How do I measure a room for baseboard?

Measure each wall along the floor from corner to corner and note each doorway's width, including the casing if the baseboard butts against it. Enter the room as width and length in feet, converting inches to decimals (6 in = 0.5 ft).
