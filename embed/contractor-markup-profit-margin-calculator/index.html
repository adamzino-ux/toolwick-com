Why This Matters

Confusing markup with margin is one of the most expensive mistakes in contracting. A contractor who adds 20% to cost believes they are earning a 20% profit, but the margin on the invoice is only 16.7%. On a $60,000 remodel that gap is roughly $2,000 of profit that never shows up. If you wanted a true 20% margin, you needed a 25% markup.

Small errors compound across a year of work. A remodeler doing $800,000 in annual sales at a real 16.7% margin instead of 20% leaves about $26,000 on the table. Specialty trades, handymen and general contractors all face the same arithmetic, and so do owners reviewing bids who want to know how much room a quote has. Lenders and bonding companies also look at margins, not markups.

Use this tool when you build an estimate, when a client pushes back on price and you need to know how low you can go, or when you are comparing a subcontractor's quote against your own numbers. It also helps when you are translating advice from other trades, because "we mark up 35%" and "we target 26% margin" describe the same job.

How It's Calculated

The calculator uses plain business arithmetic. No building code applies here, and results are pricing estimates, not tax, accounting or legal advice.

  • Direct costs = materials + labor + subcontractors + equipment/permits/other
  • Total cost = direct costs + (direct × contingency %) + (direct × overhead %)
  • Price using markup = total cost × (1 + markup %)
  • Price using target margin = total cost ÷ (1 − margin %)
  • Profit = price − total cost
  • Markup = profit ÷ cost, and Margin = profit ÷ price
  • Conversions: margin = markup ÷ (1 + markup); markup = margin ÷ (1 − margin)

Dollar amounts are rounded to the nearest cent. A margin of 100% or more is impossible, because profit can never equal or exceed the price, so the tool accepts target margins up to 99.9%.

Worked Example

A kitchen remodel has $18,000 in materials, $12,000 in labor, $6,000 in subcontractors and $1,500 in permits and equipment.

  1. Direct costs: 18,000 + 12,000 + 6,000 + 1,500 = $37,500.
  2. Contingency at 5%: 37,500 × 0.05 = $1,875.
  3. Overhead at 10%: 37,500 × 0.10 = $3,750.
  4. Total cost: 37,500 + 1,875 + 3,750 = $43,125.
  5. With a 25% markup, the price is 43,125 × 1.25 = $53,906.25.
  6. Profit: 53,906.25 − 43,125 = $10,781.25, a margin of 10,781.25 ÷ 53,906.25 = 20.0%.

If you wanted a 25% margin on this job instead, the price would be 43,125 ÷ 0.75 = $57,500, which needs a 33.3% markup.

Tips & Common Mistakes

  • Never quote on markup alone without checking margin. Read both numbers before you send a bid.
  • Include overhead in cost. Insurance, vehicles, software, office rent and your own salary are real costs. If they are not in the cost, your "profit" is really paying the bills.
  • Use fully burdened labor. Add payroll taxes, workers' comp and benefits to hourly wages, often 25–40% above the base rate.
  • Keep a contingency. Material price swings and hidden conditions are normal. Many contractors carry 3–10% depending on how well the scope is known.
  • Do not discount blindly. A 10% price cut on a 20% margin job removes about half your profit, not 10% of it.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between markup and margin?

Markup is profit as a percentage of cost, while margin is profit as a percentage of the selling price. Because price is always larger than cost, margin is always the smaller number. A 25% markup equals a 20% margin.

What markup should a contractor use?

It depends on trade, region, competition and overhead. Many residential contractors work with overhead plus a profit markup of roughly 10–25% on top, but there is no universal figure. Start from your own overhead and the net profit you need, then test the price against your local market.

How do I convert 25% markup to margin?

Divide the markup by one plus the markup: 0.25 ÷ 1.25 = 0.20, or 20%. To go the other way, divide the margin by one minus the margin: 0.20 ÷ 0.80 = 0.25. The comparison table in the results shows common conversions.

Should overhead be included before applying markup?

Yes, if you want the markup to represent profit. This calculator adds overhead into total cost first, so the markup or margin you select is what remains after paying for the business. If you treat overhead as part of the markup, use a higher percentage and set the overhead field to zero.

Is a 20% profit margin good for a contractor?

A 20% gross margin on a job is common, but it is not the same as net profit. After overhead you may keep far less, and net profit in construction is often cited in the single digits to low teens. Judge your margin against your own books and local conditions.

Does this calculator include sales tax, permits or financing?

Sales tax is not added, because rules differ by state and by whether materials or labor are taxable. Enter permit fees and equipment rentals in the "Equipment, permits & other" field. Add financing costs and taxes separately, and check local licensing and lien rules.
