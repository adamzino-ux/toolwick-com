Why This Matters

Wood studs at 16 inches on center make up roughly a quarter of a wall's area, and wood insulates at only about R-1.25 per inch. A 2×4 wall with an R-13 batt therefore performs closer to R-11.8 than R-13, and a 2×6 wall with R-20 lands near R-16.6. Continuous insulation (ci) covers the studs, cuts that thermal bridging, and keeps the sheathing warmer so it is less likely to collect condensation in winter.

The people who need this number are builders in Zones 4–8, where current energy codes use combinations like R-20+5 or R-13+10. They also include homeowners re-siding a 1970s house in Minneapolis or Boston who want to add 2 inches of polyiso while the old siding is off, and designers who must prove compliance before a permit. It also matters for budget. A 2,000 sq ft wall area at an extra inch of foam is a real material and labor decision, so it pays to know whether you need 1 inch or 2 before you order. Thicker foam also changes window jamb depth, trim details and screw length, all of which are cheaper to plan on paper.

How It's Calculated

Code target. The 2021 IECC lists prescriptive wall options as cavity R + continuous R. Zones 1–2 need R-13. Zone 3 needs R-20 or R-13+5. Zones 4–8 need R-30, R-20+5, or R-13+10. The calculator finds the smallest ci that works with your cavity R-value. If your cavity is below R-13, it adds the shortfall to the ci requirement as a conservative make-up.

Condensation control. The IRC (R702.7) sets a minimum ci for walls that rely on a Class III interior vapor retarder such as latex paint. For 2×4 / 2×6 walls the minimums are R-2.5 / R-3.75 in Zone 4C, R-5 / R-7.5 in Zone 5, R-7.5 / R-11.25 in Zone 6, R-10 / R-15 in Zone 7 and R-15 / R-20 in Zone 8. Zones 1–4 (not marine) list none. The calculator uses the larger of the code and condensation values unless you enter a custom target.

Thickness = ci target ÷ R per inch (round up to ½ in)
R paths: cavity = R_cav + R_ci + R_other; stud = 1.25 × depth + R_ci + R_other
U = (1 − f) ÷ R_cavity-path + f ÷ R_stud-path; Effective R = 1 ÷ U
Screw = ci + ½ sheathing + ¾ furring + 1½ stud embedment (round up to stock length)

R_other is 2.5, covering air films, drywall, sheathing and siding. The framing fraction f is 0.25 at 16 in. o.c. and 0.22 at 24 in. o.c. Effective R-values are rounded down. Fastener heat loss and window and door penetrations are ignored. Stock screw lengths used are 2, 2.5, 3, 3.5, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 10 and 12 inches.

Worked Example

A new 2×6 wall in Zone 5, 16 in. o.c., with an R-20 batt and foil-faced polyiso (R-5.7 per inch), planned at 1.5 inches.

  • Code target: R-20 cavity qualifies for the R-20+5 option, so ci = R-5.
  • Condensation minimum: Zone 5, 2×6 = R-7.5, which is larger, so the target is R-7.5.
  • Thickness: 7.5 ÷ 5.7 = 1.32 in., rounded up to 1.5 in. That delivers 1.5 × 5.7 = R-8.55.
  • No ci: stud path = 6.875 + 2.5 = 9.375 and cavity path = 22.5. U = 0.75/22.5 + 0.25/9.375 = 0.0600, so effective R is about 16.6.
  • With 1.5 in.: cavity path = 31.05 and stud path = 17.925. U = 0.0381, so effective R is about 26.2, roughly 57% better.
  • Screws: 1.5 + 0.5 + 0.75 + 1.5 = 4.25 in., rounded up to a 5 in. screw.

Tips & Common Mistakes

  • Don't confuse nominal and effective R. R-20+8.55 is R-28.55 on paper, but the whole wall performs closer to R-26 because studs carry heat around the batt.
  • Use long enough screws, and plenty of them. Cladding screws must reach solid framing, not just sheathing. Follow the foam and cladding manufacturers' spacing tables, and have heavy cladding such as stone or brick engineered.
  • Tape seams and integrate the water barrier. Foil-faced foam can act as the water-resistive barrier only if the manufacturer allows it and every seam is taped. Otherwise put a WRB behind or in front of the foam.
  • Plan the openings. Windows, doors and roof edges need deeper jamb extensions, flashing and trim returns. Thick foam changes all of them.
  • Don't assume polyiso's labeled R-value in extreme cold. Its performance drops at low temperatures, so in Zones 6–8 consider XPS, EPS or mineral wool or add a margin.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much continuous insulation do I need on a 2×6 wall?

In IECC Zones 4–8, a 2×6 wall with an R-20 batt needs R-5 of continuous insulation under the prescriptive table. If you use a vapor-retarder-paint interior, the IRC condensation table can require R-7.5 in Zone 5 and more in colder zones. This calculator shows both and uses the larger one.

How long should screws be for siding over exterior foam?

Add the foam thickness, sheathing, furring and at least 1½ inches of embedment into the stud, then round up to the next stock length. For 1.5 in. of foam, that is about 4.25 in., so use a 5 in. screw. Always check the screw manufacturer's tables for your cladding weight.

Does polyiso lose R-value in cold weather?

Yes. Polyiso's rated R-value is measured at a mean temperature of 75°F, and it performs noticeably lower when the foam is very cold. In Zones 6–8 many designers prefer XPS, EPS or mineral wool for exterior walls, or they add thickness to compensate.

Is continuous insulation the same as exterior sheathing?

No. Structural sheathing such as OSB or plywood adds only about R-0.5. Continuous insulation is an unbroken insulating layer over the framing, usually rigid foam or mineral wool board. Some products combine both, but only the insulating layer counts toward the ci requirement.

Can exterior insulation replace cavity insulation?

Usually not under the prescriptive code. Most paths require both a cavity R-value and a ci R-value. Some performance or U-factor paths allow other combinations, so confirm with your building department or energy rater.

Will exterior insulation cause moisture problems?

Done correctly, it reduces risk because it keeps the sheathing warmer than the dew point. Problems arise when the foam is too thin for the climate, seams are left open, or flashing is not integrated. That is why the condensation-control minimums are included here.
