How to Use This Birdsmouth Cut Calculator

Enter the roof pitch as the rise per 12 inches of run (type 6 for a 6/12 roof), pick your rafter size, and enter the width of the wall's top plate: 3.5 inches for a 2×4 wall, 5.5 inches for a 2×6 wall. Leave the seat length blank to use the full plate width, or type a shorter seat if you already plan to reduce it. Add the overhang if you want the tail length too.

The results show the heel plumb cut height (the number you mark on the rafter), the seat length, both cut angles, the rafter depth left at the notch, and a pass/reduce/fail check. A pitch table below shows how the same rafter and seat behave from 2/12 to 12/12.

Why This Matters

A birdsmouth is the notch that lets a rafter sit flat on the wall's top plate. Cut it too shallow and the rafter rides high, leaving a gap and a wavy roof line. Cut it too deep and you remove the wood that carries the load. A rafter notched through more than a quarter of its depth can split at the heel under snow, wind uplift or a heavy roofing load.

Framers, shed and garage builders, and DIYers replacing a single rafter all need the same two numbers right: the level seat length and the plumb cut height. Take a 12×16 shed with 2×6 rafters at 8/12 on a 2×4 wall. A full 3.5-inch seat needs a 2.33-inch heel cut, but a 2×6 is only 5.5 inches deep, so the quarter-depth limit is 1.375 inches. That roof needs a seat of about 2 1/16 inches or a deeper rafter. Finding that on paper costs nothing. Finding it after cutting 24 rafters costs a weekend and a pile of lumber. Steeper roofs are hit hardest, because the heel cut grows with the pitch for the same seat length.

How It's Calculated

Everything follows from the right triangle formed by the seat (level), the heel cut (plumb) and the rafter's bottom edge:

Angle θ = arctan(rise / 12)
Heel plumb cut h = seat × (rise / 12)
Max heel cut = actual rafter depth ÷ 4
Max seat = (actual depth ÷ 4) ÷ (rise / 12)
Notch edge along rafter = seat ÷ cos θ
Notch depth, perpendicular = seat × sin θ
Tail length along top edge = overhang ÷ cos θ

The depth check follows the rule in the International Residential Code (2018 and 2021, R802.7.1) that notches at the end of a sawn-lumber member must not exceed one-quarter of its depth, and the 1½-inch minimum bearing on wood in R802.6. The tool compares the vertical heel cut to a quarter of the actual depth (7.25″ for a 2×8, not 8″). That is slightly conservative, because the notch is a little shallower when measured square to the rafter. When a seat is too long, it rounds the reduced seat down to the nearest 1/16″, and cut dimensions in fractions are also rounded down.

Local codes vary, and some jurisdictions or engineered roof designs set different limits. This tool is a layout and checking aid. It is not engineering, a permit approval or an inspection sign-off, and it does not apply to trusses, I-joists or engineered rafters, which must never be notched.

Worked Example

A 6/12 roof, 2×8 rafters (7.25″ actual), a 2×4 wall (3.5″ plate), blank seat and a 12″ overhang:

  1. Angle: arctan(6 ÷ 12) = 26.57°. The plumb cut sits 63.43° from the rafter edge, and the seat cut sits 26.57° from it.
  2. Seat: blank, so the full plate width of 3.5″.
  3. Heel plumb cut: 3.5 × 6 ÷ 12 = 1.75″ (1 3/4″).
  4. Limit: 7.25 ÷ 4 = 1.8125″. Since 1.75 is no more than 1.8125, the cut passes.
  5. Notch edge: 3.5 ÷ cos 26.57° = 3.913″. Perpendicular notch depth: 3.5 × sin 26.57° = 1.565″. Rafter left at the notch: 7.25 − 1.565 = 5.685″.
  6. Tail: 12 ÷ cos 26.57° = 13.416″, about 13 7/16″ along the top edge.

Change the rafter to a 2×6 and the heel cut of 1.75″ exceeds the 1.375″ limit. The tool reduces the seat to 2 3/4″ (1.375 ÷ 0.5), giving a 1 3/8″ heel cut. It also tells you that a 2×8 would allow the full seat.

Tips & Common Mistakes

  • Use actual lumber sizes. A 2×8 is 7.25″ deep, not 8″. Using the nominal size hides a notch that is too deep.
  • Measure the plumb cut along the plumb line. Mark it with a speed square, not from the rafter's bottom edge square across.
  • Don't overcut the corner. A circular saw blade runs past the inside corner on the underside. Stop short and finish with a handsaw, because the overcut is a crack starter right where the rafter is loaded.
  • Account for sheathing and wall thickness. Locate the plumb cut at the face of the framing plate that supports the rafter. If your plans call for exterior foam or sheathing, follow the plans for where the heel lands.
  • Cut one test rafter first. Fit it on the wall, adjust, then use it as the pattern. Crown the rafters up and confirm that the plate tops are level.
  • Add the connectors your code requires. A well-cut birdsmouth bears on the plate, but rafter ties, hurricane ties and the fastener schedule keep the roof attached.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a birdsmouth cut?

It is a V-shaped notch cut into the underside of a rafter. It has a level seat that rests on the wall's top plate and a plumb heel cut that sits against the outside face of the plate. The notch lets the rafter bear flat on the wall while the rest of it slopes up to the ridge.

How deep can a birdsmouth cut be?

The IRC limits a notch at the end of a sawn rafter to one-quarter of its depth. Some older rules of thumb allow one-third, and local codes vary. This calculator uses the stricter one-quarter limit and never rounds a safety result toward the less safe side.

How do I calculate the seat length of a birdsmouth?

Start with the full width of the top plate, which is 3.5″ for a 2×4 wall. Multiply by rise divided by 12 to get the heel cut height. If that height is over one-quarter of the rafter depth, shorten the seat until it fits, but keep at least 1½″ of bearing.

What if my rafter is too shallow for the birdsmouth?

Use a deeper rafter, such as moving from a 2×6 to a 2×8. Otherwise ask the designer about a different framing detail, such as a bearing plate or metal connectors. Don't just cut deeper, and don't notch past the limit hoping the roof will be fine.

Does the overhang change the birdsmouth cut?

No. The seat and heel cut depend only on pitch, rafter depth and plate width. The overhang only moves where the tail's plumb cut lands, which is the horizontal overhang divided by the cosine of the roof angle, measured along the top edge.

Can I use this for metric dimensions?

The tool works in inches. Divide millimetres by 25.4 to convert, and use the actual depth of your lumber. The pitch input stays as rise per 12 units of run.
