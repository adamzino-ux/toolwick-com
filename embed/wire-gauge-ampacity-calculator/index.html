Why This Matters

Undersized wire is a leading cause of electrical fires. When a conductor carries more current than it's rated for, resistive heating builds up, insulation degrades, and connections loosen over time — long before a standard breaker would ever trip. The NEC's ampacity tables (310.16 for conduit, 310.17 for free air) exist to prevent exactly that.

There's a second layer most DIYers miss: NEC 240.4(D) caps the maximum overcurrent protection for 14, 12, and 10 AWG copper conductors at 15A, 20A, and 30A respectively — regardless of what the insulation's temperature-rated table ampacity would otherwise allow. So even though 14 AWG at 90°C is rated for 25A on paper, code still limits it to a 15A breaker in ordinary branch-circuit wiring.

This interacts with continuous loads too. A load that runs 3+ hours (a water heater, a workshop tool, permanently wired equipment) must be sized at 125% of its rating per NEC 210.19(A)(1). A 20A continuous load needs 25A of protection — which rules out both 14 AWG (capped at 15A) and 12 AWG (capped at 20A), pushing the requirement to 10 AWG. This calculator applies both rules automatically so the recommendation is never a false-safe undersized gauge.

Voltage drop is the quieter hazard: run a branch circuit too far on too-thin wire and lights dim, motors run hot, and electronics misbehave, even though the breaker never trips. Electricians, DIYers, solar installers, RV builders, and marine electricians all rely on getting ampacity and voltage drop right together.

How It's Calculated

Ampacity is looked up from standard NEC 310.16 (conduit) or 310.17 (free air) tables based on wire gauge, material, and insulation temperature rating. The calculator then applies the NEC 240.4(D) small-conductor breaker cap (14 AWG → 15A, 12 AWG → 20A, 10 AWG → 30A for copper; 12 AWG → 15A, 10 AWG → 25A for aluminum) and picks the smallest gauge whose effective rating covers your required overcurrent protection.

Voltage drop uses the standard K-factor formula common in electrical trade calculations:

Single-phase: VD = (2 × K × I × L) / CM
Three-phase: VD = (1.732 × K × I × L) / CM

Where K is the resistivity constant (12.9 ohm·cmil/ft for copper, 21.2 for aluminum), I is current in amps, L is one-way length in feet, and CM is the wire's circular-mil area. Voltage-drop percentage is (VD ÷ source voltage) × 100. NEC recommends keeping branch-circuit drop at or below 3%, and 5% total including the feeder.

Worked Example

Say you're wiring a continuous 20A, 120V single-phase load (a permanently wired heater) 60 feet from the panel, using 75°C copper conductors in conduit.

  1. Required protection: 20A × 1.25 (continuous factor) = 25A minimum.
  2. Check 14 AWG: NEC 240.4(D) caps it at 15A → not enough.
  3. Check 12 AWG: capped at 20A → still not enough for 25A.
  4. Check 10 AWG: capped at 30A, table ampacity at 75°C is 35A → satisfies the 25A requirement. Recommended: 10 AWG.
  5. Voltage drop check using the actual 20A load: VD = (2 × 12.9 × 20 × 60) ÷ 10,380 = 2.98V, or 2.49% of 120V — comfortably under the 3% NEC guideline, so no further upsizing is needed.

Tips & Common Mistakes

  • Never round down. If your calculated need lands right at a wire's rating, move up one size for margin — especially on continuous loads.
  • Don't ignore the small-conductor rule. A 20A breaker on 14 AWG wire is a common and dangerous mistake, even though 14 AWG "looks" rated high enough at 90°C.
  • One-way length only. The voltage-drop formula already accounts for the round trip — don't double your measured run length.
  • Conduit fill matters. More than three current-carrying conductors bundled together requires further derating beyond the base table values shown here.
  • Aluminum needs care. It requires larger gauge, anti-oxidant compound, and AL-rated connectors — never mix aluminum and copper at a connection without listed hardware.

Frequently Asked Questions

What AWG wire for a 20-amp circuit?

For a non-continuous 20A load, 12 AWG copper is standard and satisfies NEC 240.4(D), which caps 12 AWG at a 20A breaker. If the load is continuous (running 3+ hours), NEC requires sizing for 125% of the load, which typically pushes the requirement to 10 AWG instead.

What's the difference between AWG and wire diameter?

AWG is an inverse scale — a lower number means a thicker wire. Cross-sectional area (and ampacity) roughly doubles every 3 gauge steps, while the wire's diameter roughly doubles every 6 gauge steps. AWG 0 is followed by 1/0, 2/0, 3/0, and 4/0 for larger conductors.

How much voltage drop is acceptable?

NEC recommends a maximum of 3% voltage drop on branch circuits and 5% total including the feeder. For sensitive electronics or long motor runs, aim for under 2%. Excessive drop causes equipment to run hot, reduces efficiency, and can shorten appliance lifespan.

Can I use aluminum wire instead of copper?

Yes — select "Aluminum" in this calculator to get NEC conduit-ampacity and voltage-drop results using aluminum's higher resistivity (K=21.2 vs. copper's 12.9). Aluminum generally needs about two AWG sizes larger than copper for the same ampacity, and NEC doesn't recognize aluminum building conductors smaller than 12 AWG. Free-air aluminum ampacity isn't included here — check NEC Table 310.17 directly for that case.

What is the NEC 240.4(D) small-conductor rule?

It caps the maximum overcurrent protection for 14, 12, and 10 AWG copper conductors at 15A, 20A, and 30A respectively, regardless of what the insulation's temperature-rated ampacity table would otherwise allow (with similar, lower caps for aluminum). This calculator applies these caps automatically so it never recommends a breaker too large for the wire.

Why does this tool sometimes recommend a bigger wire than the raw ampacity table suggests?

Three factors can push the recommendation up: the 125% continuous-load factor, the NEC 240.4(D) small-conductor breaker cap, and a voltage-drop check that upsizes further if the initial pick exceeds 3% drop. All three are common reasons a "20A-rated" wire on paper still isn't the right choice for a specific circuit.
