For several surfaces of different sizes, switch to Multiple areas and fill in up to six rows. The output is the board feet to order, the number of kits, the lowest R-value and the material cost.

Why This Matters

Spray foam is sold by the board foot (one square foot, one inch thick), and it costs enough that ordering errors hurt. Say you are insulating 2,000 sq ft of wall and roof surface with 3.5 in of closed-cell foam. That is 2,000 × 3.5 = 7,000 board feet. At $1.25 per board foot the material is about $8,750, so a 10% miscount (700 BF) means roughly $875 wasted or missing mid-job. Kits make it concrete: a 600 BF kit covers only about 171 sq ft at 3.5 in. That job needs 12 kits before any waste allowance and 13 with 10% waste.

Homeowners use the numbers to choose between DIY kits and a contractor quote. Contractors use them to check bids, and energy auditors use them to show what each extra half-inch buys. Thickness also matters for code targets. Using this tool's nominal values, 3 in of closed-cell foam is R-19.5, which misses an R-20 target, while 3.5 in gives R-22.75. A half inch can decide whether a wall meets the number on your plans.

How It's Calculated

The tool follows the board-foot convention used by foam suppliers and nominal manufacturer R-values. It does not apply any single code table, because energy-code minimums (IRC Chapter 11 and the IECC it references) change by climate zone and code edition, and local amendments vary.

  • Board feet: Length × Width × Thickness (in) × Number of areas
  • R-value: R per inch × Thickness, using about R-3.7 per inch for open-cell and R-6.5 per inch for closed-cell. Real products range from roughly 3.5–3.8 and 6.0–7.0, so check your data sheet.
  • Board feet to order: Net board feet × (1 + waste %), rounded up to a whole board foot.
  • Kits: Board feet to order ÷ board feet per kit, rounded up to whole kits.
  • Thickness for a target: Target R ÷ R per inch, rounded up to the next half inch.
  • Cost: Board feet × cost per board foot, shown net and with waste.

With several areas, the headline R-value is the lowest of them, so one thin area cannot hide behind a thick one.

Worked Example

You are filling two identical garage walls, each 20 ft long and 8 ft tall, with closed-cell foam. The target is R-20, foam costs $1.25 per board foot, your kits cover 600 BF each, and you allow 10% waste.

  1. Surface area: 20 × 8 = 160 sq ft per wall, so 320 sq ft for two walls.
  2. Try 3 in: 6.5 × 3 = R-19.5, which is short of R-20. The tool flags this and calculates 20 ÷ 6.5 = 3.08, rounded up to 3.5 in.
  3. At 3.5 in: R = 6.5 × 3.5 = R-22.75.
  4. Net board feet: 320 × 3.5 = 1,120 BF.
  5. With waste: 1,120 × 1.10 = 1,232 BF to order.
  6. Kits: 1,232 ÷ 600 = 2.05, rounded up to 3 kits (1,800 BF of capacity, 568 BF spare).
  7. Cost: 1,232 × $1.25 = $1,540.00 including waste, or $1,400.00 for the net 1,120 BF.

Tips & Common Mistakes

  • Know what you are covering. Foam fills the cavity between framing unless you also cover the studs. A 2×4 wall cavity is 3.5 in deep, so measure the cavity face or the full surface depending on your plan, and subtract windows and doors.
  • Spray in passes. Closed-cell foam is usually applied in lifts of about 1–2 in, with time between passes. Follow the maximum lift on the data sheet, since overly thick passes can overheat or shrink.
  • Cover the foam where the code requires it. Foam plastics generally need a thermal barrier such as ½ in gypsum board in occupied spaces, or an ignition barrier in attics and crawl spaces (IRC R316). Ventilate, and wear the PPE the manufacturer specifies.
  • Keep the waste allowance. Overspray, uneven substrates and cold weather all cut yield. Use 10–15% and buy whole kits.
  • Check temperature. Most products specify a substrate and chemical temperature range. Cold surfaces cut expansion and adhesion, so read the can before you start.

Frequently Asked Questions

What's the difference between open-cell and closed-cell spray foam?
Open-cell foam is soft, light (about 0.5 lb per cubic foot), vapor-permeable and delivers roughly R-3.7 per inch. Closed-cell foam is rigid, denser (about 2 lb per cubic foot) and delivers roughly R-6.5 per inch. It costs more per board foot but gives more R-value in a thin layer.
Do I have to use closed-cell foam in damp areas?
Not automatically. Closed-cell foam is commonly chosen for rim joists, crawl spaces, below-grade walls and other damp locations because it resists moisture better. Whether it is required depends on your local code, the climate and the product's evaluation report, so ask your building department.
How many board feet does a DIY spray foam kit cover?
Kits are rated in board feet at 1 inch thick, and ratings range from a couple of hundred up to about 600 BF. A 600 BF kit sprayed 2 in thick covers about 300 sq ft. Enter your kit's rated yield, and expect real-world yield to come in a little lower.
What R-value do I need for my walls or attic?
Energy codes set minimum R-values by climate zone and code edition. In recent IECC/IRC editions, ceiling minimums run from about R-30 in warm zones to R-60 in cold ones. Wall minimums run from about R-13 up to R-30, or a cavity-plus-continuous-insulation combination. Check which edition your jurisdiction has adopted, and enter that number as the target.
How do I convert a contractor's price per square foot to a price per board foot?
Installed spray foam is often quoted per square foot at a stated thickness. Divide that price by the thickness in inches. For example, $4.00 per sq ft at 2 in is $2.00 per board foot. Installed prices also include labor and equipment, so they will be higher than kit prices.
Is a DIY kit practical for a whole house?
Usually not. Kits suit rim joists, small cavities and air-sealing gaps, and covering a whole house means dozens of kits plus careful temperature and lift control. Compare the kit count here against a professional quote before deciding.
