Why This Matters

A bare dirt floor keeps pushing ground moisture up into the framing. Wood that stays above roughly 20 percent moisture content for long periods can grow mold and start to decay, and damp insulation stops doing its job. A continuous ground cover is the cheapest part of fixing this, so it pays to buy the right amount.

Ordering is where people get caught. A 40 × 30 ft crawl space has 1,200 sq ft of floor, but the real figure is closer to 1,450 sq ft once you add the wall turn-ups, seam overlaps and waste. Buy a single 1,000 sq ft roll and you will be short in the middle of the job, with a seam in the worst possible place. Buy three rolls where two would do and you have spent an extra few hundred dollars on plastic that goes in the trash.

Roll width matters too. Many people buy 10 or 12 ft film because it is easier to drag through a tight space, but narrower rolls mean more seams, more tape and more overlap. This calculator lays out the strips for you in both directions, picks the layout that uses the least film, and checks the roll count both by area and by layout.

How It's Calculated

For unvented crawl spaces, IRC R408.3 calls for a Class I vapor retarder (0.1 perm or less) over the ground, with joints overlapped at least 6 inches and sealed or taped. The edges must extend at least 6 inches up the stem wall and be attached and sealed to the wall or its insulation. Polyethylene of 6 mil or more usually meets the perm rating, but check the product data sheet. This tool enforces the 6 inch minimums for both overlap and wall turn-up, and it does not accept lower values.

Floor = L × W
Wall turn-up = 2 × (L + W) × (turn-up in ÷ 12)
Seam overlap = (strips − 1) × (overlap in ÷ 12) × strip length
Total = (Floor + Wall + Seam) × (1 + waste %), rounded up

Strips are laid in whichever direction uses the least roll length, using the span plus both wall turn-ups. The first strip covers one roll width and every extra strip adds the roll width minus the overlap. If a strip is longer than one roll, end splices are added. Rolls to buy is the larger of the area-based count and the layout-based count, always rounded up, and the sheeting cost is charged on whole rolls because that is what you pay for.

Tape is the floor seam length, any end splices, and the full perimeter 2 × (L + W) for sealing the barrier to the wall, plus 10 percent. It is rounded up to a whole foot and multiplied by your tape price. The tool does not include fasteners, wall sealant, pier wraps or pipe boots.

Worked Example

A 40 ft × 30 ft crawl space, 6 in turn-up, 12 in overlap, 12 mil film on 16 ft × 100 ft rolls, 10% waste, $0.15 per sq ft sheeting and $0.35 per ft tape.

  1. Floor: 40 × 30 = 1,200 sq ft.
  2. Walls: 2 × (40 + 30) × 0.5 ft = 70 sq ft.
  3. Layout: strips run along the 40 ft side. Across the width is 30 + 1 = 31 ft and each strip is 40 + 1 = 41 ft long. The first strip covers 16 ft and each extra strip adds 15 ft, so 2 strips cover the 31 ft with one seam.
  4. Seam overlap: 1 seam × 1 ft × 41 ft = 41 sq ft.
  5. Subtotal: 1,200 + 70 + 41 = 1,311 sq ft. With 10% waste (131.1 sq ft) the total is 1,442.1, rounded up to 1,443 sq ft.
  6. Rolls: one roll holds 1,600 sq ft, so the area check gives 1. The layout needs 2 strips × 41 ft = 82 ft, or 90.2 ft with waste, and fits in one 100 ft roll. Buy 1 roll.
  7. Cost: 1,600 sq ft × $0.15 = $240.00. Tape: 41 ft seam + 140 ft perimeter = 181 ft, plus 10% = 200 ft × $0.35 = $70.00. Total $310.00, about $0.26 per floor sq ft.

Same space with 10 ft × 100 ft rolls. Running strips along the 30 ft width (5 strips of 31 ft) uses 155 ft of film, less than running along the length (4 strips of 41 ft, 164 ft), so the calculator picks that layout. Seams: 4 × 1 ft × 31 ft = 124 sq ft. Subtotal 1,200 + 70 + 124 = 1,394 sq ft, plus 10% = 1,533.4, rounded up to 1,534 sq ft. Each 100 ft roll holds three 31 ft strips, so 5 strips need 2 rolls (2,000 sq ft). Sheeting: 2,000 × $0.15 = $300.00. Tape: (124 + 140) × 1.1 = 291 ft × $0.35 = $101.85. Total $401.85, about $0.33 per floor sq ft. Narrower rolls cost more here because of the extra seams and tape.

A crawl space that is not a perfect rectangle needs a higher waste factor.

Tips & Common Mistakes

  • Fix water first. Standing water, a leaking pipe or poor exterior drainage must be dealt with before any plastic goes down. A barrier does not replace drainage or a sump.
  • Measure inside the walls and note obstructions. Piers, columns and plumbing each need cutting and sealing. Raise the waste factor to 15–20% for a cluttered space.
  • Do not skimp on thickness. You will be crawling over the film. 6 mil tears easily, while 12 mil or more tolerates traffic and long-term use.
  • Seal, do not just overlap. Joints should be sealed or taped, and the edges attached and sealed to the wall. Loose edges let moisture sneak behind the sheet.
  • Clear sharp debris. Rocks, wire and old nails puncture film. Rake the soil smooth before unrolling.
  • Check local rules. Vented and conditioned crawl spaces, radon-control systems and local amendments can change what is required.

Frequently Asked Questions

What thickness vapor barrier should I use in a crawl space?

Many suppliers sell 6 mil as the minimum, but 10–12 mil resists tears much better when you work on it. 20 mil reinforced film is common for full encapsulation. The code looks at the product's perm rating (Class I), so check the data sheet rather than going by mil alone.

Does the vapor barrier have to go up the walls?

For unvented crawl spaces, IRC R408.3 requires the ground cover to extend at least 6 inches up the stem wall, attached and sealed to the wall or insulation. Many encapsulation projects run the film higher, sometimes the full wall height. Rules for vented crawl spaces differ, so ask your building department.

How much should the seams overlap?

The R408.3 minimum is 6 inches, with joints sealed or taped. Many installers use 12 inches because it gives a wider tape-able margin. This calculator accepts 6 inches or more and will not go below the code minimum. Overlap alone is not a seal.

How much waste factor should I add?

10% is reasonable for a simple rectangle. Add 15–20% or more if you have piers, ducts, steps, or curved or stepped walls, because every cut-out and trim leaves offcuts that cannot be reused.

How is the tape cost estimated?

Tape length is the floor seam length plus end splices plus the full wall perimeter, increased by 10%. That length is multiplied by the per-foot price you enter. Double-sided sealant, fasteners and pier or pipe sealing are not included.

Can I install a crawl space vapor barrier myself?

Many homeowners do, since it needs basic tools and a lot of crawling. Check first for standing water, active mold, pests, exposed wiring or structural damage, and get a professional involved if you find any. Wear a respirator, gloves and knee pads, and tell someone when you are under the house.
