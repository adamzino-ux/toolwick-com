How to Use This Floor Joist Span & Size Calculator

Enter the clear span — the horizontal distance between the supports your joists rest on, such as a foundation wall, beam or bearing wall. Set the joist spacing with the slider (12, 16, 19.2 or 24 inches on center), then choose the live load, the dead load of the floor finish, your lumber species and grade, and the deflection limit. Leave "Joist to check" on its default and the calculator finds the smallest standard 2× joist (2×6 through 2×12) that passes. Pick a size instead to see its maximum span and whether your span passes. Results include a pass/fail table for every size, utilization bars for bending, shear and deflection, and a maximum-span table for all four spacings.

Why This Matters

Undersized joists rarely fail dramatically. They sag, bounce and squeak, and years later they crack tile, drywall and finish flooring. A typical living-room floor carries 40 psf of live load plus about 10 psf of dead load. At 16 inches on center, a Douglas Fir-Larch No. 2 2×8 tops out at about 12 ft 9 in., while a 2×10 reaches about 15 ft 7 in. That nearly three-foot gap is the difference between a floor that feels solid and one that feels like a trampoline.

Homeowners use this when planning an addition, finishing a basement ceiling or converting a garage. Contractors use it to sanity-check a lumber order, since stepping from 2×8 to 2×10 on a 20-joist floor adds a few hundred dollars. Buyers use it when an inspector flags 2×6 joists spanning 11 feet in an older house. A structural engineer's floor-framing review often costs several hundred dollars, so it helps to arrive with numbers.

Prescriptive span tables are in IRC Table R502.3.1(1) (sleeping rooms and attic floors, 30 psf live load) and R502.3.1(2) (other floors, 40 psf live load). Both use an L/360 live-load deflection limit from IRC Table R301.7. This tool calculates from the underlying NDS formulas so you can vary loads, species and spacing.

How It's Calculated

The joist is treated as simply supported with a uniform load. For each size the tool solves for the longest span that satisfies four checks and reports the smallest result (L = span in inches, w = load per inch of joist, b × d = actual dimensions).

Section: S = b·d²/6, I = b·d³/12 (a 2×10 is 1.5″ × 9.25″)
Load: w = psf × spacing (ft) ÷ 12
Bending: M = wL²/8 ≤ Fb′·S, so L = √(8·Fb′·S / wtotal)
Shear: V = wL/2 ≤ ⅔·Fv′·b·d, so L = 4·Fv′·b·d / (3·wtotal)
Live deflection: Δ = 5wL⁴/(384·E′·I) ≤ L/360 (or your limit)
Total deflection: same formula with live + dead load, limit L/240

Adjusted bending strength is Fb′ = Fb × CF × Cr × CM. CF is the NDS size factor for sawn lumber (1.3, 1.2, 1.1 and 1.0 for 2×6, 2×8, 2×10 and 2×12). Southern Pine's size-specific table values already include it. Cr = 1.15 is the repetitive-member factor for three or more joists at 24 inches or less with a sheathed subfloor. Load duration and lateral-support factors are 1.0. Selecting wet service applies CM = 0.85 to Fb, 0.97 to Fv and 0.90 to E. Shear gets no reduction for load near the support.

Conservative rounding: the span you enter is rounded up to the next inch, maximum spans are rounded down to the next inch, and utilization percentages are rounded up. A joist only passes if every check passes.

Worked Example

A 14 ft span, 16″ o.c., 40 psf live, 10 psf dead, Douglas Fir-Larch No. 2 (Fb 900 psi, E 1,600,000 psi, Fv 180 psi), L/360, dry.

  1. Loads: total 50 psf × 16/12 ft = 66.7 plf (5.56 lb/in). Live 40 psf gives 53.3 plf (4.44 lb/in).
  2. 2×8 (1.5″ × 7.25″): S = 13.14 in³, I = 47.63 in⁴. Fb′ = 900 × 1.2 × 1.15 = 1,242 psi, so the bending limit is √(8 × 1,242 × 13.14 / 5.56) ≈ 153.3 in. The live-deflection limit is about 154.1 in. The maximum span is 153 in = 12′-9″, which is under the 14′-0″ you need. At 168 in the 2×8 is at roughly 120% bending and 130% deflection, so it fails.
  3. 2×10 (1.5″ × 9.25″): S = 21.39 in³, I = 98.93 in⁴. Fb′ = 900 × 1.1 × 1.15 = 1,138.5 psi, so bending allows about 187.3 in. Live deflection allows about 196.6 in, shear about 599 in and total-load deflection about 208.9 in. Bending governs, giving a maximum of 187 in = 15′-7″. At 14 ft it runs at roughly 80% bending and 62% deflection, so it passes.
  4. Result: the tool recommends a 2×10 at 16″ o.c.

Tips & Common Mistakes

  • Measure the clear span, not the room. Use the distance between supports. A beam or bearing wall mid-room can cut the span in half and drop the joist size by two steps.
  • Count the floor finish. Tile on cement backer adds about 15 psf and a mortar bed 20–30 psf. Choosing the 10 psf default under a tile floor understates bending stress.
  • L/360 is not "no bounce." Deflection limits do not guarantee vibration comfort. For a stiffer feel, choose a tighter spacing, a deeper joist or L/480. The Tile Council of North America recommends L/360 for ceramic and porcelain tile and L/720 for natural stone.
  • Protect the joist depth. IRC R502.8 limits notches to one-sixth of the depth (one-fourth at the ends) and bored holes to one-third, at least 2 inches from the edges. Plan plumbing and wiring runs before you size.
  • Know what is not covered. Cantilevers, walls or posts bearing on the floor, hot tubs, point loads, notched ends and engineered lumber (I-joists, LVL) need manufacturer tables or an engineer.

Frequently Asked Questions

What size floor joist do I need for a 16-foot span?

With Douglas Fir-Larch No. 2, 40 psf live, 10 psf dead and L/360, a 2×12 at 16″ o.c. reaches about 18′-1″ and at 19.2″ o.c. about 16′-6″. A 2×10 at 12″ o.c. reaches about 18′-0″, but only about 15′-7″ at 16″ o.c. Change the species, grade or loads above for your case.

What's the difference between L/360, L/480 and L/720?

They are the maximum allowed sag as a fraction of the span. On a 16-ft (192 in) span, L/360 allows about 0.53 in, L/480 about 0.40 in and L/720 about 0.27 in. Code requires L/360 for floors under live load. The stricter limits are for brittle finishes such as stone.

Can I use I-joists or LVL instead of 2× lumber?

Yes, and they are often the better choice above about 16–18 feet because they span farther per inch of depth. This calculator covers only sawn 2× lumber, so use the manufacturer's span tables or an engineer for engineered products.

How many joists do I need for a room?

Divide the room width in inches by the spacing, round up, and add one. A 12-ft-wide room at 16″ o.c. needs (144 ÷ 16) + 1 = 10 joists. The Joist Spacing & Layout Calculator handles layout details and rim joists.

Why do my results differ slightly from the IRC span tables?

The IRC tables are prescriptive and assume specific design values. This tool calculates from NDS formulas, rounds spans down to the inch, and also checks shear and total-load deflection. If the two ever disagree, build to the shorter span and ask your building department which governs.
