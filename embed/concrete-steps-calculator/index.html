Why This Matters

Poured concrete steps are a job where the math has to be right twice: once for the geometry and once for the concrete. Get the geometry wrong and you build a trip hazard that fails inspection, or one that someone falls on. Get the volume wrong and you either pay for a second truck or watch a crew stand around with a half-finished pour while the first load stiffens.

Typical cases: a homeowner replacing a three-step front stoop (usually 1 to 2 cubic yards), a landscaper building a four-step approach to a deck, or a contractor pricing a basement walkout. A 28-inch rise at a 7¾-inch limit needs four risers, not three, and because a solid pour fills everything below each tread, one extra step adds noticeably more concrete. Many ready-mix plants also add a short-load fee on small orders, so knowing whether you need 1.25 or 1.75 yards helps you decide between a truck and bagged mix. Ordering a little over is cheap. Ordering short on a monolithic pour can mean a cold joint in the middle of your stairs.

How It's Calculated

The calculator treats the steps as a solid, monolithic pour: each tread is concrete from grade up to that tread's height. With n risers, there are n − 1 treads plus the top landing.

n = ⌈ rise ÷ max riser ⌉ (always rounded up) R = rise ÷ n (actual riser height) Treads = W × T × R × (n−1) × n ÷ 2 Landing = W × L × n × R Base = W × ((n−1) × T + L) × B Volume = Treads + Landing + Base Order = Volume × (1 + waste%), rounded up

W is width, T is tread depth, L is landing depth and B is base slab thickness. The sum 1 + 2 + … + (n−1) = n(n−1)/2 gives the stacked tread heights. Volumes are computed in cubic inches, then converted: 1 yd³ = 46,656 in³ = 27 ft³, and 1 m³ = 61,023.7 in³. The order quantity rounds up to the next ¼ yd³ (or 0.1 m³). Bag counts use typical yields of 0.60 ft³ for an 80 lb bag and 0.45 ft³ for a 60 lb bag, rounded up.

Limits follow the 2021 IRC R311.7 for residential (7¾-inch maximum riser, 10-inch minimum tread, 36-inch minimum width and landing) or the 2021 IBC §1011 for commercial (7-inch maximum riser, 4-inch minimum, 11-inch minimum tread, 44-inch width used as a conservative figure). Adopted editions and amendments vary by jurisdiction, so confirm with your local building department. A flight is limited here to 12 ft of rise, which is the conservative limit. The 2·riser + tread comfort check (24 to 25 inches) is a rule of thumb, not a code requirement.

Worked Example

Suppose a front stoop has a 28-inch rise, a 48-inch width, 11-inch treads, a 36-inch landing, the IRC basis and 10% waste.

  • Steps: 28 ÷ 7.75 = 3.61, rounded up to n = 4 risers. Riser R = 28 ÷ 4 = 7.00 in, with 3 treads.
  • Treads: 48 × 11 × 7 × (3 × 4 ÷ 2) = 48 × 11 × 7 × 6 = 22,176 in³.
  • Landing: 48 × 36 × 4 × 7 = 48,384 in³.
  • Net volume: 70,560 in³ = 40.83 ft³ = 1.512 yd³.
  • With 10% waste: 1.512 × 1.10 = 1.664 yd³ (44.9 ft³), rounded up to 1.75 yd³ to order.
  • Bags: 44.9 ÷ 0.60 = 74.9, so 75 bags of 80 lb, or 100 bags of 60 lb.

Check: 2 × 7 + 11 = 25 inches, which falls in the comfortable range. The tool's defaults reproduce this example.

Tips & Common Mistakes

  • Measure the rise last and from the real surfaces. Measure from compacted finished grade to the finished top of the landing, not to the form board or a slab edge you plan to cut.
  • Don't round the step count down. If the answer is 3.6 steps, you need 4. Riser height must never exceed the code maximum, and all risers in a flight must be nearly equal (IRC allows a ⅜-inch variation).
  • Remember the landing and handrails. Exterior doors need a landing at least 36 inches deep, and four or more risers generally require a handrail. Open sides more than 30 inches above grade need guards.
  • Don't ignore the footing. Steps attached to a house often need frost-depth footings or a thickened edge. The base slab input adds concrete but doesn't design the footing, so ask your building department.
  • Order more than the net number. Uneven subgrade, form bulge and spillage commonly eat 5 to 10%. Use 10 to 15% on rough or excavated ground.

Frequently Asked Questions

How many cubic yards of concrete do I need for 3 steps?

It depends on width, tread depth and landing. For a 21-inch rise (three 7-inch risers), 48 inches wide, 11-inch treads and a 36-inch landing, the net volume is about 1.02 yd³. With 10% waste you would order 1.25 yd³. Change the inputs above to match your steps.

What is the maximum riser height for concrete steps?

The 2021 IRC sets a 7¾-inch maximum riser for residential stairs, and the 2021 IBC sets 7 inches for commercial. Some jurisdictions use older or stricter rules, so check locally. A riser of 6½ to 7 inches usually feels more comfortable outdoors.

How deep should each concrete step tread be?

Residential code requires at least 10 inches and commercial code at least 11 inches. Many builders use 11 to 12 inches for exterior steps so people can place their whole foot. Deeper treads also add concrete and extend the footprint.

How many bags of concrete do I need for steps?

One cubic yard is 27 ft³, or about 45 bags of 80 lb mix at 0.60 ft³ each. A 1.5 yd³ stoop is around 68 bags before waste. Above roughly 1 yd³, ready-mix is usually easier and gives a more uniform pour.

Do concrete steps need a footing or rebar?

Often yes, especially when attached to a heated structure or built in a freezing climate. Frost depth ranges from nearly zero to several feet depending on region. Reinforcement and footing details are set by your local code and a designer or inspector, not by this calculator.

Can I hollow out the steps to use less concrete?

Yes, steps can be formed as a reinforced slab over compacted fill, and that uses less concrete. This calculator assumes a solid pour, which gives a safe upper estimate. Hollow designs need proper reinforcement and base preparation, so consult a professional.
