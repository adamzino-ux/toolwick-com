Why This Matters

The R-value printed on an insulation package is not the R-value of your wall. A 2×6 wall filled with R-21 batts is often sold as an "R-21 wall," but the studs, plates, headers and corners are wood at about R-1.25 per inch, so they conduct heat far faster than the insulation. Roughly 25% of a typical wall's area is framing, and that framing can carry 40–45% of the heat that escapes. A wall with R-20 in the cavity may perform closer to R-16 overall.

Knowing the real number helps you make better decisions. If you are building new, you can compare a 2×4 wall with R-13 batts against a 2×6 wall, or see whether one inch of exterior foam is worth the cost. If you are sizing a furnace or heat pump, the U-factor feeds directly into your heat loss calculation. If you are checking energy code compliance, you can see how your assembly compares with the prescriptive values. Homeowners planning a re-side can find out whether adding R-5 foam under new siding would cut wall heat loss by about a quarter, and builders can show clients why thermal bridging matters.

How It's Calculated

The calculator uses the parallel-path method described in the ASHRAE Handbook of Fundamentals. Heat flows through two paths at once: the insulated cavity and the wood framing. Each path gets its own total R-value, found by adding the R-values of every layer in series:

Rcavity path = Routside film + Rsiding + Rcontinuous insulation + Rsheathing + Rcavity insulation + Rdrywall + Rinside film
Rframing path = the same layers, but with the cavity replaced by wood framing at R-1.25 per inch of depth.

The two paths are then combined by area, using the framing fraction f:

U = f ÷ Rframing path + (1 − f) ÷ Rcavity path
Effective R = 1 ÷ U
Heat loss (BTU/hr) = Area × (Tin − Tout) × U

Air films are fixed at R-0.68 inside and R-0.17 outside (15 mph wind). Cavity insulation is its per-inch value times the cavity depth. The table below lists the per-inch values used.

Conservative rounding: the effective R-value is rounded down, while the U-factor and heat loss are rounded up, so the tool never overstates insulating performance. Worked Example

Take a 2×6 wall, 25% framing, fiberglass batts (3.7 × 5.5 = R-20.35), OSB sheathing, vinyl siding and 1/2 in drywall, with 1,000 sq ft of wall at 70°F inside and 20°F outside.

  1. Common layers: 0.17 + 0.61 + 0.50 + 0.45 + 0.68 = R-2.41.
  2. Cavity path: 2.41 + 20.35 = R-22.76.
  3. Framing path: 2.41 + (1.25 × 5.5) = 2.41 + 6.875 = R-9.285 (shown as 9.28 after rounding down).
  4. U = 0.25 ÷ 9.285 + 0.75 ÷ 22.76 = 0.02692 + 0.03295 = 0.05988.
  5. Effective R = 1 ÷ 0.05988 = R-16.7, about 45% of the heat passing through the studs.
  6. Heat loss = 1,000 × 50 × 0.05988 = about 2,994 BTU/hr.

Add 1 inch of XPS (R-5.0) outside the sheathing and the common layers become R-7.41. The framing path rises to R-14.285 and the cavity path to R-27.76, so U falls to 0.04452 and the effective R climbs to about R-22.4. That is a 34% gain from an R-5 layer, because the foam covers the studs too.

Tips & Common Mistakes

  • Don't use the batt label as the wall R-value. Framing, headers, corners and window trim reduce the whole-wall value well below the cavity R-value.
  • Continuous insulation outside the studs is the most effective upgrade. It covers the framing, so it improves both heat paths.
  • Don't compress batts. An R-21 batt squeezed into a 3.5 in cavity won't deliver R-21. Match batt thickness to cavity depth.
  • Use a higher framing fraction for complicated walls. Many windows, doubled studs and tight corners push the fraction toward 28–30%.
  • Watch moisture. Adding exterior foam changes where condensation can form. Check your climate zone's recommended minimum R-value for foam over the sheathing.
  • Air sealing matters as much as R-value. Gaps and leaks can cancel out much of the insulation's benefit.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a good R-value for exterior walls?

Under the 2018 IECC, most of the U.S. calls for R-20, or R-13 plus R-5 continuous insulation, in Zones 3–5, and R-20+5 or R-13+10 in Zones 6–8. Warmer Zones 1–2 call for R-13. Those are nominal values, so the effective whole-wall R-value will be lower.

What is the R-value of a 2×4 wall?

A 2×4 wall with R-13 fiberglass batts has a cavity R-value around 13, but the whole-wall effective value is about R-10 to R-11 once the studs, siding, sheathing and drywall are counted. Run the numbers in the calculator above with your own materials.

What is the R-value of a 2×6 wall?

A 2×6 wall with R-20 or R-21 insulation usually comes out near R-16 to R-17 effective with typical sheathing and siding. Adding 1 inch of exterior XPS or similar foam typically lifts that to the low 20s.

How do I convert R-value to U-factor?

U-factor is the inverse of R-value: U = 1 ÷ R. A wall with R-16.7 has a U-factor of about 0.060 BTU/hr·ft²·°F. To convert to metric RSI, divide the R-value by 5.678.

Why is whole-wall R-value lower than the insulation R-value?

Wood studs have a much lower R-value per inch than insulation, so heat flows through them more easily. This is called thermal bridging. The whole-wall value blends the cavity and framing paths by area, which is why it comes out lower than the cavity R-value.

Does the air film count in R-value?

Yes. Still air against the interior surface adds about R-0.68, and the exterior air film adds about R-0.17 under a 15 mph wind. They are small but are included in standard assembly calculations, and this calculator adds them automatically.
