Why This Matters

Concrete is one of the few building materials you cannot return or top up later. Ready-mix trucks charge by the cubic yard, the clock starts when the driver arrives, and a wall that runs out of concrete halfway up leaves a cold joint, a weak seam where fresh concrete meets concrete that has already begun to set. Order too much and you pay $150 to $200 per yard for material that ends up in a pile.

The usual jobs are easy to picture. A 40-foot foundation stem wall, 3 feet high and 8 inches thick, holds about 80 cubic feet, roughly 2.96 cubic yards before waste. A 30 by 8 foot basement wall at 8 inches thick is almost 6 cubic yards. Homeowners use the number to decide between bagged mix and a truck, since beyond about one cubic yard bags become impractical. Contractors use it for bids, and many suppliers add a short-load fee on small orders, so knowing your true total helps you decide whether to combine pours.

How It's Calculated

Everything starts with the volume of a rectangular slab standing on its edge:

Volume (ft³) = length (ft) × height (ft) × thickness (in) ÷ 12 × number of walls
Net volume = volume − (opening area × thickness ÷ 12 × walls)
Total = net volume × (1 + waste %)
Cubic yards = ft³ ÷ 27 Metric: m³ = length × height × thickness (all in m)

The order quantity is rounded up to the next quarter cubic yard (or 0.1 m³), never down. Bag counts divide your total by typical yields, 0.30 ft³ for a 40 lb bag, 0.45 ft³ for 60 lb and 0.60 ft³ for 80 lb, and round up to whole bags. Weight uses 150 lb per cubic foot (about 2,400 kg/m³) applied to the total including waste, so it errs heavy.

For rebar, the number of bars in each direction is the dimension divided by your spacing, rounded up, plus one. Vertical bars run the wall height and horizontal bars run the wall length. The calculator adds 15% for lap splices and cuts, then divides by 20 ft stock bars (6 m in metric) and rounds up. Openings are not deducted from rebar.

As a safety check the calculator compares your thickness with the common ACI 318 bearing-wall rule of thumb, the greater of 4 inches or one twenty-fifth of the shorter unsupported dimension, and warns if you fall below it. Residential foundation walls are covered by prescriptive tables in IRC R404.1. This tool is a quantity estimator only. It is not an engineering design, permit or inspection approval, and local codes and adopted editions vary.

Worked Example

Say you are pouring one wall 30 ft long, 8 ft high and 8 in thick, with no openings, a 10% waste allowance and a single layer of #4 rebar at 16 in spacing.

  • Net volume: 30 × 8 × (8 ÷ 12) = 160 ft³, or 5.93 yd³.
  • With 10% waste: 160 × 1.10 = 176 ft³, or 6.52 yd³.
  • Order quantity: rounded up to the next quarter yard, 6.75 yd³.
  • Weight: 176 × 150 = 26,400 lb, about 13.2 US tons.
  • Bags: 176 ÷ 0.60 = 293.3, so 294 bags of 80 lb. Order ready-mix instead.
  • Rebar: vertical bars = ceil(30 ÷ 1.333) + 1 = 24 at 8 ft = 192 ft. Horizontal bars = ceil(8 ÷ 1.333) + 1 = 7 at 30 ft = 210 ft. Total 402 ft × 1.15 = 462.3 ft, which is 24 stock bars of 20 ft and about 309 lb.

These are the values the calculator shows with its default inputs.

Tips & Common Mistakes

  • Add the footing separately. This tool covers the wall only. Calculate the footing (length × width × depth) and add it to your order, and make sure it reaches below local frost depth.
  • Measure the real forms. If forms bow out just ½ inch, a 30 × 8 ft wall takes about 10 extra cubic feet, roughly 0.37 yd³.
  • Don't over-deduct openings. Small openings rarely justify the savings, and any bucks, bulkheads and uneven subgrade eat the difference. Keep 5 to 10% waste for forms and 10 to 15% for rough ground.
  • Respect height limits. Walls over about 8 ft, walls holding back soil, and any wall carrying structural load beyond a prescriptive table should be designed by a licensed engineer. Forms must be built for the lateral pressure of fresh concrete, so pour tall walls in lifts.
  • Confirm rebar and cover with your local code. The rebar quantity here is a take-off for the spacing you enter, not a structural specification.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much concrete do I need for a wall?

Multiply length by height by thickness (in the same units) to get volume, then divide cubic feet by 27 for cubic yards. Add 5 to 10% for waste and round up. This concrete wall calculator does all of that for you.

How many bags of concrete make a wall?

An 80 lb bag yields about 0.60 ft³, so a 160 ft³ wall needs roughly 267 bags before waste. For anything over about one cubic yard, ready-mix delivery is almost always cheaper and gives a more consistent pour.

How thick should a concrete wall be?

Common residential foundation walls are 8 inches thick, with 6 inches used in some prescriptive cases and 10 inches or more for taller or more heavily loaded walls. Check the IRC R404.1 tables or ask an engineer, because soil pressure, height and reinforcement all matter.

How much waste should I allow when ordering concrete?

Plan on 5 to 10% for straight forms on a level base and 10 to 15% for uneven ground, long pumping lines or complicated shapes. The cost of a little extra is small compared with a short pour and cold joint.

Does this calculator include the footing?

No. It calculates the wall only. Work out the footing volume separately and add the two together before placing your order.

Is the result safe to build from or submit for a permit?

It is a material quantity estimate, not a structural design. Always follow your local building code, the edition your jurisdiction has adopted, and the advice of a licensed engineer for load-bearing, retaining or tall walls.
