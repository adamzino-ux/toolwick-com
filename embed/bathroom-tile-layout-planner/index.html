Why This Matters

Tile is one of the few materials you can't easily reorder. Dye lots differ between production runs, so a second order six weeks later may not match the first, and a discontinued line can't be matched at all. Running short mid-floor is a real problem. Over-buying 30 square feet of $4 porcelain is $120 you'll never see again.

Layout also decides how the finished room looks. In a 5×8 ft bathroom, a start line that leaves a ¾-inch sliver along the doorway is seen every day for twenty years. Shifting the grid a few inches can turn that sliver into two even half-tiles. Tile setters do a dry layout before mixing any thinset for exactly this reason.

This planner is that dry run on screen. You get the numbers to take to the store, the cut sizes to expect, and a quick check on whether a diagonal or herringbone pattern is worth the extra 5 to 10 percent of material. Homeowners, flippers and installers pricing jobs all use it.

How It's Calculated

Everything is converted to inches first. The steps follow common TCNA Handbook practice and industry waste rules of thumb. Manufacturer instructions and local codes vary, so treat the output as a planning estimate.

pitch p = tile size + grout joint
span = room dimension + one joint
full tiles = floor(span ÷ p)
cut piece = remainder − joint
  • Cut pieces: a remainder counts as a cut tile only if the cut piece is wider than ¼". Narrower gaps are absorbed in the joints. Starting from a wall puts the cut on one side. Centered splits the remainder into two equal cuts.
  • Offset: every second row shifts by half a tile, so those rows need cuts at both ends.
  • Layout tiles: each cut position uses one tile. If two cut pieces plus a ⅛" saw kerf fit in one tile, they share it.
  • Area count: room area ÷ (pitch length × pitch width), multiplied by (1 + waste %).
  • Waste is never stacked: your waste % is the total allowance. Patterns only set a minimum: 0% straight, 10% offset, 15% diagonal, 20% herringbone. If your entry is lower, the tool raises it and says so.
  • Tiles to buy: the larger of the area count with waste and the layout tiles + 1 spare, rounded up. Boxes are always rounded up.
  • Grout: joint volume per sq ft = 144 × (L + W) × joint × thickness ÷ (L × W) in³. Multiply by room area, 0.0672 lb/in³ and a 1.25 allowance, then round up to a whole pound.

Diagonal and herringbone are counted by area only, and their cut count is an estimate of one cut piece per tile-width of wall.

Worked Example

Room 10 ft × 8 ft (length 120", width 96", 80 sq ft). Tile 12" × 12" × ⅜" thick, ⅛" joint, 10% waste, straight grid from a wall, 10 tiles per box, $3.50 per sq ft.

  1. Pitch = 12.125". Across the width: 7 full tiles use 84.875", leaving a cut of 11⅛". That is 8 columns.
  2. Down the length: 9 full tiles use 109.125", leaving a cut of 10⅞". That is 10 rows.
  3. Layout = 8 × 10 = 80 tiles: 63 full and 17 cut pieces (10 in the last column, 8 in the last row, minus the shared corner).
  4. Area count = 11,520 ÷ 147.016 = 78.36 tiles. With 10% waste that is 86.2, rounded up to 87. The layout floor is 80 + 1 = 81, so 87 tiles to buy.
  5. Boxes: 87 ÷ 10 = 8.7, rounded up to 9 boxes (90 tiles). Cost: 90 sq ft × $3.50 = $315.00.
  6. Grout: 1.125 in³ per sq ft × 80 = 90 in³ × 0.0672 × 1.25 = 7.56, rounded up to 8 lb.

Switch to Centered and the cut pieces rise from 17 to 36. Edge cuts pair up on shared tiles, so you still buy 87. The comparison table shows offset at 87, diagonal at 91 (15%) and herringbone at 95 (20%).

Tips & Common Mistakes

  • Buy every box at once and compare lot numbers. A later order often comes from a different dye lot, and keeping a box or two for future repairs is cheap insurance.
  • Check the narrowest cut. If the tool warns about a sliver under a quarter tile, switch between wall-start and centered, or shift the starting line so edge pieces are at least half a tile.
  • Enter the tile's real size. Rectified porcelain is often 11.8" rather than 12", and a metric tile entered in the wrong unit can throw the count off badly.
  • Don't forget flatness. TCNA and ANSI A108 guidance commonly cited is ¼" in 10 ft for smaller tile and ⅛" in 10 ft for large-format tile (an edge of 15" or more). Verify the current edition and the tile maker's limits.
  • Plan shower floors separately. Floors need slope to the drain (commonly ¼" per foot minimum, but local code governs), so small mosaics usually work better than large tiles.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much waste should I add for bathroom tile?

Ten percent is the usual allowance for a straight grid. Offset patterns need at least 10%, diagonal at least 15%, and herringbone about 20%. This tool raises a lower entry to the pattern minimum and never stacks the two. Add a few more percent if the room has many niches or corners.

What is the best tile size for a small bathroom?

For a 5×8 ft bathroom, 12×12 inch tiles are a safe choice because they look current and keep cuts manageable. Large-format tiles can make a small room feel bigger but need a flatter subfloor and often create more edge waste. Try both sizes here and compare the cut widths.

How do I calculate how many boxes of tile to buy?

Divide the tiles to buy by the tiles per box, then round up. Enter tiles per box and the tool does this for you. Never round down, because a partial reorder later may come from a different dye lot.

Does grout joint size change how many tiles I need?

Slightly. A ⅛" joint on a 12" tile makes each position 12.125" wide, which can drop or add one row or column in a particular room. A wider joint also shifts where the edge cuts land, so always enter your actual joint.

Should I center the tile layout or start from a wall?

Starting from the wall people see most, or from the back wall of a shower, keeps full tiles where they show. Centering gives equal cuts on both sides, which looks balanced but creates more cut pieces. Run both and compare the cut sizes and counts.

Should I subtract the tub, vanity or toilet area?

This tool treats the floor as a full rectangle, which rounds up on the safe side. Tile cut around fixtures produces extra waste anyway. For an L-shaped room, calculate each rectangle separately and add the results.
