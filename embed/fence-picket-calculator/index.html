Why This Matters

Pickets are usually the biggest line item in a wood fence by piece count. A 100-foot run with 5.5-inch pickets takes 219 pickets with no gaps, 201 with a half-inch gap, and 185 with a one-inch gap. At $2.50 to $4 each, that 34-picket spread is $85 to $136. Lumber from different lots also varies in color and moisture, so running short and returning a month later often leaves a visibly mismatched strip of fence.

Homeowners use a picket count to compare contractor quotes. DIYers use it to place a single lumber order, and landscapers use it to price jobs. It also matters for compliance. Many jurisdictions cap fence height, commonly around 4 ft in front yards and 6 ft in back yards. Pool barriers and child-safety rules commonly require openings smaller than 4 inches, which limits how wide your gap can be. Local rules and HOA covenants vary, so confirm them before you buy.

How It's Calculated

The first picket takes one full width. Each additional picket then adds one "pitch", the center-to-center distance between neighbors. The calculator rounds up so you are never one picket short.

Pickets = ⌈(R − w) ÷ pitch⌉ + 1

R is the run length in inches and w is the picket width. Pitch is w + gap for a side-by-side fence, or w − overlap for board-on-board. Gates use the same formula with each gate's width and are added only if you choose to picket them.

Total to buy = ⌈(fence + gate pickets) × (1 + waste %)⌉

Post sections equal the net run divided by post spacing, rounded up. Rails per section follow a common rule of thumb, not a code requirement: 2 rails up to 4 ft tall, 3 rails up to 6 ft, and 4 rails above that. Fasteners are pickets × rails × 2, one pair where each picket crosses each rail. If you spread the pickets evenly, the real gap becomes (R − n × w) ÷ (n − 1), where n is the picket count. The tool shows this fit under the results table.

Worked Example

These are the default values in the tool: a 100 ft fence, 5.5 in pickets, 0.5 in gap, 6 ft tall, posts 8 ft apart, one 4 ft picketed gate and 10% waste.

  1. Net fence run: 100 − 4 = 96 ft = 1,152 in.
  2. Pitch: 5.5 + 0.5 = 6 in.
  3. Fence pickets: (1,152 − 5.5) ÷ 6 = 191.08, rounded up to 192, plus 1 = 193.
  4. Gate pickets: 4 ft = 48 in, so (48 − 5.5) ÷ 6 = 7.08, rounded up to 8, plus 1 = 9.
  5. Subtotal 202. With 10% waste: 222.2, rounded up to 223 pickets to buy.
  6. Sections: 96 ÷ 8 = 12. A 6 ft fence uses 3 rails, so 36 rail pieces.
  7. Fasteners: 223 × 3 × 2 = 1,338.
  8. Even-spread gap: (1,152 − 5.5) ÷ 192 = 5.971 in pitch, minus 5.5 = about 0.47 in.
  9. At $2.50 per picket, 223 × $2.50 = $557.50.

Tips & Common Mistakes

  • Use actual widths, not nominal. A "1×6" picket is about 5.5 in wide, and dog-ear or cedar pickets can differ. Measure one from the stack.
  • Subtract gate openings from the run. Counting gates as fence and then adding gate pickets double-counts them. The tool handles this for you.
  • Keep gaps under 4 in if children or pools are involved. Many local rules use a 4-inch sphere test. Check with your building department.
  • Expect wet lumber to shrink. Pressure-treated pickets installed wet open up as they dry, so butt them tightly for privacy and allow a slightly higher waste figure.
  • Use corrosion-resistant fasteners. Hot-dip galvanized or stainless screws and nails suit treated lumber. Buy extra, since fasteners are cheap and trips are not.

Frequently Asked Questions

How many pickets do I need per foot of fence?

It depends on width and gap. 5.5-inch pickets with no gap need about 2.2 per foot (12 ÷ 5.5), and with a half-inch gap exactly 2 per foot. Narrow 3.5-inch pickets with a 1-inch gap need about 2.7 per foot.

How many pickets are in a 100 ft fence?

With 5.5-inch pickets, a 100 ft run needs 219 with no gaps, 201 with a half-inch gap, and 185 with a one-inch gap, before waste. Subtract any gates and add 5–10% for cuts and defects.

How much gap should I leave between fence pickets?

Privacy fences use 0 to ¼ inch. Decorative or semi-private fences often use ½ to 3½ inches. Keep gaps below 4 inches wherever pool-barrier or child-safety rules apply, and confirm local requirements.

How does board-on-board change the picket count?

The overlap shrinks the pitch, so you need more pickets. With 5.5-inch pickets and a 1-inch overlap, a 100 ft run needs about 267, roughly 22% more than a butted fence. The payoff is a fully private fence that stays opaque as the wood shrinks.

How much waste should I add when ordering pickets?

Ten percent is a safe default for a first-time builder. Experienced crews may use 5% on straight, high-grade stock. Add more if you have many angles, a sloped site or a lot of warped lumber.

Is this calculator enough to get a permit or sign off the build?

No. It is a material estimator that rounds up on counts. Fence height, setbacks, pool barriers, wind exposure and HOA rules vary by location, so check with your local building department before you build.
