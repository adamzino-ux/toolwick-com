Why This Matters

A shed floor is the one part you cannot easily fix once the walls are up. Undersized joists leave a bouncy floor that cracks workbench legs and makes a loaded mower rack sag, while a foundation that settles or heaves in its first winter racks the frame until the door will not close. The right answer depends on what the shed holds. A 10×12 garden shed with bins and tools is a 40 psf light-storage floor. A 12×16 workshop with a table saw and benches is closer to 60 psf. A 12×20 building that stores a ride-on mower, an ATV or a gun safe should be treated as 100 psf, and a slab is usually the better floor.

The weight adds up faster than most people expect. A 12×16 shed with a 30 psf snow roof puts roughly 25,000 pounds on its footings. On 1,500 psf soil that means about 17 square feet of bearing area, spread across piers or skids. Knowing this before you buy materials lets you compare a skid base, a pier-and-beam base and a slab on cost and effort. It also gives you real numbers to take to the building department, where many areas exempt small sheds from permits but still enforce setbacks and, in some places, frost-depth rules.

How It's Calculated

Loads. Design load per square foot of footprint is q = (floor live + 10) + 1.1 × (snow + 10) + wall weight. Wall weight is the perimeter × 8 ft × 8 psf divided by floor area, and the 1.1 allows for roof overhang. Floor and snow loads are added together, which is deliberately conservative.

Joists. Each joist is treated as a simple beam between support lines. The maximum span is the smallest of four limits: bending L = √(8·Fb′·S / w), shear, live-load deflection of L/360, and total-load deflection of L/240 using L = ∛(384·E·I / (5·n·w)). Fb′ is the No. 2 grade bending value for the species with its size factor and the 1.15 repetitive-member factor, and S and I come from the actual lumber dimensions. The result is cut by 5% and rounded down to the whole inch. If the shed width is longer than that span, the width is split into equal spans by adding beam or skid lines. The limits match the basis of IRC R502.3, but the spans are calculated, not copied from a table, so compare them with your local span tables.

Foundations. A pier footing is sized as pier load ÷ soil bearing, where pier load = q × beam tributary width × pier spacing × 1.1, rounded up to a standard width from 12 to 48 in. Footing depth is the frost depth rounded up to the next 6 in, with a 12 in minimum. Skid loads are spread through a 6 in gravel pad. Slabs get a thickened edge at least 12 in wide and as deep as the frost depth rounded up to 6 in. Soil values are presumptive: 2,000 psf good, 1,500 psf average, 1,000 psf poor.

Worked Example

A 12 ft × 16 ft workshop shed: 60 psf floor load, average soil, 24 in frost depth, 30 psf snow load, 16 in spacing, Douglas Fir-Larch, joist size on Auto.

  1. Area is 192 sq ft and perimeter 56 ft. Design load q = 70 + 1.1 × 40 + (56 × 64 ÷ 192) = 70 + 44 + 18.7 ≈ 132.7 psf.
  2. Conservative maximum spans at 16 in are about 8 ft 1 in for 2×6, 10 ft 3 in for 2×8, 12 ft 6 in for 2×10 and 14 ft 6 in for 2×12. Only 2×10 and 2×12 cross the 12 ft width in one span, so Auto picks 2×10 with two support lines.
  3. The area is over 144 sq ft, so Auto recommends piers and beams. Each beam line carries 6 ft of width, about 796 lb per foot. Doubled 2×10 beams need piers about every 5.3 ft, giving 4 piers per line and 8 in total.
  4. Each pier carries about 4,670 lb, which needs 3.1 sq ft of bearing, so 24 in square footings set 24 in deep.
  5. Materials: about 1.08 cubic yards of concrete with 10% waste, 13 joists plus two rim boards, and 7 sheets of decking. At the default placeholder prices the total is about $1,044.

Tips & Common Mistakes

  • Get your frost depth from the local building department, not from a map or a guess. Footings above the frost line heave.
  • Strip topsoil and roots, compact the subgrade and slope the gravel so water drains away. Poor drainage defeats a good footing.
  • Use ground-contact pressure-treated lumber for skids and anything near the soil, and keep untreated joists well off the ground.
  • Add doubled joists or extra blocking under workbenches, safes and lifts. Point loads are not covered by a uniform floor load.
  • Match decking to spacing: 3/4 in sheets suit 16 in centers at moderate loads, while 24 in spacing or heavy use calls for 1-1/8 in T&G.
  • Do not swap species without recalculating. Spruce-Pine-Fir and Hem-Fir span less than Douglas Fir-Larch at the same size.

Frequently Asked Questions

How deep should shed foundation footings be?

Footings should bottom out below your local frost depth, which ranges from 0 in in warm states to 48 in or more in cold ones. This calculator rounds the frost depth up to the next 6 in and never goes under 12 in. Always confirm the figure with your local building department.

Do I need a concrete foundation for a shed?

Not always. Small storage sheds often sit on gravel-pad skids, which many jurisdictions allow for non-habitable buildings. Larger sheds, heavy loads, soft soil or permanent structures usually call for piers or a slab, and some areas require footings below frost regardless of size.

What size joists do I need for a 10×12 shed?

In the default case of a 60 psf floor, Douglas Fir-Larch at 16 in spacing, a 2×8 spans about 10 ft 3 in, which just clears the 10 ft width. Lower-strength species, 24 in spacing or heavier loads need a 2×10 or a center beam line, so run your exact inputs through the calculator.

Should I space shed floor joists 16 or 24 inches on center?

Sixteen inches is the safer, stiffer choice and allows standard 3/4 in decking. Twenty-four inches saves lumber but shortens the allowable span and needs thicker decking, so it suits light storage only. Twelve inches is worth it for heavy equipment floors.

Skids, piers or a slab: which is best for a shed?

Skids on a gravel pad are cheapest and fine for small, light sheds on firm, well-drained ground. Piers cost more but handle larger sheds, slopes and frost. A slab suits heavy equipment and big workshops and doubles as the floor, at the highest cost.

How far apart should shed piers be?

For doubled 2×10 beams the calculator tests spacings of 8, 6, 4 and 3 ft and keeps the widest one that passes bending and deflection checks. With a typical 60 psf floor and 30 psf snow load that is usually near 5 to 6 ft. Heavier loads shrink the spacing.
