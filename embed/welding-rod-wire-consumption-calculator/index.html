Why This Matters

Filler metal is easy to under-buy. A fabrication shop welding 500 ft of 5/16" fillets with E7018 deposits roughly 0.18 lb of weld metal per foot, so about 90 lb of weld metal. At 70% efficiency that is nearly 130 lb of electrode, or around $450 at $3.50/lb. Guess low and the job stops while someone drives to the supplier; guess high and rod sits in the cabinet and absorbs moisture.

Estimators, ironworkers, farm and ranch welders and school shop teachers use the same math. A stick electrode only becomes weld metal in part: stubs, slag and spatter take the rest. Low-hydrogen rod also has to be kept in a rod oven, so buying only what you need matters. Comparing processes helps too. The same 6.57 lb of weld metal needs about 9.4 lb of E7018 but only about 7.1 lb of MIG wire, which can change the bid.

How It's Calculated

The tool uses the standard geometric weld-volume method: find the cross-section, multiply by length, convert to weight, then divide by efficiency. Steel density is 0.2836 lb/in³.

V-groove area = tan(bevel°) × T² + root gap × T
Fillet area = 0.5 × leg²
Area used = nominal area × 1.10 (reinforcement)
Weld metal (lb) = area used × length (in) × 0.2836
Order (lb) = weld metal ÷ efficiency × (1 + allowance)

Efficiency is the weight of weld metal you get per pound of electrode or wire purchased, so stubs, flux coating and spatter are already inside it. It is applied once only. Process defaults (editable) are: E6010/E6011 60%, E6013 65%, E7018 70%, TIG rod 90%, MIG 92%, gas-shielded flux-cored 85%, self-shielded flux-cored 80%. They sit at the conservative end of typical ranges.

Rod count: a whole 14" stick electrode weighs core wire × coating factor (E6010 1.25, E6013 1.40, E7018 1.50); TIG rod is 36" bare wire. The order weight is divided by that whole-rod weight and rounded up. Wire length comes from the wire's cross-section (flux-cored wire is assumed 15% lighter per foot than solid). Passes on the groove tab are approximate, assuming each stringer bead fills about 1.8 × diameter² of the groove. Rod weights vary by manufacturer, so treat counts as planning figures.

Weld sizes should follow the drawings and the code that applies to the job, such as AWS D1.1 for structural steel. This method is a common estimating practice, not a code requirement. Everything that affects ordering is rounded up: rods, pounds and cost.

Worked Example

Butt joint, 10 ft (120 in) long, 1/2" plate, 30° bevel each side, 1/16" root gap, 1/8" E7018, $3.50/lb, 70% efficiency, 10% allowance. These are the tool's defaults.

  1. Groove area = tan 30° × 0.5² + 0.0625 × 0.5 = 0.1443 + 0.0313 = 0.1756 in². With 10% reinforcement: 0.1931 in².
  2. Volume = 0.1931 × 120 = 23.18 in³.
  3. Weld metal = 23.18 × 0.2836 = 6.57 lb.
  4. Electrode before allowance = 6.57 ÷ 0.70 = 9.40 lb (129 rods).
  5. With 10% allowance = 10.33 lb. A 14" 1/8" E7018 rod weighs about 0.0731 lb, so 10.33 ÷ 0.0731 = 141.3, which rounds up to 142 rods.
  6. Cost = 10.33 lb × $3.50 ≈ $36.16. Passes ≈ 0.1931 ÷ 0.0281 = 6.9, so about 7.

Tips & Common Mistakes

  • Don't use 100% efficiency. Stick runs 55–70%, flux-cored 75–90%, solid MIG about 92–98%. Assuming 100% under-orders by a third on stick jobs.
  • Enter the fillet leg you will actually build. Metal grows with the square of the leg: a 3/8" fillet needs 2.25 times the metal of a 1/4" fillet. The pass count does not change the total metal.
  • Measure the real bevel and gap. A 45° bevel on 1" plate needs about twice the metal of 30°. Poor fit-up with wide gaps adds more.
  • Keep the allowance for rework and restarts. 10% suits shop work; use 15–20% for field, out-of-position or code-critical welds.
  • Check rod condition. Low-hydrogen rod that has absorbed moisture is wasted. Order what you can use and store it per the maker's guidance.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is deposition efficiency in welding?

It is the share of purchased electrode or wire that ends up as weld metal. The rest is lost as stub ends, slag, flux and spatter. Stick is typically 55–70%, solid MIG wire 92% or more.

How many E7018 rods do I need per pound of weld metal?

A 14" 1/8" E7018 rod weighs about 0.073 lb and, at 70% efficiency, delivers about 0.051 lb of weld metal. That works out to roughly 20 rods per pound of weld metal, or about 14 rods per pound of electrode purchased.

Can I use this for stainless steel or aluminum?

The geometry is the same, but the tool assumes steel at 0.2836 lb/in³. Stainless is about 2% heavier, and aluminum weighs only about a third as much (0.0975 lb/in³). Treat results for those metals as rough and check the filler manufacturer's recovery data.

Why does the calculator add 10% for reinforcement?

Real welds have convex caps and fillets are rarely perfectly flat triangles, so they hold more metal than the nominal area. Adding 10% keeps the estimate on the safe side. It is separate from the waste slider.

How much MIG wire do I need per foot of 1/4" fillet?

One foot of 1/4" fillet holds about 0.117 lb of weld metal with the reinforcement included. At 92% efficiency that is about 0.127 lb of wire per foot before any allowance.

How accurate are the results?

They are theoretical figures for ideal geometry. Real jobs commonly run 5–20% higher depending on skill, fit-up and position, so keep a sensible allowance and confirm sizes against your WPS.
