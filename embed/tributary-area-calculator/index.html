Why This Matters

Every load path in a building ends at the ground, and tributary area is how you trace it. Before you can size a beam, a post or a footing, you need to know how many square feet of floor or roof that member actually carries. Guess too low and you build something undersized. Guess too high and you pay for steel and concrete you did not need.

Consider a few real situations. A homeowner removing a bearing wall needs to know whether a new header carries 8 feet or 14 feet of floor. A deck builder spacing posts 10 feet apart, with joists spanning 12 feet, needs the load on each post and the footing size under it. A contractor framing a 20 × 24 ft bay in a two-story building needs the ground-floor column load, which stacks both levels. The arithmetic is always area times pressure: at a combined 55 psf, a 480 sq ft interior bay puts 26,400 lb on its column from a single level.

Engineers do the same thing at the start of every design. Use this tool to sanity-check a plan, compare layouts, or prepare for a conversation with your engineer or building department.

How It's Calculated

All three modes start from the idea that load is shared halfway to the neighbouring supports. In the formulas below, DL is dead load, LL is live load (both in psf), A and B are the distances to the framing on each side of a beam, X and Y are the bay sizes, and n is the number of levels.

Beam:
  Tributary width  wT = (A + B) / 2
  Tributary area   AT = wT × span
  Line load        w  = wT × (DL + LL)         [plf]
  End reaction     R  = w × span / 2           [lb]
  Maximum moment   M  = w × span² / 8          [lb-ft]
Column:
  Interior  AT = X × Y
  Edge      AT = X × Y / 2
  Corner    AT = X × Y / 4
  Load      P  = AT × (DL + LL) × n
Footing:
  Design load   = 1.10 × P   (10% allowance for footing weight)
  Required area = design load / allowable bearing q
  Side          = √(required area), rounded UP to the next 3 in
                  (never less than 12 in)

Service loads (DL + LL) are what you compare with allowable soil bearing and use for allowable stress design. The factored load, 1.2DL + 1.6LL, is the ASCE 7 load combination used for strength design. Live loads in the presets come from IRC Table R301.5 and ASCE 7 minimums, and soil values from IRC Table R401.4.1 (2021 edition). No live load reduction is applied, which is conservative. Every result is rounded up, never down, and local codes vary.

Worked Example

An interior column sits in a two-story building with 20 ft × 24 ft bays. Dead load is 15 psf, live load is 40 psf on both levels, and the soil is sand with 2,000 psf allowable bearing.

  • Tributary area: 20 × 24 = 480 sq ft (interior column, so the full bay).
  • Service load per level: 480 × (15 + 40) = 26,400 lb. Across 2 levels: 52,800 lb (14,400 lb dead + 38,400 lb live).
  • Factored load: 1.2 × 15 + 1.6 × 40 = 82 psf. 480 × 82 × 2 = 78,720 lb.
  • Footing design load: 52,800 × 1.10 = 58,080 lb.
  • Required area: 58,080 / 2,000 = 29.04 sq ft. The square root is 5.39 ft.
  • Recommended side: rounded up to the next 3 in gives 5.50 ft (5 ft 6 in). Provided area is 30.25 sq ft, so the actual bearing pressure is 1,920 psf, below the 2,000 psf limit.

Tips & Common Mistakes

  • Measure to the midpoint, not the wall. Tributary boundaries sit halfway between supports. If the spans on the two sides of a column differ, enter the average of the two.
  • Don't forget stacking. A ground-floor column carries every level above it plus the roof. Roofs usually have different dead and snow loads, so calculate them separately and add the results.
  • Use realistic dead loads. Light wood floors are often 10–15 psf, but tile, a concrete topping or heavy partitions can push that to 25–60 psf or more.
  • Add point loads separately. Posts, walls or equipment bearing on a beam are not part of the uniform tributary load. Add them to the reactions yourself.
  • Area is not a member size. Beam and header sizing also depends on deflection, shear, bearing length and connections. Footings also need thickness, reinforcement and a frost-depth check.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is tributary area?

Tributary area is the portion of a floor or roof whose load travels into a particular beam, column or footing. Its boundaries are usually halfway to the neighbouring supports. Multiply it by the pressure (psf) to get the load on that member.

How do you calculate tributary area for a column?

Multiply the two bay dimensions around the column: X × Y for an interior column, half of that for an edge column and a quarter for a corner column. For a 20 × 24 ft bay that is 480, 240 and 120 sq ft respectively.

How do you find the tributary width of a beam?

Add the distances to the adjacent beams or walls on each side and divide by two. A beam with framing 12 ft away on both sides has a 12 ft tributary width. Over a 16 ft span that is 192 sq ft, or 660 plf and 5,280 lb at each end at 55 psf.

What live load should I use?

Use the value required by your local code for the occupancy. Typical minimums are 40 psf for residential living areas, 30 psf for sleeping rooms and 20 psf for roofs. Choose a higher number if you are unsure, because it errs on the safe side.

Is a rectangular tributary area accurate for two-way slabs?

Two-way slabs split load along 45° lines, giving triangular or trapezoidal shares for each supporting beam. Treating the load as rectangular strips overestimates beam load, which is the safe direction for a quick check. Column tributary areas stay X × Y either way.

Why does the footing result add 10%?

Allowable soil bearing has to carry the footing's own weight and any soil above it, not just the column load. A 10% allowance is a common rough figure for spread footings. Your engineer may use a more exact value.

Can I use these results for a permit?

No. This is a preliminary estimate for planning and sanity checks. Permit drawings and member sizes should come from a licensed engineer or be verified by your local building department, since codes and soil conditions vary.
