Why This Matters

Laminate is sold by the full box, usually 15 to 25 square feet each, and a mismatch is expensive either way. Buy one box too few and you may find the style discontinued or from a different production lot, with slightly different shading that shows up as a stripe across your floor. Buy several boxes too many and, since many retailers restrict returns on opened or special-order flooring, you have paid for material you cannot use.

Consider a 15 × 12 ft bedroom: that is 180 square feet. With 10% waste you need 198 square feet, which is 10 boxes of 20 square feet, not 9. A diagonal installation in the same room pushes the target to 207 square feet, or 11 boxes, because every plank end is cut at an angle and offcuts are harder to reuse. Homeowners planning a weekend project, landlords flipping units and contractors writing quotes all need this number early, because it drives the budget, the delivery size and the acclimation space. Laminate planks typically need to sit in the room for around 48 hours before installation, so you want everything on site at once.

How It's Calculated

Net area = (Length × Width) + (Extra L × Extra W) − Subtracted area
Area to buy = Net area × (1 + Waste % ÷ 100)
Boxes = ROUND UP (Area to buy ÷ Coverage per box)
Planks = ROUND UP (Area to buy ÷ Plank area)
Underlayment rolls = ROUND UP (Net area × 1.05 ÷ Roll size)
Trim length = Sum of rectangle perimeters × 1.10

The waste factor covers end-of-row offcuts, miscut planks and planks with chipped edges. Common rules of thumb are about 5–7% for a simple straight lay in a square room, 10% for typical rooms with doorways and closets, 15% for diagonal layouts and 20% or more for herringbone or heavily broken-up spaces. These are industry rules of thumb, not code requirements, and manufacturers publish their own guidance.

Boxes are always rounded up, never down, so the result errs on the side of enough material. Underlayment assumes rolls of 100 sq ft (10 m² in metric mode) with 5% added for overlaps and cuts. The trim figure adds 10% for corner cuts and is deliberately generous because it adds every rectangle's full perimeter, including edges that may be shared. Laminate installation is generally not subject to a specific building code formula, but local codes, fire-rating rules for multi-family buildings and your product's warranty may impose requirements. This tool is a planning estimate, not an inspection or permit approval.

Worked Example

A living room measures 18 × 14 ft, with a 3 × 5 ft closet that gets the same flooring and a 12 sq ft built-in cabinet that will not. The laminate comes in 20 sq ft boxes at $48 each, planks are 48 × 7.5 in, and the layout is straight with 10% waste.

  1. Main room: 18 × 14 = 252 sq ft. Closet: 3 × 5 = 15 sq ft.
  2. Net area: 252 + 15 − 12 = 255 sq ft.
  3. With waste: 255 × 1.10 = 280.5 sq ft.
  4. Boxes: 280.5 ÷ 20 = 14.03, rounded up to 15 boxes.
  5. Cost: 15 × $48 = $720.00. Coverage purchased is 300 sq ft, leaving 45 sq ft spare for future repairs.
  6. Planks: each is 360 sq in = 2.5 sq ft, so 280.5 ÷ 2.5 = 112.2, or about 113 planks.
  7. Underlayment: 255 × 1.05 = 267.75 sq ft, so 3 rolls of 100 sq ft.
  8. Trim: (64 + 16) × 1.10 = 88 linear ft.

Notice that 14.03 boxes forces a 15th box. That small overage is normal and usefully becomes your repair stock.

Tips & Common Mistakes

  • Measure at the widest points. Rooms are rarely perfectly square. Use the longest length and width, and add alcoves or closets separately.
  • Do not subtract doorways. Floor running through a door opening is usually needed, and a small allowance beats being short.
  • Buy everything from one production lot. Check the lot number on each box. Slight colour differences between batches are very visible on a finished floor.
  • Keep the expansion gap in mind. Laminate floats and needs a perimeter gap, commonly ¼ to ⅜ in (6–10 mm). Many products also limit the maximum run length before a transition is needed, so check the manufacturer's instructions.
  • Use the coverage on the box, not a guess. Box coverage varies from about 15 to 26 sq ft depending on plank size and plank count.
  • Prepare the subfloor first. Most makers require a flat, dry subfloor, often within about 3/16 in over 10 ft. Fixing it later means pulling the floor up.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much extra laminate flooring should I buy?

A common rule of thumb is 5–10% extra for straight installations, 15% for diagonal layouts and up to 20% for herringbone. Rooms with many corners, closets or angled walls should use the higher end. Always follow your product's installation guide if it recommends a specific figure.

How do I calculate how many boxes of laminate flooring I need?

Multiply room length by width for the area, add waste, and divide by the square footage on one box. Then round up to the next whole box. This laminate flooring calculator does those steps and also shows alternate waste scenarios.

Should I include closets and hallways in my measurement?

Yes, if the same flooring will run into them. Use the extra-area fields for closets, alcoves or short hallways. If a hallway is a long run connected to the room, measure it as its own rectangle.

Do I need underlayment under laminate flooring?

Most laminate needs an underlayment for sound absorption, minor levelling and a moisture barrier over concrete. Some planks come with a pad already attached, so check the product details before buying extra. The calculator shows rolls based on net area plus 5% for overlaps.

How many laminate planks are in a box?

It depends on plank size. A typical 48 × 7.5 in plank covers 2.5 sq ft, so a 20 sq ft box holds about 8 planks. Check the packaging for the exact count, and use the plank fields for an approximate total.

Can I use this calculator for vinyl plank or tile?

The area and waste math is the same, but waste rates and box sizes differ by material. For other materials, try the related calculators below, which use settings suited to each product.
