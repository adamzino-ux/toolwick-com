Why This Matters

Recessed cans are permanent. Once you cut ten holes in drywall, moving them means patching, repainting and rewiring. A 12 × 15 ft living room with 9 ft ceilings needs a very different plan from a 9 × 11 ft galley kitchen or a 3 ft wide hallway, and a rough guess is how people end up with dark corners, scalloped light on the walls, or a ceiling that looks like a runway.

Every extra can also adds a ceiling hole, wiring, a trim kit and labor, often well over $100 once everything is counted. Electricians use a layout like this to quote the job, homeowners use it before the rough-in inspection, and designers use it to decide between 4-inch accent cans and 6-inch general cans. Kitchens, baths and basements with 8 ft ceilings all call for tighter spacing than a 14 ft vaulted great room. Planning on paper takes ten minutes and avoids most of the expensive mistakes.

How It's Calculated

The tool starts from the standard rule of thumb that cans should be spaced no farther apart than half the ceiling height, then adjusts for room type and fixture size:

Max spacing = (Ceiling height ÷ 2) × Room factor × Size factor × Spacing adjustment

Room factors are 1.00 for general and hallway, 0.80 for kitchen and bath (tighter task lighting) and 1.20 for accent. Size factors are 0.85 for 4-inch, 1.00 for 6-inch and 1.15 for 8-inch cans. The slider multiplies the result from 70% to 130%.

Even bays (default): fixtures per axis = round up (dimension ÷ max spacing). The room is divided into that many equal bays with one fixture centered in each, so actual spacing = dimension ÷ count and the wall offset is half the spacing. Fixed offset: available = dimension − 2 × offset, gaps = round up (available ÷ max spacing), fixtures = gaps + 1. Both methods round up, so spacing never exceeds the maximum.

Footcandles = Fixtures × Lumens × CU ÷ Room area (sq ft)

CU (coefficient of utilization) is 0.65, 0.55 or 0.45 for light, medium or dark finishes. Rule-of-thumb targets are 20–30 fc general, 30–50 fc kitchen, 40–60 fc bath, 10–20 fc hallway and accent. Total load is fixtures × watts. Circuits are total watts ÷ 1,440 W, the 80% limit of a 15 A, 120 V circuit, rounded up. Annual energy assumes 3 hours a day and $0.16 per kWh.

Worked Example

A 12 × 15 ft living room, 9 ft ceiling, 6-inch LED cans (750 lm, 11 W), general lighting, light finish, slider at 100%.

  • Max spacing = 9 ÷ 2 × 1.00 × 1.00 = 4.5 ft.
  • Width: 12 ÷ 4.5 = 2.67, rounded up to 3 columns. Spacing = 4.00 ft, offset = 2.00 ft.
  • Length: 15 ÷ 4.5 = 3.33, rounded up to 4 rows. Spacing = 3.75 ft, offset = 1.88 ft (about 1′ 11″).
  • Fixtures = 3 × 4 = 12, or 15 sq ft each.
  • Light level = 12 × 750 × 0.65 ÷ 180 = 32.5 fc, slightly above the 20–30 target, so put them on a dimmer.
  • Load = 132 W, one 15 A circuit by wattage, about 145 kWh a year.

Comparison: at 120% spacing the layout is 3 × 3 = 9 fixtures at 24.4 fc. At 80% it is 4 × 5 = 20 fixtures at 54.2 fc, which suits a kitchen better than a living room.

Tips & Common Mistakes

  • Check the framing first. Joists, ducts and beams often force 3–6 inch shifts, so compare this grid against your joist layout before cutting. Keep the grid symmetrical.
  • Use IC-rated, airtight cans in insulated ceilings. Non-IC housings must stay clear of insulation, and energy codes in many areas require sealed fixtures under attics.
  • Zone and dim. Put general, task and accent cans on separate switches, and use dimmers rated for LED loads. Check the dimmer's maximum LED wattage, which is often lower than its incandescent rating.
  • Don't mix color temperatures. Pick one (2700K–3000K for homes) across every can in sight of each other, since a mix looks like a mistake.
  • Chalk the layout before you cut. Snap lines at the column and row positions from the table and step back to judge it from the doorway.

Frequently Asked Questions

How far apart should recessed lights be?

The common rule is no more than half the ceiling height, so about 4.5 ft apart under a 9 ft ceiling. Use about 20% less in kitchens and baths, and a little more for accent lighting. Larger cans can run slightly wider than 4-inch cans.

How far should recessed lights be from the wall?

A typical offset is half the fixture spacing, so about 2 ft for a 4 ft spacing. Moving cans 1.5–2 ft from a wall can wash it with light and highlight art, but too close creates harsh scallops. The calculator also supports a fixed offset.

How many recessed lights do I need for a room?

With 6-inch cans under 8–9 ft ceilings, expect roughly one per 15–20 sq ft for general lighting and one per 9–13 sq ft in kitchens. The exact number depends on the grid, which is why the calculator rounds up to full bays on each axis.

Should I use 4-inch or 6-inch recessed lights?

Six-inch cans give broader light and need fewer holes, making them a good general-purpose choice. Four-inch cans suit accent lighting, low ceilings and narrow halls. You can mix sizes if they sit on separate switches.

How many recessed lights can go on one 15-amp circuit?

By wattage, a 15 A circuit allows 1,440 W of continuous load, which is over 100 LED cans at 11 W. In practice, dimmer ratings, switch zones and local rules decide, and many installers plan zones of roughly 6–12 fixtures. Confirm with your electrician.

Does ceiling height change recessed light spacing?

Yes. Light spreads from the fixture in a cone, so taller ceilings let beams overlap farther away and allow wider spacing. A 12 ft ceiling gives about 6 ft maximum spacing, but you will want more lumens per fixture to hold the light level at the floor.
