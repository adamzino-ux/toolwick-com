Why This Matters

The rim joist (also called the band joist or box sill) is the board that wraps the top of your foundation wall and caps the ends of your floor joists. It is wood sitting on concrete, full of gaps, and in most basements it is either bare or stuffed with a loose scrap of fiberglass. That makes it one of the leakiest spots in the house: cold air, insects and moisture can all get in, and in winter the floor above feels it.

A typical 30 × 40 ft house has 140 linear feet of rim joist. With 2×10 joists and a covered sill plate that is about 125 sq ft of area, roughly the size of one small bedroom wall. Insulating it to R-20 with closed-cell spray foam takes around 400 board feet of foam. That is a weekend project for a confident DIYer, or a half-day job for a spray crew.

Homeowners use this calculation before buying materials, before comparing contractor quotes, and when planning a basement finish. Knowing the area and thickness lets you check that a quote is realistic and that your shopping list covers the whole perimeter, not just the easy walls.

How It's Calculated

The calculator works from the geometry of the rim joist and the R-value per inch of each material.

Perimeter (ft)     = 2 × (length + width)
Covered height     = joist depth + 1.5 in (if sill plate covered)
Gross area (sq ft) = perimeter × covered height ÷ 12
Net area           = gross area − openings
Order area         = net area × (1 + waste %)
Thickness needed   = target R ÷ R-per-inch, rounded UP to stock size
Achieved R         = thickness × R-per-inch
Spray foam         = order area × thickness  (board feet)
Foam board         = ceil(order area ÷ 32) × layers  (4×8 sheets)
Joist cavities     = ceil(perimeter × 12 ÷ joist spacing)

R-values per inch used: closed-cell spray foam 6.5, XPS 5.0, polyiso 5.6 (a conservative aged, cold-weather figure), EPS 4.0, mineral wool 4.2 and fiberglass 3.2. Spray foam is rounded up to the next ¼ in, foam board to the next ½ in (minimum 1 in, stacked in layers when needed), and batts to the next standard depth of 3.5, 5.5 or 7.25 in.

Every rounding step goes up, never down, so the achieved R-value meets or beats your target. Default targets (R-13 in zones 1–2, R-20 in zones 3–4, R-25 in zones 5–6, R-30 in zones 7–8) are rule-of-thumb figures based on the wall insulation ranges in the IECC (R402.1) and IRC Chapter 11 (N1102), set on the cautious side. Foam plastics also fall under IRC R316 thermal-barrier rules. Code editions and local amendments vary, so confirm your own requirements.

Worked Example

A 30 × 30 ft house with 2×10 joists at 16 in on center, sill plate covered, climate zone 4 (target R-20), 10% waste and 5 sq ft of openings:

  • Perimeter: 2 × (30 + 30) = 120 ft
  • Covered height: 9.25 + 1.5 = 10.75 in
  • Gross area: 120 × 10.75 ÷ 12 = 107.5 sq ft; minus 5 = 102.5 sq ft net
  • Order area: 102.5 × 1.10 = 112.75 sq ft
  • Closed-cell spray foam: 20 ÷ 6.5 = 3.08 in, rounded up to 3.25 in, which gives R-21.1. Quantity: 112.75 × 3.25 ≈ 367 board feet, or one 600 bd ft kit.
  • XPS foam board: 20 ÷ 5.0 = 4.0 in, which is two layers (for example 2 in + 2 in). Quantity: ceil(112.75 ÷ 32) = 4 sheets per layer, so 8 sheets in total.
  • Joist cavities: 120 × 12 ÷ 16 = 90 cavities to cut and seal.

Tips & Common Mistakes

  • Air-seal first, insulate second. Most heat loss at the rim is air leakage. Caulk or foam every joint between sill plate, rim board and subfloor.
  • Don't rely on fiberglass alone. Loose batts let air and moisture reach the cold rim board. Use air-sealed foam, or foam board with batts behind it.
  • Check thermal-barrier and fireblocking rules. Exposed foam plastics generally need a thermal barrier such as ½ in drywall (IRC R316), though exceptions exist for some rim areas. Ask your building department.
  • Measure the real perimeter. Stairs, bump-outs and porches add linear feet. Walk the basement with a tape rather than assuming a rectangle.
  • Mind termite inspection gaps in termite-prone regions. Some jurisdictions require a visible strip or an approved foam product.
  • Spray foam safety: wear a respirator, eye protection and gloves, ventilate, and apply in lifts per the manufacturer's data sheet.

Frequently Asked Questions

What R-value do I need for a rim joist?

Most codes treat the rim joist like an exterior wall, so a common range is R-13 in warm climates up to R-20 or more in cold ones. Check your local code, since it may specify combinations such as R-13 cavity plus R-5 continuous.

How thick should spray foam be on a rim joist?

Closed-cell foam gives about R-6.5 per inch, so R-20 needs roughly 3 to 3.25 in. Many installers apply 2 in for air sealing and add more up to the target. Follow the manufacturer's maximum lift thickness.

Can I use fiberglass batts in a rim joist?

You can, but it performs poorly on its own. Fiberglass doesn't stop air movement, and moist air can condense on the cold rim board behind it. If you use batts, seal the rim with caulk or foam first, or add a layer of rigid foam.

Is rigid foam board or spray foam better for rim joists?

Spray foam seals irregular gaps in one step and costs more per board foot. Cut-and-cobble foam board is cheaper and DIY-friendly, but every piece has to be sealed at the edges with can foam or caulk.

Will insulating my rim joist cause moisture problems?

Done well, it reduces moisture because the rim board stays warmer and air can't carry vapor into the cavity. Fix any bulk water leaks first, and use foam or another material that blocks air and vapor against the wood.

How do I measure the rim joist perimeter?

Measure the inside of the foundation wall along every straight run and add them up. For a rectangle, use 2 × (length + width). Subtract wall sections with no rim access, such as a garage firewall, using the openings field.
