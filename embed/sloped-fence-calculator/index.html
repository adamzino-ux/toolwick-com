Why This Matters

On flat ground a fence is easy to plan: divide the run by the panel width, round up, and add one post. On a hill the numbers change. A 40-foot run that falls 30 inches needs five panels if you step them, but if you rack them parallel to the ground the longer sloped distance can push you to six. Miss that and you are a panel short on Saturday afternoon with the auger rental due back.

Slope also changes post length. With stepped panels each post must reach the top of the higher panel, so on a 6-inch step a 6-foot fence needs posts about 6 inches taller than on level ground, and deeper holes to match. Homeowners use a fence on a slope calculator to order lumber and concrete once, contractors use it to quote jobs, and anyone fencing a pool, a dog run, or a paddock uses it to check the gap under each panel. Gaps over roughly 4 inches let small pets and children through, so the step drop is shown in plain numbers.

How It's Calculated

Everything starts from the run and rise you enter. The tool uses these formulas:

  • Slope length = √(run² + rise²). Slope % = rise ÷ run × 100. Angle = arctan(rise ÷ run).
  • Sections: stepped = ⌈run ÷ max width⌉; racked = ⌈slope length ÷ max width⌉. Posts = sections + 1.
  • Step drop (stepped) = rise ÷ sections. The gap under the downhill end of a panel is the step drop plus 2 in of ground clearance.
  • Post height above ground: stepped = fence height + 2 in + step drop (the post must reach the higher panel); racked = fence height + 2 in.
  • Embed depth = the largest of 24 in, half the above-ground height (so at least one-third of the post is buried), and your frost depth, rounded up to the next 6 in.
  • Post length = above-ground height + embed, rounded up to the next 6 in, then matched to the next stock length (6, 7, 8, 9, 10 or 12 ft).
  • Rails: 2 per section up to 48 in tall, 3 above that, multiplied by section length and rounded up to whole feet.
  • Concrete per hole = (π × (hole diameter ÷ 2)² − post width²) × embed, plus 10% waste. A 60 lb bag is taken as 0.45 cu ft and an 80 lb bag as 0.60 cu ft.

These follow common industry rules of thumb, not a specific code section. No national building code sets fence post depth. Frost depth, permits, height limits, and setbacks vary by city, county, and HOA, so confirm them locally. Every safety-related figure is rounded toward more posts, deeper holes, and longer posts. The results are planning estimates, not engineering, permit, or inspection approval.

Worked Example

Say a fence run is 40 ft long and drops 30 in. You want a 72 in fence with 8 ft sections, stepped, on 4×4 posts, and you left frost depth blank.

  • Slope: 30 ÷ 480 = 6.25%, about 3.6°, or 0.75 in per foot. Slope length = √(40² + 2.5²) = 40.08 ft.
  • Sections: ⌈40 ÷ 8⌉ = 5 sections and 6 posts. Step drop = 30 ÷ 5 = 6 in, so the gap under the downhill end of a panel is up to 8 in. The tool flags that.
  • Post height: 72 + 2 + 6 = 80 in above ground for the tallest post. Embed = half of 80 = 40 in, rounded up to 42 in. Longest post = 80 + 42 = 122 in, rounded to 126 in (10 ft 6 in), so order 12 ft stock.
  • Rails: 3 rails × 5 sections × 8 ft = 120 ft.
  • Concrete: a 12 in hole with a 3.5 in post at 42 in deep is about 2.45 cu ft per hole. Six holes is 14.71 cu ft, or 16.18 cu ft with 10% waste. That is 36 bags of 60 lb or 27 bags of 80 lb.

Switch to racked and the 40.08 ft slope length divided by 8 ft is just over 5, so you need 6 sections and 7 posts. Posts are shorter (10 ft stock) and there is no stepped gap, but you need 121 ft of rail and 42 bags of 60 lb concrete.

Tips & Common Mistakes

  • Measure the run horizontally, not along the ground. Pull a string level between two stakes and measure the rise with a tape at the low end.
  • Check the racking limit. Many pre-built panels, especially vinyl and aluminum, only rack about 1.5 in per foot (around 12.5%). Wood rails you cut yourself can follow steeper ground.
  • Set gate and corner posts deeper. End, corner, and gate posts carry more load than line posts. Add 6 in or more and use a larger post.
  • Dig 4 in deeper for gravel. A gravel base under the post drains water away and slows rot. The tool's dig depth includes it.
  • Order extra. Cut waste, a bent rail, or one bad post is normal. Buy a spare post and a few extra feet of rail.
  • Check permits first. Many places require one above 6 ft, and some limit front-yard fences to 4 ft. Call utility locators before you dig.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do you build a fence on a slope?

You either step the panels, keeping each one level and dropping at each post, or rack them so the rails follow the ground. Stepped suits steeper ground and rigid panels. Racked gives a continuous line on gentle slopes. Set posts plumb either way, and measure post spacing horizontally.

Is a stepped or racked fence better on a hill?

Racked looks smoother and leaves no big gaps, but it needs panels that can flex and it uses more material. Stepped works with any panel and is often easier to build, but leaves triangular gaps under each step. On slopes over about 12.5%, stepped is often the practical choice.

How much slope can racked fence panels handle?

It depends on the product. Many vinyl and aluminum panels rack roughly 1.5 inches per foot or less. Custom wood rails can follow steeper ground. Check the manufacturer's racking limit before you buy.

How deep should fence posts be on a slope?

A common rule is to bury at least one-third of the post length, never less than 24 inches, and below the local frost line. On a slope, size the embed from the tallest post, because stepped fences need taller posts. This calculator rounds that depth up to the next 6 inches.

Do I measure fence length along the ground or horizontally?

Measure the run horizontally for stepped fences, since each panel stays level. For racked fences, panel length is measured along the slope, which is slightly longer. This tool uses the run for stepped and the slope length for racked.

Do I need a permit for a fence on a slope?

Permit rules depend on your city, county, and HOA, and mostly cover height and setbacks. Slopes can also count toward the measured height of a fence, especially at the downhill side. Ask your local building department before you buy materials.
