Why This Matters

Scaffolding is one of the easiest line items to get wrong. Order too little and crews stand idle while a truck makes a second trip. Order too much and you pay rent on steel that never leaves the pile. A 40 ft by 20 ft wall in frame scaffolding is 8 bays wide and 4 tiers high. That is 72 frames (a front and a rear line), 64 cross braces and 96 planks. First-time estimates are often off by half in one direction or the other, usually because they forget the second frame line.

Time matters as much as parts. The same job needs about 80 person-hours to erect and dismantle. At $45 an hour that is $3,600, several times the $896 of rental on a 14-day hire, so labor often decides the budget. Painters, siding crews, masons, roofers and homeowners hiring out a two-story repaint all need a fast figure before calling a rental yard. Having quantities in hand also lets you compare quotes line by line instead of accepting one lump sum.

The Purchase tab answers the follow-up question: if you will build this kind of scaffold several times a year, does owning make sense?

How It's Calculated

Every count is rounded up, never down, because a short scaffold is a safety problem and a long one is only a cost problem. Layout rules follow common OSHA 29 CFR 1926.451 practice. Local codes vary, so treat the output as a starting point for a conversation with your supplier.

Bays = ceil(length ÷ bay width)
Tiers = ceil(platform height ÷ lift height)
Built height = tiers × lift height
Bay-levels = bays × tiers
Frames = (bays + 1) × 2 lines × tiers
Cross braces = bay-levels × 2
Planked levels = all tiers, the top 2, or the top 1 (your choice)
Planks = bays × planked levels × planks per bay (3 for frame, 4 for others)
Base plates = (bays + 1) × 4 (two lines, two legs per frame)
Guardrail levels = planked levels (or only those with platforms over 10 ft)
Guardrail sets = (bays × 2 + 2) × guardrail levels (front, rear, both ends)
Tie rows = 0 if built height ≤ 4 × base width, otherwise max(1, ceil((built height − 8 × base width) ÷ 20) + 1)
Wall ties = tie rows × (ceil(length ÷ 30) + 1)
Rental = bay-levels × daily rate × days
Labor = person-hours × hourly rate (not divided by crew)

Bay width is 5 ft for frame and tube and clamp, and 6 ft for ringlock and modular. Lift height is 6 ft for frame and 6.5 ft for the others. Base width, used for the 4:1 tie rule, is assumed to be 5 ft for frame, 4 ft for tube and clamp, and 3 ft for ringlock and modular. Ties use the tighter 20 ft vertical and 30 ft horizontal grid OSHA sets for narrow scaffolds, for every type, which counts more ties rather than fewer.

Labor uses person-hours per bay-level: 1.5 to erect and 1.0 to dismantle for frame, and 2.0–3.0 and 1.3–2.0 for the other systems. Crew size only converts those hours into working days (hours ÷ crew ÷ 8). The damage waiver is 4% of rental. Rent versus buy break-even is the purchase total divided by the cost of one rental (rent, waiver and delivery). Labor is left out of both sides because you pay it either way.

Worked Example

A wall is 40 ft long with a top platform at 20 ft. Use frame scaffolding, every level planked, rails on every planked level, 14 days at $2 per bay-level per day, $45 per hour labor, a crew of 2 and $150 delivery.

  1. Bays = ceil(40 ÷ 5) = 8. Tiers = ceil(20 ÷ 6) = 4, so the built height is 24 ft. Bay-levels = 8 × 4 = 32.
  2. Frames = 9 × 2 × 4 = 72. Braces = 32 × 2 = 64. Planks = 8 × 4 × 3 = 96. Base plates = 9 × 4 = 36. Guardrail sets = (8 × 2 + 2) × 4 = 72.
  3. Wall ties: 24 ft is more than 4 × 5 ft = 20 ft, so ties are needed. Rows = max(1, ceil((24 − 40) ÷ 20) + 1) = 1. Per row = ceil(40 ÷ 30) + 1 = 3. Total = 3.
  4. Rental = 32 × $2 × 14 = $896.00.
  5. Labor: erect 32 × 1.5 = 48 hours and dismantle 32 × 1.0 = 32 hours. That is 80 person-hours × $45 = $3,600.00.
  6. Damage waiver = 4% × $896 = $35.84. Delivery = $150.00.
  7. Total = $896.00 + $3,600.00 + $35.84 + $150.00 = $4,681.84. A crew of 2 needs 80 ÷ 2 ÷ 8 = 5 working days.

Buying the same wall at the default prices costs 72 × $68 + 64 × $22 + 96 × $38 + 36 × $14 + 72 × $26 + 3 × $12 = $12,364.00. With 10% tax and markup the total is $13,600.40. One rental before labor is $896.00 + $35.84 + $150.00 = $1,081.84, so break-even is $13,600.40 ÷ $1,081.84 ≈ 12.6 jobs.

Tips & Common Mistakes

  • Measure each face separately. Corners and returns need their own bays, so wrapping a house is several runs, not one long one.
  • Enter the platform height, not the roofline. You stand on the platform, and the top rail sits about 3.5 ft above it.
  • Plank only what you will use. If you work from the top two levels, "Top two levels only" cuts plank count and cost. Never stand on an unplanked level.
  • Do not strip guardrails or ties to save money. OSHA requires rails on platforms over 10 ft, and many sites and local rules require them lower. The safer default covers every planked level.
  • Check ground conditions. Soft or sloped ground needs sills and screw jacks, and loaded legs should never sit on loose fill.
  • Add a rental buffer. Erection and dismantling eat into your rental days, and weather delays are normal. Extra days are cheaper booked up front.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much scaffolding do I need for a two-story house?

For a wall 40 ft long with a 20 ft top platform in frame scaffolding, expect 8 bays and 4 tiers. With full planking that is 72 frames, 64 cross braces, 96 planks, 36 base plates and 3 wall ties. Repeat the calculation for every side of the house you need to reach.

Is it cheaper to rent or buy scaffolding?

For occasional jobs, renting wins. In the example above one rental (rent, waiver and delivery) is $1,081.84 before labor, while buying is $13,600.40, which is about 12.6 jobs of that size. Labor is excluded from both sides because you pay it either way, and storage, inspection and resale value are not counted.

Does a bigger crew lower the labor cost?

No. Labor cost is person-hours times the hourly rate, so adding workers finishes the job sooner but does not change the total hours. This tool shows the working days a crew of your size needs.

What's the difference between frame and tube and clamp scaffolding?

Frame scaffolding uses pre-welded H-frames and goes up quickly, which suits straight walls on houses and light commercial work. Tube and clamp adapts to curves and odd shapes but takes roughly twice the labor per bay.

Do I need a permit or an engineer?

Many cities require a permit for scaffolds over a certain height or next to sidewalks, so check locally. OSHA requires a registered professional engineer to design scaffolds above 125 ft, and this tool does not accept heights above that.

How accurate are these estimates?

They are planning numbers, not quotes. Rental rates, access, package pricing and the mix of parts vary by region and supplier, and real quotes often differ by 25% or more. Collect two quotes and compare them against the parts list here.
