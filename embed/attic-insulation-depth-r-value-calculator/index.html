Why This Matters

The attic is usually the cheapest place to improve a home's energy performance. Heat rises, and a poorly insulated attic floor can account for a large share of winter heat loss and summer cooling load. Many homes built before 1990 have only R-11 to R-19, which is 4 to 6 inches of fiberglass. Current code for a cold climate such as Chicago is R-49 to R-60, so those houses are often short by 30 to 40 R-value points.

Say you own a 1,200 sq ft ranch in Denver with 6 inches of old blown fiberglass. That is roughly R-15 against a code target of R-60. Adding about 14 to 15 inches of cellulose closes the gap, and it usually costs a small fraction of a window replacement while saving more energy. The calculation also helps you avoid under-ordering, which stalls a weekend job, and over-ordering, which wastes money. Knowing the final depth also tells you whether the insulation will bury the joists, which matters for storage, walkways and the clearance around vents and flues.

How It's Calculated

R-value adds up in layers, so the math is simple:

Existing R = existing depth × R-per-inch × condition factor (1.0, or 0.75 if compressed)
R needed = target R − existing R (never below 0)
Depth to add = R needed ÷ new R-per-inch, rounded up to the next 0.5 in
Final R = existing R + depth added × new R-per-inch
Volume = area × depth ÷ 12, plus 10% for waste

The R-per-inch values are deliberately conservative, settled figures: fiberglass batt 3.1, blown fiberglass 2.5, cellulose 3.2, mineral wool 3.0, open-cell foam 3.7 and closed-cell foam 6.0. Bag charts often list a higher initial thickness for loose-fill, so check the chart on the bag for the installed depth to blow. Targets come from the IECC ceiling R-value tables: Table R402.1.3 in the 2021 edition and Table R402.1.2 in the 2018 and 2015 editions. Local codes vary, so confirm what your jurisdiction has adopted. This is an estimate, not an engineering, permit or inspection approval.

Worked Example

A home in Zone 5 (IECC 2021 target R-60) has 1,200 sq ft of attic floor, 2x6 joists and 6 inches of existing loose-fill fiberglass. The owner will add blown cellulose.

  1. Existing R = 6 in × 2.5 = R-15.0
  2. R needed = 60 − 15 = R-45.0
  3. Depth = 45 ÷ 3.2 = 14.06 in, rounded up to 14.5 in
  4. Final R = 15 + 14.5 × 3.2 = R-61.4
  5. Total depth = 6 + 14.5 = 20.5 in, which is 15 in above the top of 2x6 joists
  6. Volume = 1,200 × 14.5 ÷ 12 = 1,450 cu ft, or about 1,595 cu ft with 10% waste

Tips & Common Mistakes

  • Install baffles at the eaves. Insulation that blocks soffit vents traps moisture and causes mold and ice dams. Use rafter baffles so air can still flow.
  • Air seal first. Caulk or foam gaps around wires, pipes, duct boots and the attic hatch before adding insulation. Air leaks can waste more heat than thin insulation does.
  • Respect clearances. Keep insulation away from flues, chimneys and non-IC-rated recessed lights as the manufacturer and code require. Use IC-rated covers or barriers.
  • Don't cover problems. Wet, moldy or pest-damaged insulation should be removed, not buried. Have an electrician evaluate any active knob-and-tube wiring first, and never disturb vermiculite until it has been tested for asbestos.
  • Don't compress batts. Squashing batts, or laying them over a storage platform, cuts their R-value. Lay a second layer of batts perpendicular to the joists to cover the framing.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much insulation do I need in my attic?

It depends on your climate zone. IECC 2021 calls for R-30 in Zone 1, R-49 in Zones 2 and 3, and R-60 in Zone 4 and colder. That is roughly 15 to 20 inches of blown cellulose or fiberglass in most of the country.

How many inches is R-38 or R-49 insulation?

R-38 is about 12 inches of cellulose or 15 inches of loose-fill fiberglass. R-49 is about 15.5 inches of cellulose or 20 inches of loose-fill fiberglass. Batts and mineral wool fall in between.

Can I add new insulation on top of old insulation?

Yes, as long as the old material is dry, clean and free of mold, and no vapor barrier is trapped between the layers. Unfaced batts or blown-in insulation can go over it. Add the R-values together to find your total.

Is it worth upgrading from R-30 to R-60?

The extra savings are smaller than going from R-11 to R-30, but they are still real in cold climates. Insulation is cheap compared with equipment replacement, and the added comfort and moisture control often justify it. Use the cost calculator linked below to compare.

Does blown-in insulation settle over time?

Yes. Cellulose can settle 10 to 20 percent, and loose-fill fiberglass settles somewhat too. This calculator uses settled R-per-inch values and rounds depth up, so the finished R-value should hold. Some bag charts show a deeper initial thickness to allow for this.

Should insulation cover the tops of the joists?

For cold climates, usually yes. Covering the joists reduces heat loss through the wood, and the calculator shows how far your total depth sits above or below the joists. Leave walkways or raised platforms only where you need access.
