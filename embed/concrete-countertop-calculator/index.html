Why This Matters

A concrete countertop has to be poured in one continuous session. If you run out of mix halfway through, the second batch will not bond cleanly to the first, and you end up with a cold joint that shows up as a crack or a visible line. Ordering one bag too many costs about $25 to $30. Ordering one too few can ruin a slab you spent two days forming and reinforcing.

Weight matters just as much. A typical 96 × 25 in kitchen run at 1.5 in thick, with a sink cutout and a 4 in backsplash, holds about 2 cubic feet of concrete and weighs roughly 300 lb. That is more than most people can safely lift, and it is a real load for standard cabinets. Knowing the number before you build lets you decide whether to split the top into sections, strengthen the base cabinets, or cast the pieces upside down in a shop and set them with help.

Homeowners, remodelers, and woodworkers who build their own forms use this kind of estimate to budget the job, to plan how many mixing batches fit in a barrel or mixer, and to decide how many people to line up on install day.

How It's Calculated

The calculator works in cubic inches, then converts. For each section it takes the top surface area, subtracts the cutouts, and multiplies by thickness. The backsplash is added as a second slab the same thickness as the top.

Slab = (Length × Width − Cutouts × CutL × CutW) × Thickness
Backsplash = Length × Backsplash height × Thickness
Net volume (cu ft) = (Slab + Backsplash) × Sections ÷ 1,728
Mix volume = Net volume × (1 + Waste %)
Bags = Mix volume ÷ Bag yield, rounded up
Weight (lb) = Net volume × 150 lb/cu ft, rounded up to 5 lb

Bag yields follow typical manufacturer label values: about 0.30 cu ft for a 40 lb bag, 0.375 for 50 lb, 0.45 for 60 lb and 0.60 for 80 lb. Specialty countertop mixes can differ slightly, so check the yield printed on your bag. The 150 lb/cu ft density is a common figure for normal-weight concrete; real slabs range from about 145 to 155 lb/cu ft and reinforcement adds a little, so treat weight as an estimate and round up for planning.

No building code such as the IRC or IBC sets countertop thickness, mix or weight. This tool uses common rule-of-thumb guidance (roughly 1.5 to 2 in thickness for reinforced cast concrete) and rounds every result toward the safe side: more bags, more weight. Local codes and cabinet manufacturer limits vary, and the output is not engineering, permit or inspection approval.

Worked Example

Suppose you are casting one 96 in × 25 in countertop, 1.5 in thick, with a 4 in backsplash and one 30 × 16 in sink cutout, using 80 lb bags (0.60 cu ft each) and 10% waste.

  1. Top area after cutout: 96 × 25 = 2,400 in², minus 30 × 16 = 480 in², leaves 1,920 in² (13.33 sq ft).
  2. Slab volume: 1,920 × 1.5 = 2,880 in³.
  3. Backsplash volume: 96 × 4 × 1.5 = 576 in³.
  4. Net volume: 3,456 in³ ÷ 1,728 = 2.00 cu ft.
  5. With 10% waste: 2.00 × 1.10 = 2.20 cu ft to mix.
  6. Bags: 2.20 ÷ 0.60 = 3.67, rounded up to 4 bags. At $28 a bag that is about $112.
  7. Weight: 2.00 × 150 = 300 lb, or 18.8 lb per sq ft of top surface.

At 300 lb in a single piece, the notes in the results will recommend four or more people or lifting equipment, or splitting the top into two sections with a seam.

Tips & Common Mistakes

  • Do not skip the waste allowance. Concrete sticks to buckets, mixers and wheelbarrows, and forms are rarely perfectly level. Ten percent is a sensible minimum, and 15% suits complex shapes or first-time pours.
  • Do not go thinner than 1.5 in without a plan. Thin reinforced concrete is brittle. Counters thinner than that usually use GFRC or fiber-reinforced mixes and experienced finishers.
  • Watch the rim around cutouts. Less than about 3 in of concrete beside a sink is a common crack point. Add rebar or wire mesh at the cutout corners and avoid sharp inside corners.
  • Support overhangs. As a rule of thumb, overhangs beyond about 10 in need brackets or corbels. The calculator does not check structural capacity.
  • Check the cabinets and floor. A 1.5 in top weighs about 19 lb per sq ft. Confirm the cabinet frames, shims and floor can carry it, and ask a qualified professional if you are unsure.

Frequently Asked Questions

How many bags of concrete do I need for a countertop?

Multiply the finished length, width and thickness to get volume, add backsplashes, and divide by your bag yield after adding waste. An 8-foot, 1.5 in countertop with a backsplash and sink cutout typically needs about 4 bags of 80 lb mix. Enter your own numbers above for an exact count.

How thick should a concrete countertop be?

Reinforced cast-in-place or precast countertops are commonly 1.5 to 2 in thick. GFRC tops can be about 0.75 to 1 in but use special mixes and glass fibers. Thicker tops resist cracking better but weigh more and need stronger support.

How much does a concrete countertop weigh?

Normal-weight concrete is about 150 lb per cubic foot, so each inch of thickness adds roughly 12.5 lb per square foot. A 1.5 in countertop is about 19 lb per square foot, and a 2 in countertop is about 25 lb per square foot.

Should I subtract the sink cutout from the concrete volume?

Yes, a cutout is void space, so it does not use concrete. This calculator subtracts it, then adds your waste allowance so you still buy a safety margin. Increase the waste slider if your cutout forms are complex.

Can I pour a concrete countertop in one piece?

You can, but long or heavy single pieces are hard to move and crack more easily. Sections over about 10 feet or 250 lb are commonly split with a seam placed at a sink or corner. Plan seams before building the forms.

Why does the calculator round bags up?

You can only buy whole bags, and running short in the middle of a pour causes cold joints. Rounding up, plus a waste allowance, protects the pour. The leftover mix is cheap insurance.
