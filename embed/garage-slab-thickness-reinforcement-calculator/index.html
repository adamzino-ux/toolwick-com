Why This Matters

A garage slab is the one part of a house you cannot easily fix later. Breaking out and replacing a 24×24 ft slab typically costs several thousand dollars once demolition, hauling and a new pour are added. Going from 4 to 5 inches at the start adds only about 1.8 cubic yards on that slab, roughly $270–$360 at $150–$200 per delivered yard.

Vehicle weight matters less than most people think. A sedan weighs 3,000–3,500 lbs, a midsize SUV 4,000–5,000 lbs, and a full-size SUV or half-ton pickup 5,000–6,500 lbs. Each tire carries only about 1,000–2,000 lbs, which a 4-inch slab on a well-compacted base handles easily. Heavy-duty pickups at 7,000–8,500 lbs, loaded trailers, boats, RVs and car lifts concentrate far more load and justify thicker slabs.

Most failed garage floors fail from the ground up. Soft or expansive clay, a thin or poorly compacted gravel base, and water getting under the slab cause settling and cracking long before the concrete itself is overloaded. Climate also decides the mix: in Minnesota, severe freeze-thaw and road salt call for air-entrained concrete near 4,500 psi, while the same garage in Phoenix needs far less. This calculator turns those variables into a clear starting specification you can take to your contractor or engineer.

How It's Calculated

This is a rule-of-thumb planning tool, not a structural slab design. It does not run a PCA or ACI 360R wheel-load stress analysis and it does not use a subgrade modulus (k-value). It combines common residential practice with a few code references:

  1. Thickness. Starts from a base by vehicle weight class: 4″ for cars and for SUVs and half-ton pickups, 5″ for heavy-duty pickups and trailers, and 6″ for RVs and equipment. Poor soil adds 1″. The result is never below 4″, which is above the 3.5″ minimum for concrete floors on ground in IRC R506.
  2. Concrete strength. The higher of a load minimum (3,000 to 4,000 psi) and an exposure minimum. For garage slabs, IRC Table R402.2 lists about 3,500 psi for moderate and 4,500 psi for severe weathering. Editions and local amendments differ, so check yours.
  3. Sub-base depth. 4″ on good or excellent soil, 6″ on fair soil, in severe freeze-thaw, or under heavy loads, and 8″ on poor soil. Volume is ordered 25% over to allow for compaction.
  4. Reinforcement. Uses the ACI 318 temperature and shrinkage steel ratio of 0.0018 as a benchmark. Spacing is solved so each bar size meets it, rounded down to a standard spacing (6, 8, 9, 10, 12, 15 or 18″) and capped at 18″ (12″ for heavy loads).
  5. Control joints. Spaced at 24 times the thickness in inches (2 ft per inch of slab), capped at 12 ft. ACI guidance allows up to 36 times, so this is the conservative end.
Steel ratio ρ = bar area ÷ (spacing × slab thickness)
Maximum spacing = bar area ÷ (0.0018 × thickness)
Volume (cu yd) = length × width × (thickness ÷ 12) ÷ 27

Welded wire mesh cannot reach this benchmark in common sizes: 6×6 W1.4 gives ρ of about 0.0006 and W2.9 about 0.0012 at 4″. The tool still lets you select mesh, but it flags the shortfall. Many jurisdictions accept #3 bars at 18″ (ρ about 0.0015) in light garages, so the 0.0018 benchmark is deliberately conservative.

Worked Example

A 24×24 ft garage (576 sq ft) for SUVs and a half-ton pickup, on good sandy soil, with seasonal freeze-thaw, and reinforcement set to Auto:

  1. Thickness: 4″ base for the medium class + 0″ for good soil = 4″.
  2. Strength: the higher of 3,500 psi (load) and 3,500 psi (moderate exposure) = 3,500 psi, air-entrained at 5–6%.
  3. Rebar: #3 has an area of 0.11 in². Maximum spacing = 0.11 ÷ (0.0018 × 4) = 15.3″, so use 15″. ρ = 0.11 ÷ (15 × 4) = 0.00183, which meets 0.0018.
  4. Concrete: 576 × 4 ÷ 12 = 192 cu ft = 7.11 cu yd. Add 10% for 7.82, rounded up to a ¼-yard order of 8.00 cu yd.
  5. Rebar quantity: bars at 15″ with 3″ end clearance need 20 per direction. 20 × 24 ft × 2 = 960 linear ft, plus 10% for laps and waste = 1,056 ft, or 53 twenty-foot sticks.
  6. Sub-base: 4″ deep = 7.11 cu yd compacted; order 25% more = 8.89 cu yd.
  7. Joints: a maximum spacing of 8 ft gives 2 joints each way, 4 in total.

Going to 5″ would raise the order to 10.00 cu yd, about 2 cu yd more, and tighten the #3 spacing to 12″.

Tips & Common Mistakes

  • Prepare the base first. A 4″ slab over 4–6″ of compacted ¾″ crushed stone beats a 6″ slab poured on soft clay. Compact in lifts and keep it uniform.
  • Lift the steel. Mesh or rebar lying on the ground does almost nothing. Support it on chairs at mid-depth and tie or lap it as specified.
  • Cut joints early. Saw or tool control joints about ¼ of the slab depth within 6–18 hours, keeping panels roughly square.
  • Thicken the edges. Garage slabs are often poured monolithically with a thickened perimeter or footing. Frost depth and footing rules are local, so ask your building department.
  • Cure and wait. Keep the slab damp or sealed with curing compound. Wait about 7 days for cars and 28 days for heavy trucks or equipment, and never add water to the truck to make placing easier.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is a 4-inch slab thick enough for a garage?

For cars, SUVs and half-ton pickups on a well-compacted base and good soil, 4 inches of reinforced concrete is the usual residential standard. Heavy-duty trucks, trailers, lifts and poor soil call for 5 inches or more, and a sound base matters as much as thickness.

Do I need rebar, or is wire mesh enough?

Common 6×6 W1.4 mesh provides about a third of the 0.0018 steel ratio, so it mainly limits shrinkage cracks. Rebar such as #3 at 15″ in a 4″ slab meets the benchmark, so choose rebar for heavier loads, weaker soil or freeze-thaw. Either must sit at mid-depth, not on the ground.

What psi concrete should I order for a garage floor?

IRC Table R402.2 lists roughly 3,500 psi for moderate and 4,500 psi for severe weathering on garage slabs, with air entrainment in freezing climates. Your weathering region and local amendments decide the exact figure, so tell your ready-mix supplier where the slab is and ask what they recommend.

How thick should the gravel base be?

Plan on at least 4 inches of compacted crushed stone, and 6 to 8 inches over fair or poor soil, in severe freeze-thaw, or under heavy loads. A deeper base improves drainage and spreads load, but it does not cure expansive clay on its own.

How long before I can park on a new slab?

Light passenger vehicles can usually park after about 7 days of curing in mild weather. Wait roughly 28 days for heavy trucks, RVs or equipment, and longer in cold weather when the concrete gains strength more slowly.

Do I need a permit or an engineer for a garage slab?

Most jurisdictions require a permit for a new garage slab, and inspectors usually check the base, steel and forms before the pour. Slabs for RVs, box trucks, lifts, or poor or expansive soil should be designed by an engineer, and this calculator is not a substitute for that.
