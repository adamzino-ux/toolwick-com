How to Use This Fillet Weld Size Calculator

Pick your unit system, design method, electrode and base metal, then enter the load the joint must carry, the thickness of the thinner plate, the length of each weld run and how many runs share the load. Set the slider to the angle between the load and the weld axis. The calculator returns the smallest standard leg size that passes strength, minimum-size and maximum-size checks, plus a comparison table of common sizes.

Why This Matters

Fillet welds are the most common weld in structural steel, trailer frames, machine bases, brackets, tabs and gussets, and picking the leg size is a daily decision for fabricators. Too small and the weld can fail in shear well below the load you expect. Too large and you burn extra filler metal, add heat and distortion, and waste labor, because weld volume grows with the square of the leg size. Going from 1/4 in to 3/8 in more than doubles the weld metal for a 50% increase in leg.

Typical cases: a welder building a 20,000 lb hoist bracket, a farmer adding a hitch plate to a skid steer, a fabricator checking whether a 3/16 in weld is enough on a 3/8 in gusset, or an inspector sanity-checking a detail. In each case you want a fast, defensible answer based on a published method instead of a guess or a rule of thumb like "weld size equals plate thickness." That rule is often wrong in both directions. This tool gives you a conservative starting size you can then confirm with the drawings, the welding procedure and a qualified engineer.

How It's Calculated

The calculator uses the simplified method in AISC 360 (Specification for Structural Steel Buildings), Chapter J, Section J2.4, with minimum and maximum sizes from Table J2.4 and Section J2.2b. The nominal strength of a linear fillet weld group is:

Rn = 0.60 · FEXX · (0.707 · w) · L · (1 + 0.5 · sin1.5θ)

Here FEXX is the electrode classification strength, w is the leg size, 0.707·w is the effective throat of an equal-leg weld, L is the total length of all runs, and θ is the angle between the load and the weld axis. Allowable strength is Rn/Ω with Ω = 2.00 (ASD), or design strength is φRn with φ = 0.75 (LRFD).

As a conservative extra check, the calculator also limits the shear on the base metal fusion face to 0.60·Fu·w·L, without the directional bonus. The smaller of the two strengths governs. The required leg is then solved directly:

w(required) = Load ÷ [ (1/Ω or φ) · governing stress · L(total) ]

The result is rounded up to the next 1/16 in (US) or the next whole millimeter (metric) and raised to the minimum size if needed. The minimum is 1/8 in for plate up to 1/4 in, 3/16 in up to 1/2 in, 1/4 in up to 3/4 in and 5/16 in above that. The maximum along a plate edge is the plate thickness for material under 1/4 in, or 1/16 in less for thicker plate. Metric minimums are the inch values rounded up to whole millimeters.

This is a simplified, single-weld-group check. It does not cover eccentric loads, fatigue, seismic detailing, weld groups with mixed directions, or code editions other than AISC 360. Local codes and AWS D1.1 may differ, so always have critical connections reviewed by a licensed engineer.

Worked Example

Take a bracket in 3/8 in A36 plate with E70XX electrodes, an ASD service load of 20,000 lbf parallel to the weld (0°), and two welds each 4 in long.

  • Total weld length = 2 × 4 = 8 in. Angle factor = 1 + 0.5·sin1.5(0°) = 1.000.
  • Weld metal stress = 0.60 × 70 × 0.707 × 1.000 = 29.70 ksi. Base metal stress = 0.60 × 58 = 34.8 ksi. Weld metal governs.
  • Allowable stress = 29.70 ÷ 2.00 = 14.85 ksi.
  • Required leg = 20 kip ÷ (14.85 ksi × 8 in) = 0.168 in, which rounds up to 3/16 in (0.1875 in).
  • Minimum size for 3/8 in plate = 3/16 in. Maximum = 3/8 − 1/16 = 5/16 in. The 3/16 in size fits between them.
  • Allowable strength at 3/16 in = 14.85 × 0.1875 × 8 ≈ 22.27 kip, so utilization is about 90%.

A 1/8 in weld would carry only about 14,850 lbf and fails, which is why the comparison table flags it as "Too small." Weld length is 4 in, more than 4 times the leg (0.75 in), so the length rule is satisfied.

Tips & Common Mistakes

  • Don't confuse leg with throat. Weld size callouts on drawings are the leg. The throat is only 0.707 times the leg, and strength depends on the throat.
  • Count only runs that actually carry the load. A weld that is hidden, short or poorly accessible should not be counted. When in doubt, use fewer runs.
  • Match the load basis to the method. Enter a service load for ASD and a factored load for LRFD. Mixing them gives an answer that is 40–60% off.
  • Keep length at least 4 times the leg size. Very short welds are not effective, and long end-loaded welds over 100 times the leg lose efficiency.
  • Don't forget fit-up and penetration. Strength figures assume sound welds with full fusion at the root. Dirty steel, gaps and undercut reduce real capacity.
  • Don't use it for lifting, fatigue or safety-critical gear without engineering review and the applicable standard.

Frequently Asked Questions

What size fillet weld do I need for 1/4 inch plate?

AISC sets a minimum of 1/8 in for plate up to 1/4 in thick, and the maximum along an edge of 1/4 in plate is 3/16 in. The right size within that range depends on load and weld length, so run your numbers through the calculator instead of defaulting to 3/16 in.

Is the fillet weld size the leg or the throat?

Size is the leg, measured along the plate faces from the root to each toe. The effective throat is the shortest distance from the root to the face and equals about 0.707 times the leg for an equal-leg, flat-face weld.

Why does the calculator round the weld size up?

Welds are specified in standard increments, and a smaller weld would have less capacity than your load requires. Rounding up to the next 1/16 in (or 1 mm) keeps the result on the safe side of the calculated requirement.

Does a transverse weld get stronger than a longitudinal weld?

AISC allows up to a 1.5 times increase in weld metal strength when the load is perpendicular to the weld. This calculator also checks base metal shear on the fusion face at 0.60·Fu, which limits the gain. For E70XX on A36 plate the effective increase is about 17%.

Should I use ASD or LRFD?

Use whichever your project, drawings or engineer specify. ASD takes the actual service load and divides weld strength by 2.00. LRFD takes a load already multiplied by code load factors and multiplies weld strength by 0.75.

Can I use this for aluminum, stainless or fatigue-loaded welds?

No. The tool is built around carbon steel with E60XX to E90XX electrodes under AISC 360 static loading. Aluminum, stainless, cyclic loads and lifting equipment use different standards and safety factors.
