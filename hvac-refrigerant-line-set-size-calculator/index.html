How to Use This Refrigerant Line Set Size Calculator

Enter the system capacity in tons, the total tubing length from the outdoor unit to the indoor coil (including any vertical sections), and the vertical height difference if there is one. Choose the refrigerant, system type, number of elbows and your design ambient temperature. The calculator returns the suction (vapor) and liquid line outside diameters, a pressure-drop check, suction insulation thickness, and the extra refrigerant charge for the run. A comparison table shows how the sizes change with length.

Why This Matters

The line set is the one part of a system that cannot be swapped out later without cutting into walls or ceilings, so it has to be right the first time. An undersized suction line forces vapor through at high velocity. That costs capacity and efficiency, adds noise and makes the compressor work harder. An oversized suction line drops the velocity so low that oil stops returning to the compressor, which is a slow way to kill it.

Real jobs make this concrete. A 3-ton R-410A condenser behind the garage with the air handler in the attic might need 75 ft of tubing and a 10 ft lift. Install 5/8" suction on that run and the loss is roughly 4°F of saturation temperature, twice the usual 2°F design limit. A 4-ton unit on a 120 ft run to a rooftop-adjacent closet has the same problem. Long liquid lines can also lose enough pressure to flash, starving the metering device. Replacement jobs are just as risky, because the old line set is often the wrong size for new equipment. Use this tool for planning, material take-offs and sanity-checking a quote, then confirm against the equipment manufacturer's piping chart.

How It's Calculated

The calculator uses refrigerant-piping design practice from the ASHRAE Handbook (Refrigeration and Fundamentals). It applies the friction-loss method rather than a lookup table alone. The steps are:

  1. Load. Q = tons × 3.517 kW (1 ton = 12,000 BTU/hr).
  2. Mass flow. ṁ = Q ÷ net refrigeration effect. The effect is taken at 40°F suction saturation and a liquid temperature 15°F above the hotter of your ambient and 95°F.
  3. Equivalent length. Leq = tubing length + elbows × 30 × inside diameter. Each standard copper 90° elbow adds roughly 30 pipe diameters.
  4. Pressure drop. Darcy–Weisbach with the Swamee–Jain friction factor for drawn copper:
ΔP = f × (Leq / D) × (ρ × v² / 2)
v = ṁ / (ρ × A)

The suction line is the smallest size, no smaller than typical residential manufacturer charts, whose loss stays at or under 2°F equivalent saturation temperature. The liquid line must stay under 1°F and 350 ft/min. Sizes are always rounded up, never down. Liquid lift adds static head (ρ × g × height), and charge per foot is the liquid density times the tube's internal volume. Suction insulation is 3/4" wall, or 1" for ambients of 90°F or more, runs over 50 ft or systems over 5 tons. IRC M1411 requires at least R-4 on suction lines. Mini-splits use typical flare-connection sizes, not this method.

This is an estimate based on common rules of thumb and approximate refrigerant properties. Manufacturer installation instructions and local codes override it, and it is not engineering, permit or inspection approval.

Worked Example

Take a 3-ton R-410A split A/C with 75 ft of tubing, indoor coil 10 ft above the condenser, 4 elbows, 95°F ambient and 15 ft factory-charged. Enter those values and the tool follows these steps:

  1. Mass flow: 3 × 3.517 kW ÷ 163.9 kJ/kg ≈ 0.0644 kg/s, or about 511 lb/hr.
  2. Suction line: 3-ton systems start at 7/8" OD (0.785" ID). Equivalent length is 75 + 4 × 1.96 ≈ 82.9 ft. Velocity is about 1,240 ft/min, and the drop is about 1.78 psi, or 0.83°F. That is under the 2°F limit, so 7/8" stands.
  3. Liquid line: 3/8" would lose about 6.8 psi (1.4°F), over the 1°F limit. So the tool moves to 1/2", which loses about 1.5 psi (0.32°F).
  4. Lift: 10 ft of liquid at 1,000 kg/m³ adds about 4.34 psi, so total liquid loss is about 5.9 psi (1.2°F).
  5. Charge: 1/2" liquid line holds about 1.01 oz/ft, so (75 − 15) × 1.01 ≈ 60 oz (3.8 lb) must be added.
  6. Insulation: 1" wall, because the ambient is at least 90°F and the run exceeds 50 ft.

Result: 7/8" suction, 1/2" liquid, 1" insulation, roughly 60 oz of added charge.

Tips & Common Mistakes

  • Measure tubing, not straight-line distance. Add every offset and loop. Pressure drop depends on the tubing actually installed.
  • Don't use plumbing nominal sizes. Refrigeration copper (ACR) is sized by actual outside diameter. A 3/4" ACR tube is not 3/4" plumbing pipe.
  • Don't oversize "to be safe" on suction. Too much diameter lowers vapor velocity and traps oil, especially in vertical risers.
  • Weigh in the extra charge. Added refrigerant is calculated from liquid line size and length beyond the factory-charged length. Always confirm it with the manufacturer's charging chart and by measuring subcooling or superheat.
  • Insulate and protect. Keep the suction line insulated end to end with UV-resistant covering outdoors. Insulate the liquid line too where it crosses a hot attic.
  • Flush or replace old line sets. Reused tubing can hold acid, moisture or the wrong oil. Always pressure test with nitrogen and evacuate to 500 microns or lower.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between the suction line and the liquid line?

The suction line is the larger, insulated line. It carries cool, low-pressure vapor from the indoor coil back to the compressor. The liquid line is the smaller line. It carries warm, high-pressure liquid from the condenser to the metering device. Swapping them hurts capacity and can damage the compressor.

Can I reuse an R-22 line set with R-410A?

Sometimes, but it is not guaranteed. The tubing must be correctly sized and have a wall rated for R-410A's higher pressure. It must also be flushed of old mineral oil and contaminants. Many manufacturers recommend replacing it, and some void the warranty if you don't flush it.

How does a longer line set affect performance?

Every added foot raises pressure drop, which lowers capacity and efficiency. Past about 50 ft many manufacturers require a larger suction line or added charge, and most set a maximum length, often 100 to 150 ft. Always check your unit's installation manual for its limit.

Why do R-32 and R-454B get the same sizes as R-410A?

For the same capacity, these refrigerants move about the same volume of vapor as R-410A. Friction loss depends on that volume, so the calculated sizes match. They are mildly flammable (A2L), so follow the equipment manual for charge limits, leak detection and brazing precautions.

Do mini-splits follow the same sizing?

No. Mini-splits use fixed flare connections and manufacturer-published line sizes and maximum lengths. Do not upsize them. The tool shows typical sizes (for example 1/4" liquid and 3/8" suction for a 1-ton unit) for planning only.

Does vertical rise change the line size?

It can. A suction line that runs upward must keep vapor moving fast enough, about 1,000 ft/min, to carry oil up. A liquid line that lifts loses about 0.43 psi per foot of R-410A to static head. The calculator flags both situations.
