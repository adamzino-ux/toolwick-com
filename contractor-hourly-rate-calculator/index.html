How to Use This Contractor Hourly Rate Calculator

Enter the yearly take-home pay you want, your annual business overhead (insurance, truck, tools, software, licensing), any benefits you pay for yourself, and your estimated effective tax rate. Then set your weeks off, weekly working hours, and the share of those hours you can realistically bill. The calculator returns the hourly rate you need, a break-even rate, a day rate, a cost breakdown, and a table showing how the rate changes with utilization. The slider updates the result instantly once you have calculated.

Why This Matters

Most new contractors set their rate by taking a former employee wage and adding a few dollars. That is how a carpenter who earned $32 an hour on payroll ends up charging $45, believes it is a raise, and then discovers at tax time that they took home less than before. An employer silently covers payroll taxes, liability insurance, vehicles, paid holidays, health coverage, and the hours spent driving, estimating, and invoicing. When you work for yourself, every one of those costs lands in your rate.

The biggest trap is non-billable time. A contractor who works 40 hours a week rarely bills 40. Quoting jobs, buying materials, answering calls, bookkeeping, and travel between sites often eat 30 to 40 percent of the week. If your overhead is $18,000 and you bill only 1,222 hours, each billable hour has to carry about $14.73 of overhead before you earn a cent. This tool is useful for electricians, plumbers, remodelers, handymen, roofers, freelance estimators, and anyone selling their time on a job-by-job basis. It also helps you test whether a client's offered rate is worth taking.

How It's Calculated

The calculator works backward from the money you want to keep. It grosses your take-home goal up for tax, adds business costs, adds a profit reserve, and divides the total by billable hours.

Pre-tax income = Take-home ÷ (1 − tax rate)
Break-even revenue = Pre-tax income + Overhead + Benefits
Target revenue = Break-even revenue ÷ (1 − margin)
Billable hours = (52 − weeks off) × hours/week × utilization
Hourly rate = Target revenue ÷ Billable hours

Tax assumption: tax is applied only to your take-home goal. Overhead and self-funded benefits are treated as deductible business expenses, so they are paid from revenue before tax and are not grossed up. The profit/reserve margin is treated as untaxed retained cash. If you expect the reserve to be taxed too, or some of your costs are not deductible, raise the tax rate by a few points to compensate.

The profit margin is a percentage of revenue, not a markup on cost, so it is divided out rather than multiplied in. The suggested quote rate is rounded up to the next whole dollar, never down, so rounding never cuts into your income. The tax figure is a single blended estimate covering income and self-employment taxes. Real tax depends on deductions, your state, and entity type, so treat the result as a planning estimate. Licensing, bonding, and insurance requirements also vary by locality.

Worked Example

A remodeler wants $75,000 take-home, has $18,000 of overhead, pays $9,600 a year for health insurance, expects a 25% effective tax rate, takes 5 weeks off, works 40 hours a week, bills 65% of those hours, and wants a 10% reserve.

  • Gross up for tax: $75,000 ÷ (1 − 0.25) = $100,000, so taxes are $25,000.
  • Add costs: $100,000 + $18,000 + $9,600 = $127,600 break-even revenue.
  • Add the margin: $127,600 ÷ 0.90 = $141,777.78, which includes a $14,177.78 reserve.
  • Billable hours: 47 weeks × 40 = 1,880 working hours × 65% = 1,222 billable hours.
  • Rate: $141,777.78 ÷ 1,222 = $116.02 per hour, rounded up to $117 for quoting. The break-even rate is $104.42.

Raising utilization to 80% gives 1,504 billable hours and a rate of $94.27 ($141,777.78 ÷ 1,504), which shows how much better scheduling and fewer unpaid hours can improve your pricing.

Tips & Common Mistakes

  • Don't assume 100% billable time. Most solo contractors land between 50% and 75%. Track a typical month before trusting a higher number.
  • Count every overhead item. Include vehicle costs, fuel, phone, insurance, licenses, tool replacement, accounting, and advertising. Missing $200 a month adds about $2 to your hourly rate.
  • Use an effective tax rate, not a bracket. Self-employment tax adds roughly 15% on top of income tax, so 25% to 35% is a common planning range. Ask an accountant for your real figure.
  • Plan for unpaid weeks. Holidays, bad weather, sick days, and gaps between jobs are all weeks off. Four to six is realistic.
  • Revisit the rate every year. Material, insurance, and fuel costs rise, so update the inputs each January and when your goals change.

Frequently Asked Questions

What hourly rate should a contractor charge?

It depends on your trade, region, costs, and utilization, which is why a single national number is misleading. Use your own income goal and overhead in the calculator above. Then compare the result to local market rates to see whether the number is competitive.

What is a good billable utilization rate?

Solo contractors typically bill 50% to 75% of working hours. Established crews with a steady backlog can reach 80% or higher. If you are unsure, use 60% for a conservative estimate.

Should my hourly rate include taxes?

Yes. Your rate has to cover the income and self-employment tax you owe on profit, or you will end up with less take-home than planned. This calculator grosses up your target income by your effective tax rate so taxes are built in.

How is a contractor rate different from an employee wage?

An employee wage excludes payroll taxes, insurance, equipment, paid time off, and benefits, which an employer pays separately. A contractor's rate has to cover all of those plus unbillable time. Contractor rates commonly run 1.5 to 2.5 times the equivalent employee wage.

Can I use this rate for fixed-price bids?

Yes. Estimate the billable hours a job needs, multiply by your rate, and add materials with your usual markup. If a fixed bid works out to less than your break-even rate per hour, you are likely losing money on that job. The markup and profit margin calculator helps with the materials side.

How often should I raise my rate?

Review it at least once a year, and again whenever insurance, fuel, or material costs jump. Many contractors raise rates 3% to 8% annually for new clients and phase it in for repeat customers.
