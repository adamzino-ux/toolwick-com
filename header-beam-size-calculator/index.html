How to Use This Header Beam Size Calculator

Enter the clear width of the opening, the number of floors above, the roof snow load, the tributary width and the lumber species. Choose a 2-ply or 3-ply built-up header, then press Calculate. You get the smallest 2×6 to 2×12 header that passes every check, the jack studs needed at each end, the utilization of each check, and a table showing how every size performs for your span.

Why This Matters

A header carries everything above an opening: roof, snow, floors, walls and people. If it is too shallow it sags, doors stop closing, drywall cracks above the trim, and in the worst case the wall fails. Fixing an undersized header after the finishes are on can cost thousands of dollars, while sizing it correctly during framing costs only a few extra dollars of lumber.

Typical cases include cutting a new 6-foot window into a bearing wall, widening a doorway from 3 to 5 feet, installing a 9-foot garage door in a two-story house, or checking whether the headers in a plan are believable. Snow load matters more than most people expect. A 12-foot tributary width at 50 psf of snow puts 600 pounds per foot on the header before any floor is counted. This calculator makes that load visible so you can see why a header that works in Texas may be too small in Minnesota.

How It's Calculated

This is a simplified allowable-stress design check using National Design Specification (NDS) style design values for sawn lumber, with the live-load deflection limits used in IRC R301.7. It is not a lookup of the prescriptive header tables in IRC R602.7, and its answer can differ from those tables, so always compare it with the table that your jurisdiction has adopted.

Dead load wD = 20 psf × T + floors × (10 psf × T + 80 plf wall)
Live load wL = snow × T + floors × 40 psf × T
Design span L = clear span + 3 in
Moment M = w·L² / 8 → bending fb = M / S ≤ Fb
Shear V = w·L / 2 → fv = 1.5·V / A ≤ Fv
Deflection Δ = 5·w·L⁴ / (384·E·I)
live load ≤ L/360; 1.5 × dead + live ≤ L/240

Here T is the tributary width, S is the section modulus, A is the area and I is the moment of inertia of the built-up section (each ply is 1.5 inches wide). Fb is the No.2 bending value for each depth with the NDS size factor already included, and Southern Yellow Pine values are rounded down. No repetitive-member factor is applied, and the load-duration factor is held at 1.0, which keeps the result on the safe side. The 1.5 multiplier on dead load allows for long-term creep. Only 2×6, 2×8, 2×10 and 2×12 are considered. If none pass, the tool tells you to use an engineered beam such as LVL or PSL.

The maximum span shown for each size is rounded down to 0.1 ft. Jack studs per end come from the bearing check (Fc⊥ × bearing area) and a rule-of-thumb minimum of 1, 2 or 3 by span. The maximum span does not include the bearing check.

Worked Example

Take a 6-foot window opening, one floor above, 30 psf roof snow, 8 ft tributary width, Douglas Fir-Larch No.2, 2-ply header.

  1. Dead load = 20 × 8 + (10 × 8 + 80) = 320 plf. Live load = 30 × 8 + 40 × 8 = 560 plf. Total w = 880 plf.
  2. Design span = 6 ft + 3 in = 6.25 ft = 75 in. Moment = 880 × 6.25² / 8 = 4,297 ft-lb (51,563 in-lb).
  3. 2-ply 2×10: S = 42.78 in³, so fb = 1,205 psi against Fb = 990 psi. That is 122% and fails.
  4. 2-ply 2×12: S = 63.28 in³, so fb = 815 psi against Fb = 900 psi. That is 91% and passes.
  5. Shear: V = 2,750 lb, fv = 122 psi against 180 psi (68%). Live deflection is 0.034 in against 0.208 in (17%). Total deflection is 0.063 in against 0.313 in (21%).
  6. Bearing: each jack stud supports 625 psi × 4.5 in² = 2,813 lb, so the 2,750 lb end reaction needs 1 stud. The rule-of-thumb minimum for this span is 2.

Result: a 2-ply 2×12 header with 2 jack studs per end, with bending as the governing check. The maximum clear span for that size is 6.3 ft.

Tips & Common Mistakes

  • Measure the clear span, not the rough opening width including studs. The calculator adds bearing length itself.
  • Do not shrink the tributary width to get a smaller header. A common error is entering the full building width instead of half of it, or ignoring a roof overhang that adds load.
  • Match the header to the wall thickness. A 2-ply header (3 in) needs a ½ in spacer to fill a 2×4 wall. A 3-ply header (4.5 in) fits a 2×6 wall.
  • Do not skip the jack studs. A big header on too few supports can crush the wood at its ends. Continue the load path down through the floor to the foundation.
  • Switch to an engineered beam when the tool says 2×12 is not enough, or when you have point loads from girders or posts, which this calculator does not handle.

Frequently Asked Questions

What size header do I need for a 6 foot opening?

It depends on the load above, not just the span. With one floor above, 30 psf snow and an 8 ft tributary width, a 2-ply Douglas Fir-Larch 2×12 is needed in this calculator. A roof-only wall with a smaller tributary width may pass with a 2×8 or 2×10.

Is this the same as the IRC header span tables?

No. The IRC R602.7 tables are prescriptive and depend on building width, snow load and wall type. This tool runs its own simplified bending, shear and deflection checks, so results can be higher or lower than the tables. Use the table adopted in your area as the code reference.

When should I use LVL instead of built-up 2× lumber?

Consider LVL when the tool shows the load exceeds a 2×12, when spans pass roughly 8 to 10 feet under floor or snow loads, or when wall space is limited. LVL is stronger and straighter, but it must be sized from the manufacturer's tables or by an engineer.

Does a non-bearing wall need a header?

This calculator only covers bearing walls. Non-bearing partitions carry little load, and the rules vary, so check your local code. Some areas still require a flat or on-edge member above wide openings.

Why does a bigger snow load change the header so much?

Snow is a live load applied over the whole tributary width of the roof. At 50 psf and a 10 ft tributary width, that is 500 plf by itself, often more than a floor adds. Check your local ground snow load with your building department.

Can I use this for a garage door header?

You can for a clear opening up to 20 feet, but a 16-foot garage door in a bearing wall usually exceeds a built-up 2× header. Expect the tool to recommend an engineered beam, which should be specified and verified by a supplier or engineer.
