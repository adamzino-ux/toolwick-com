Why This Matters

Buying too little gravel means a second trip to the quarry — often with a minimum delivery fee of $50–$150. Buying too much wastes money and leaves you with a pile to dispose of. A standard 50 ft × 12 ft driveway at 4 inches deep works out to about 7.41 cubic yards of raw volume — but once you add a standard 10% wastage allowance for compaction and spreading, that becomes roughly 8.15 cubic yards, or about 11 tons of crushed stone. At a typical price of $30–$65 per ton, that's roughly $330–$715 in material alone.

Depth matters enormously. Going from 3 inches to 6 inches doubles your material cost. For a new driveway, most contractors recommend a 4–6 inch base layer plus a 2-inch top dressing. For an existing driveway top-up, 1.5–2 inches is usually enough. This calculator helps you price out each scenario before committing.

Homeowners, landscapers, and contractors all rely on accurate material estimates to write bids, plan deliveries, and avoid costly overages. Getting the math right upfront is the single biggest way to control project costs.

How It's Calculated

The core formula converts your driveway dimensions into cubic yards, applies a wastage allowance, then converts to tons:

Volume (ft³) = Length (ft) × Width (ft) × Depth (ft)
Volume (yd³) = Volume (ft³) ÷ 27
Volume with wastage (yd³) = Volume (yd³) × (1 + Wastage %)
Tons = Volume with wastage (yd³) × Density (tons/yd³)

All input units are converted to feet first. The wastage multiplier (default 10%) accounts for material that compacts, spreads beyond the edge, or is lost during spreading. The density figures used here — 1.25 to 1.5 tons per cubic yard depending on gravel type — are typical industry averages, not exact lab values. Actual density can vary with moisture content, stone size, and compaction, so always confirm the specific figure with your supplier for large orders.

Worked Example

Let's walk through the calculator's own default example: a driveway 50 ft long and 12 ft wide, with a 4-inch layer of crushed stone (density 1.35 tons/yd³) and the default 10% wastage allowance.

  1. Convert depth to feet: 4 in ÷ 12 = 0.333 ft
  2. Volume in cubic feet: 50 ft × 12 ft × 0.333 ft = 200 ft³
  3. Convert to cubic yards: 200 ft³ ÷ 27 = 7.41 yd³
  4. Add 10% wastage: 7.41 × 1.10 = 8.15 yd³
  5. Convert to tons: 8.15 yd³ × 1.35 tons/yd³ ≈ 11.0 tons
  6. Estimate material cost at $45/ton: 11.0 × $45 ≈ $495, plus roughly $75–$200 for delivery

So this driveway needs about 8.15 cubic yards, or roughly 11 tons of crushed stone, with a total material-plus-delivery budget in the neighborhood of $570–$695. These are the exact numbers you'll see if you load this calculator with its default values.

Tips & Common Mistakes

  • Measure twice. A 5-foot error on a 50-foot driveway is a 10% material error — nearly a full ton of gravel.
  • Don't skip wastage. At minimum, add 5–10% for compaction. For rough or sloped ground, use 15%.
  • New vs. top-up driveways: New installs typically need 4–6 inches of base. Refreshing an existing driveway usually only needs 1.5–2 inches.
  • Order by the ton, not by the bag. Bulk delivery is dramatically cheaper once you need more than roughly half a ton.
  • Check local delivery minimums. Most quarries have a 1–2 ton minimum order — confirm before calculating a very small project.
  • Treat density as an average. Ask your supplier for their specific tons-per-yard figure if you're ordering a large quantity, since actual density varies by moisture and stone size.

Frequently Asked Questions

How deep should a gravel driveway be?

For a new driveway, a depth of 4–6 inches is standard for the base layer, topped with 1–2 inches of smaller decorative gravel. For topping up an existing driveway, 1.5–2 inches is typically sufficient. Heavier vehicles like trucks or RVs benefit from a 6-inch base.

How many tons of gravel are in a cubic yard?

It varies by material, but typical averages are about 1.35 tons per cubic yard for crushed stone, 1.4 tons for pea gravel, and 1.5 tons for limestone. These are industry-average figures — always confirm the exact density with your supplier since it can shift with moisture and stone size.

What's the difference between cubic yards and tons?

Cubic yards measure volume — the space the gravel takes up. Tons measure weight. Suppliers sell and deliver by the ton, so you need to convert from volume to weight using the material's density, which this calculator does automatically based on the gravel type you select.

How much does a gravel driveway cost?

Material costs typically run $30–$65 per ton depending on gravel type and location. A 50×12 ft driveway at 4 inches deep needs about 11 tons after a standard 10% wastage allowance, putting material cost at roughly $330–$715. Add $75–$200 for delivery and $200–$600 for labor if you hire installation out.

Does this calculator include compaction and spillage?

Yes — the wastage field (default 10%) is added on top of the raw volume calculation specifically to account for compaction, uneven spreading, and material lost during delivery. You can adjust it up to 15% or more for rough or sloped sites.

Can I use this for a gravel path or parking pad instead of a driveway?

Absolutely. The math is identical — length × width × depth — so it works equally well for gravel paths, parking pads, patios, or drainage beds. Just enter the correct dimensions for your project.
