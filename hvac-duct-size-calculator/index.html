Why This Matters

Duct sizing is one of the most consequential decisions in HVAC design — and one of the most frequently botched. An undersized duct creates excessive velocity, producing noise, pressure drop, and higher energy bills. An oversized duct wastes money on materials and can cause poor air distribution and comfort complaints.

For a typical 2,000 sq ft house, the main trunk duct might carry 1,600–2,000 CFM. Branch ducts to individual rooms typically range from 100 to 600 CFM. A 400 CFM branch duct should be sized to carry air at 700–900 FPM — which works out to about a 10" round duct. Using an 8" round instead would push velocity above 1,100 FPM, creating audible whistling and causing the system to work harder.

HVAC contractors, mechanical engineers, building inspectors, and advanced DIYers all run a duct size calculator CFM check on nearly every project. Even a simple bathroom exhaust fan upgrade benefits from proper duct sizing to ensure adequate CFM delivery.

How It's Calculated

All duct sizing uses the fundamental airflow equation:

CFM = Area (ft²) × Velocity (FPM)

Rearranged: Area = CFM ÷ Velocity

For a round duct: Area = π × (D/2)² → solve for D: D = 2 × √(CFM ÷ (π × Velocity)) × 12 inches. This calculator then rounds up to the next standard manufactured size (never down) — a widely used sheet-metal industry rule of thumb for quieter, lower-resistance airflow. It's a general convention, not a verbatim requirement of SMACNA or any single code body, so always confirm against your local mechanical code and manufacturer's catalog.

For a rectangular duct: Area = W × H. Given an aspect ratio R = W/H, then H = √(Area ÷ R) and W = H × R. The equivalent diameter (for comparing to round ducts) uses the ASHRAE formula: De = 1.3 × (a×b)^0.625 ÷ (a+b)^0.25

Worked Example

Say a bedroom needs 400 CFM of supply air and you want a residential-quiet velocity of 800 FPM.

Step 1: Required area = 400 ÷ 800 = 0.5 ft² = 72 in².

Step 2: Exact round diameter = 2 × √(72 ÷ π) = 2 × √22.9 = 2 × 4.79 = 9.58 inches.

Step 3: Round up to the nearest standard size: 10" round duct.

Step 4: Actual area at 10" = π × 5² = 78.5 in² (0.545 ft²). Actual velocity = 400 ÷ 0.545 = 734 FPM — comfortably in the quiet residential range for a supply duct.

Tips & Common Mistakes

  • Don't size for maximum velocity. Residential supply ducts should stay under 900 FPM and return ducts under 700 FPM to keep noise levels acceptable — use the Duct Application selector to check the right threshold.
  • Round up to standard sizes. Flex duct comes in 4", 5", 6", 8", 10", 12", 14", and 16" diameters. Always round up to the next standard size, never down — a slightly larger duct is quieter and more efficient, never the wrong choice.
  • Account for duct friction. Each 90° elbow adds roughly 10–15 feet of equivalent length. Long duct runs need to be sized for the total equivalent length, not just actual distance.
  • 2:1 or 3:1 is a practical compromise, not the "most efficient" shape. A square (1:1) duct actually has the smallest surface area for a given cross-sectional area, minimizing material and heat loss — but square ducts are harder to fit in shallow joist bays. A 2:1–3:1 ratio balances installation space against that efficiency; avoid ratios above 4:1, which increase friction loss disproportionately.
  • System static pressure matters. These calculations give you the correct free-area size. Always verify your blower fan can deliver the required CFM against your system's total static pressure — consult a licensed HVAC professional for final design.
  • Watch for the oversize warning. If a result exceeds the largest listed standard duct size, don't just accept a silently clamped number — split the airflow across multiple ducts or get a custom fabrication sized by an engineer.

Frequently Asked Questions

What CFM should I use for each room?

A general rule of thumb is 1 CFM per square foot of conditioned space for standard 8-foot ceilings. A 200 sq ft bedroom would need approximately 200 CFM, though a proper Manual J load calculation will give you a more precise number based on insulation, windows, and climate.

What velocity is too loud for residential HVAC?

Above 900 FPM in supply ducts and 700 FPM in return ducts, occupants typically begin to notice airflow noise. For bedrooms and quiet spaces, staying below 700 FPM is recommended. Commercial spaces tolerate up to 1,600 FPM without significant comfort complaints.

How do I convert between round and rectangular duct sizes?

Use the equivalent diameter formula (ASHRAE 2009): De = 1.3 × (a×b)^0.625 ÷ (a+b)^0.25, where a and b are the duct width and height in inches. This gives you a round duct diameter with the same pressure drop characteristics as the rectangular duct — not the same cross-sectional area.

Should I size for the main trunk or branch ducts?

Both. The main trunk carries total system CFM and should be sized at commercial velocities (1,000–1,200 FPM) to keep it compact. Branch ducts carry individual room CFM and should be sized at residential velocities (600–900 FPM) for quiet operation. Trunk-and-branch systems need the trunk to step down in size after each branch takeoff.

Does this calculator replace a Manual D duct design?

No. This tool gives a quick, code-informed estimate based on standard velocity and area formulas, but a full Manual D calculation accounts for total external static pressure, friction rate, and fitting losses across the entire duct system. Use a licensed HVAC contractor for final permit-ready designs.

What's the difference between supply and return duct sizing?

Return ducts are typically sized for slightly lower velocities (around 500–700 FPM) than supply ducts because return grilles are more noticeable to occupants and often located in high-traffic areas. Use the same CFM ÷ velocity formula, just switch this calculator's Duct Application selector to "Return Duct" so the rating badges reflect the stricter threshold.
