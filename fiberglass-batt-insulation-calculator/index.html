How to Use This Fiberglass Batt Insulation Calculator

Pick the part of the house you are insulating, then enter its length and height (or width for floors and ceilings). Subtract the area of windows and doors, choose your framing spacing, framing depth and batt R-value, and type in the coverage printed on the package. The calculator returns the packages to buy, rounded up, along with net area, waste, linear feet of batt, framing bays and optional cost.

Why This Matters

Insulation is cheap to buy and expensive to be short on. Stopping a Saturday job because you are two bags shy means a second store trip, and a different production run can have slightly different thickness. Over-buying wastes money, and opened bags that sit in a garage soak up moisture and lose loft. A 1,500 sq ft attic floor at roughly $0.60 to $0.80 per sq ft for batts is a $900 to $1,200 purchase, so a 10% error is $90 to $120 either way.

Good estimates matter for energy as well. The EPA's ENERGY STAR program estimates that air sealing plus adding insulation can cut heating and cooling costs by about 15% on average. A common scenario is a homeowner in a 1970s house topping up R-11 attic batts to R-38, or insulating the walls of a new garage conversion with R-13 in 2x4 framing. Contractors use the same area take-off to write quotes, and owner-builders use it to compare batts against blown-in or spray foam before they commit.

How It's Calculated

The calculator uses a straightforward area take-off with conservative rounding:

Gross area = length × height × sections Net area = gross area − openings Area to buy = net area × (1 + waste %) Packages = ROUNDUP(area to buy ÷ coverage per package) Linear feet = area to buy ÷ (batt width in ÷ 12) Bays ≈ ROUNDUP(length × 12 ÷ spacing) × sections

Framing lumber is not deducted from the area. Batts are cut slightly wider than the cavity so they friction-fit, and the lumber share (about 7% at 16 in on center) is absorbed by cuts around wiring, boxes and plumbing. Packages are always rounded up, never down. Batt width is 15 in for 16 in spacing and 23 in for 24 in spacing.

The R-value check compares the typical thickness of the batt you choose with your framing depth. A batt thicker than its cavity is compressed and delivers less than its labeled R-value, so the tool flags it and suggests the highest R-value that fits. Thicknesses are nominal and vary by brand, so confirm them on the package. R-value targets come from the IECC 2021 and IRC Chapter 11 (N1102); your local code edition may differ. This tool is a material estimate, not engineering, permit or inspection approval.

Worked Example

Say you are insulating the exterior walls of a small house with 2x4 framing at 16 in on center. The perimeter is 120 ft, walls are 8 ft tall, and there are six windows at 15 sq ft and two doors at 21 sq ft (132 sq ft of openings). You choose R-13 batts, a 10% waste allowance, and a package that covers 88 sq ft. These are also the calculator's default values.

  1. Gross area: 120 × 8 × 1 = 960 sq ft
  2. Net area: 960 − 132 = 828 sq ft
  3. Waste allowance: 828 × 10% = 82.8 sq ft, so area to buy is 910.8 sq ft
  4. Packages: 910.8 ÷ 88 = 10.35, rounded up to 11 packages
  5. Coverage purchased: 11 × 88 = 968 sq ft (57.2 sq ft beyond the allowance)
  6. Linear feet of 15 in batt: 910.8 ÷ 1.25 = 728.6 ft; bays ≈ 120 × 12 ÷ 16 = 90
  7. If you enter $52 per package: 11 × $52 = $572, or about $0.69 per net sq ft

Tips & Common Mistakes

  • Don't compress batts. Stuffing R-19 into a 2x4 wall does not give you R-19. Match the batt to the cavity depth, or use high-density R-15 in 2x4 walls and R-21 in 2x6 walls.
  • Cut, don't fold. Split batts around wires and pipes and cut them to fit boxes. Gaps, voids and folded edges cut real-world performance.
  • Keep ventilation and heat sources clear. Use baffles at soffit vents, and keep insulation about 3 in from flues and non-IC rated recessed lights.
  • Measure openings honestly. Forgetting to subtract windows overbuys. Subtracting more than the real opening area underbuys, so round openings down.
  • Wear protection. Gloves, long sleeves, eye protection and a dust mask or respirator keep fibers off skin and out of lungs.

Frequently Asked Questions

How many square feet does a bag of fiberglass batt insulation cover?

It depends on batt size and count. Common retail bags range from about 40 sq ft to over 110 sq ft. Always read the coverage on the package label and enter it in the calculator rather than guessing.

How much extra insulation should I buy for waste?

Five percent works for open, rectangular attic floors, and 10% is a safer default for walls. Use 15% to 20% for complex framing, many obstructions, or a first-time install.

Should I subtract the studs from the wall area?

This calculator doesn't, on purpose. The batts are wider than the cavity and trimming adds scrap, so the framing share is roughly offset. That keeps the count on the safe side of running short.

Can I put R-19 batts in a 2x4 wall?

Not without compressing them, and a compressed batt loses R-value. For 2x4 walls use R-13 or high-density R-15. If you need more R-value, consider continuous exterior insulation or a different product, and check your local code.

Should I choose 16 inch or 24 inch wide batts?

Match the batt to your framing spacing. Standard stud and joist spacing is 16 in or 24 in on center, and a batt that is about 1 in wider than the cavity stays in place without staples or wire.

What R-value do I need?

It depends on your climate zone and local code. IECC 2021 prescriptive targets run from about R-30 to R-60 for attics and from R-13 to R-30 for wall cavities. Your building department has the final say.
