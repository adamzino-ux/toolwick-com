How to Use This Rafter Vent & Attic Baffle Calculator

Pick Attic eave baffles if you have a ventilated attic with trusses or joists and need baffles at the soffit to hold insulation back from the vent. Pick Cathedral rafter vents if insulation sits between sloped rafters and you need a vent channel the full length of every bay. Enter lengths, spacing, pitch and your insulation target. The calculator returns the cut length per bay, pieces per bay, a total to buy including 10% waste, and the insulation depth that actually fits.

Why This Matters

Most attic moisture and ice-dam problems trace back to blocked soffit vents. Blown-in insulation slides downhill into the eaves in minutes if there is no baffle, and the soffit airflow that carries moisture and heat out of the roof assembly stops. A 1,600 sq ft ranch with 40-foot eaves and trusses at 24 inches has about 40 bays across both sides. Skipping even a handful can create cold, damp pockets, frost on the sheathing, and shingles that cook from underneath.

Baffles matter more every year because attic insulation targets keep rising. Many climate zones now call for R-49 or R-60, which is 14 to 20 inches of loose-fill. That is far deeper than the 3.5-inch heel of a standard truss, so the baffle has to run a long way up the slope before the roof is high enough to hold the full depth. In vaulted ceilings the problem is different: a 2x10 rafter only has 9.25 inches of depth, and after a 1-inch air channel you are limited to about R-28 with batts. Knowing that before you buy materials saves a trip back to the store, or a failed inspection.

How It's Calculated

Attic eave baffles. The baffle must run up the roof slope from the wall plate until the roof underside is high enough that insulation can no longer slide under it, then continue a further 6 inches as a safety margin (a common installer rule of thumb, not a code number).

Design depth = target R ÷ R-per-inch × settling factor
Slope sin = pitch ÷ √(144 + pitch²)
Baffle length = (design depth − heel height) ÷ sin + 6 in
(minimum 24 in, rounded up to the next whole inch)

Settling factors are 1.18 for blown cellulose and 1.10 for blown fiberglass, so the baffle covers the as-blown depth rather than the settled depth. Batts use 1.0. Baffle pieces are 48 inches long and overlap 4 inches where more than one is needed. Bay count is eave length ÷ spacing, rounded up, per eave.

Cathedral rafter vents. Rafter length is run × 12 × √(1 + (pitch ÷ 12)²). The maximum insulation depth is the rafter depth minus 1 inch, following the 1-inch clearance between insulation and roof sheathing in IRC R806.3 (2018 and 2021 editions). The achievable R-value is rounded down. Local codes vary, so confirm with your building department.

Worked Example

A gable house has 40-foot eaves on both sides, trusses at 24 inches on center, a 6/12 pitch, a 3.5-inch standard heel, and R-38 blown cellulose.

  • Bays: 40 × 12 ÷ 24 = 20 per eave, so 40 total.
  • Design depth: 38 ÷ 3.5 = 10.86 in, × 1.18 = 12.81 in as blown.
  • Slope sine at 6/12: 6 ÷ √180 = 0.4472.
  • Baffle length: (12.81 − 3.5) ÷ 0.4472 + 6 = 26.8 in, rounded up to 27 in. One 48-inch baffle per bay, cut to length.
  • Quantity: 40 baffles + 10% waste = 44 to buy.
  • At the wall plate only 3.5 − 1 = 2.5 in fits, about R-8. The calculator warns about this, and a raised heel would solve it.

For a cathedral version of the same house with a 14-foot run, 2x10 rafters at 16 inches and R-30 batts: rafter length is 188 in, so 5 overlapped pieces per bay across 60 bays, or 330 with waste. The 2x10 holds 8.25 in of insulation (R-26 with batts), so R-30 does not fit. You would need 2x12 rafters or added rigid foam.

Tips & Common Mistakes

  • Don't confuse the baffle with the soffit vent. The baffle only protects the air path. Total ventilation still has to meet the net free area requirement, so check it with the roof attic ventilation calculator.
  • Staple baffles at the top and sides so loose-fill can't push them down. Foam-board dams at the wall plate stop wind washing under the baffle.
  • Measure the real heel. A truss labeled raised-heel can still be only 6 inches. Measure from the top plate to the underside of the sheathing.
  • Add depth markers. Staple rulers or mark rafters so installers blow insulation to the planned depth, and keep it below the baffle top.
  • Don't compress batts to fit. A compressed R-30 batt delivers less than R-30. Pick a deeper rafter or a denser product.

Frequently Asked Questions

How many rafter vents do I need per bay?

You need one baffle per rafter or truss bay along the eave. For cathedral ceilings you need a continuous channel from soffit to ridge in every bay, so several overlapped pieces per bay is normal. The calculator rounds up and adds 10% for cuts and damaged pieces.

How far should an attic baffle extend past the insulation?

A common rule of thumb is to extend the baffle at least 4 to 6 inches above the finished insulation surface. This calculator uses 6 inches. Always check the manufacturer's installation instructions for your product.

Do I need a baffle if I use batt insulation?

Yes. Batts can droop or shift into the soffit area, and the 1-inch clearance rule still applies. A baffle keeps the channel open and also protects against wind washing, which lowers the effective R-value of the batt near the eave.

How much air space does insulation need under the roof deck?

IRC R806.3 calls for a minimum 1-inch space between the insulation and the roof sheathing where eave vents are installed. Some foam vent products need 1.5 inches. Local amendments differ, so confirm requirements with your inspector.

Why does the calculator say less insulation fits at the wall?

At a standard truss heel there is only about 3.5 inches from top plate to roof, and the baffle takes 1 inch of it. That leaves roughly R-8 over the wall unless you use a raised-heel truss or dense foam in that zone. It's a common weak point in attic energy performance.

Can this calculator approve my project for a permit?

No. It gives planning estimates using IRC R806.3 clearances and typical installer practice. It isn't engineering, permit or inspection approval, so always confirm with your local building department.
