How to Use This Weld Weight Calculator

Pick your unit system and joint type, then enter the dimensions from your drawing or welding procedure: plate thickness, root gap, root face, groove angle, reinforcement height and weld length. For fillet welds you only need the leg size. Set the process (or drag the efficiency slider) and press Calculate. The tool returns the weld metal deposited in the joint, the filler metal you need to purchase after spatter and stub losses, and a suggested order quantity with a 10% contingency.

Why This Matters

Filler metal is a real line item. A fabricator welding 400 feet of 3/8 in. fillet on a skid frame deposits roughly 96 lb of steel, and with stick electrodes at 60% efficiency that means buying about 160 lb. Guess low and the job stops while you wait for a delivery; guess high and you tie up cash in rods that absorb moisture on the shelf.

Weld weight also drives labor and schedule. Arc time is roughly deposited weight divided by the process deposition rate, so estimators use weld metal weight to price a job, compare processes, and decide whether a bevel, a smaller root gap or a different joint design saves money. Because fillet volume grows with the square of the leg size, going from a 1/4 in. to a 3/8 in. fillet uses 2.25 times the metal, which is why over-welding quietly eats margin and adds heat distortion.

Procurement, shipping and crane planning for large structures, pressure piping and shipbuilding often start from weld metal tonnage. Hobbyists and farm shops use the same math to decide whether a 33 lb spool of wire will finish a trailer build.

How It's Calculated

The calculator uses the standard estimating method: find the cross-sectional area of the weld, multiply by length to get volume, multiply by the material density to get weight, then divide by deposition efficiency to get the filler you must buy.

Area (fillet) = leg² / 2 + (2/3) × (leg × √2) × h Area (single V) = g×t + d²×tan(α/2) + cap, d = t − r Area (double V) = g×t + 2×d²×tan(α/2) + 2×cap, d = (t − r) / 2 Area (single bevel)= g×t + d²×tan(β)/2 + cap, d = t − r Area (square) = g×t + cap Cap (reinforcement)= (2/3) × W × h, W = groove top width + 2h Weight = Area × Length × Density × Number of welds Filler to buy = Weight ÷ Efficiency

Here t is plate thickness, g is root gap, r is root face, α is the included groove angle, β is the bevel angle and h is reinforcement height. The cap is modelled as a parabolic segment, which closely matches a real convex bead. Densities are 7.85 g/cm³ for carbon steel, 7.93 g/cm³ for stainless and 2.70 g/cm³ for aluminum.

Conservative rounding: filler quantities are always rounded up, never down. Efficiency values are typical figures (stick about 60%, flux-cored about 85%, solid MIG about 95%, TIG rod about 95%, submerged arc about 99%); check your consumable manufacturer's data. This is a geometric estimating method for ordering material. Weld size, joint design and procedure must come from your qualified WPS and the governing code (for example AWS D1.1 or ASME Section IX), and local codes vary. This tool is not engineering, inspection or code-compliance approval.

Worked Example

Single-V butt joint in carbon steel, 1/2 in. plate, 1/8 in. root gap, 1/16 in. root face, 60° included angle, 1/16 in. reinforcement, 20 ft long, welded with solid MIG wire at 95%.

  1. Groove depth d = 0.5 − 0.0625 = 0.4375 in. tan(30°) = 0.5774.
  2. Groove area = g×t + d²×tan(α/2) = 0.0625 + 0.1914 × 0.5774 = 0.1730 in².
  3. Top width = 0.125 + 2 × 0.4375 × 0.5774 = 0.6302 in. Cap width W = 0.6302 + 0.125 = 0.7552 in. Cap area = (2/3) × 0.7552 × 0.0625 = 0.0315 in².
  4. Total area = 0.2045 in². Volume = 0.2045 × 240 in. = 49.07 in³.
  5. Weight = 49.07 × 0.2836 lb/in³ = 13.92 lb (about 0.696 lb per foot).
  6. Filler to buy = 13.92 ÷ 0.95 = 14.65 lb. With 10% contingency, order 16.2 lb.

Those are the numbers the calculator shows with its default inputs.

Tips & Common Mistakes

  • Use the as-welded dimensions. A root gap of 3/16 in. instead of 1/8 in. adds more than 10% to a V-groove on 1/2 in. plate. Take dimensions from the WPS, not from memory.
  • Do not forget both sides. A T-joint welded on both sides needs two welds. Enter 2 in "Number of identical welds."
  • Convert pipe welds to a length. For a girth weld, use the circumference (π × diameter) as the weld length.
  • Watch fillet size. Metal grows with the square of the leg, so a 1/16 in. oversize on a 1/4 in. fillet adds about 56% more weld metal.
  • Add a contingency. Start/stop waste, tack welds, repairs and test coupons are not in the geometry. The 10% suggested order covers typical shop waste, but increase it for out-of-position or repair-heavy work.
  • Match efficiency to the actual consumable. Self-shielded flux-cored wire and low-hydrogen stick electrodes have different efficiencies than the defaults. Use the slider for custom values.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do you calculate the weight of a weld?

Multiply the weld's cross-sectional area by its length to get volume, then by the metal's density. For steel that is about 0.2836 lb per cubic inch. A 1/4 in. equal-leg fillet has an area of 0.03125 in², so one foot weighs about 0.106 lb before convexity.

What is deposition efficiency and why does it matter?

It is the share of purchased filler that ends up in the weld. The rest is lost as spatter, slag, fumes and electrode stubs. Stick electrodes run about 60%, solid MIG wire about 95%, so the same joint needs far more stick electrode by weight.

How much filler metal do I need per foot of fillet weld?

A 1/4 in. steel fillet deposits about 0.106 lb per foot, and a 3/8 in. fillet about 0.239 lb per foot, before convexity and losses. Divide by your process efficiency and add a contingency to get the amount to order.

Does the calculator include spatter, stubs and waste?

Spatter, slag and stub losses are covered by the deposition efficiency setting. The suggested order adds 10% on top for start/stop waste and minor repairs. Large rework or scrap is not modelled.

Can I use it for stainless steel or aluminum?

Yes. Choose the material in the dropdown and the calculator switches the density. Aluminum weighs about a third as much as steel for the same weld volume, so spool weights are much lower even though the geometry is identical.

Is this result good enough for design or inspection?

No. It is an estimating tool for material quantities only. Weld sizes, joint preparation and procedures must come from a qualified WPS and applicable codes such as AWS D1.1 or ASME Section IX, and local requirements may differ.
