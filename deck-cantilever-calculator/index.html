How to Use This Deck Cantilever Calculator

Choose whether joists or the beam is cantilevering. Enter the backspan: for joists, the distance from the ledger (or inside beam) to the beam that carries the overhang; for a cantilevered beam, the post-to-post span. Then drag the slider to the overhang you would like. The tool returns the maximum overhang, whether your plan is within the one-fourth rule, the minimum backspan you would need, joist or beam length, and the loads each joist puts on its supports. The diagram updates as you change the span or slider.

Why This Matters

A cantilever lets a deck extend past its last beam without extra posts, so you skip a row of footings, dodge tree roots, avoid deep frost-depth holes and hide the beam under the deck edge for a cleaner look. But a cantilever flips the structure: the overhanging end pushes down while the backspan tries to lift off its support. Push it too far and you get a bouncy edge, split joists, or a ledger that pulls away from the house.

Real numbers show how fast it adds up. A 10 ft backspan allows 2 ft 6 in of overhang; a 12 ft backspan allows 3 ft. Builders often want 4 ft to fit a bistro table past the beam, which would need a 16 ft backspan. Homeowners planning a deck, contractors pricing a job, and inspectors checking a plan all need this number before any lumber is cut. Running it first also tells you if you need an extra beam instead.

How It's Calculated

This tool applies the one-fourth rule used in the prescriptive deck provisions of the International Residential Code (IRC): joist overhangs may not exceed one-fourth of the actual joist backspan, and beam overhangs past the end posts may not exceed one-fourth of the beam span. This is consistent with the DCA 6 prescriptive deck guide. Section numbers, tables and footnotes differ between code editions (in the 2018 and 2021 IRC, beams are in R507.5 and joists in R507.6), and local codes may be stricter or adopt a different edition. Check the edition your jurisdiction uses.

Maximum overhang = backspan ÷ 4, rounded down to the nearest 1/8 inch. Minimum backspan = overhang × 4, rounded up to the nearest 1/8 inch. Rounding always goes to the conservative side.

For joist loads, the tool treats each joist as a beam over two supports. With backspan L, overhang c and load w (pounds per foot = psf × spacing in feet):

Ledger reaction = w × (L² − c²) ÷ (2L)
Beam reaction = w × (L + c) − ledger reaction
Moment at the beam = w × c² ÷ 2

The uplift check loads the overhang with dead plus live load while the backspan carries dead load only. If the ledger reaction turns negative, the backspan is trying to lift. The tool does not size joists or beams. Total joist span, species, grade and spacing still need to satisfy the joist span table in your adopted code.

Worked Example

A deck has a 10 ft 0 in joist backspan, 16 in spacing, 16 ft width, 10 psf dead load, 40 psf live load, and a planned 2 ft overhang.

  • Maximum overhang: 120 in ÷ 4 = 30 in = 2′ 6″. The planned 24 in is 20% of the backspan, so it is within the rule.
  • Minimum backspan for 24 in: 24 × 4 = 96 in = 8′ 0″.
  • Total joist length: 10 ft + 2 ft = 12 ft, so a 12 ft board.
  • Joist count: 16 ft × 12 ÷ 16 in = 12, plus 1 = 13 joists, or 156 linear feet.
  • Line load: 50 psf × 1.333 ft = 66.7 lb/ft. Ledger reaction = 66.7 × (100 − 4) ÷ 20 = 320 lb. Beam reaction = 66.7 × 12 − 320 = 480 lb. Moment = 66.7 × 4 ÷ 2 = 133 ft-lb, shown rounded up to 134.
  • Uplift check: (13.3 × 100 − 66.7 × 4) ÷ 20 = 53 lb still pressing down, so no uplift.

Tips & Common Mistakes

  • Measure the actual backspan between bearing points, not the overall deck depth. A 12 ft backspan with a 3 ft overhang is exactly at the limit (3 ÷ 12 = 25%) and makes a 15 ft deck. A 9 ft backspan allows only 2 ft 3 in, so a 3 ft overhang there is well over.
  • Do not put a stair landing, hot tub or heavy planter on the overhang. The one-fourth rule assumes ordinary residential loads.
  • Guard posts attached to cantilevered joist ends need blocking and proper hardware. Guards must resist a 200 lb concentrated load at the top.
  • Keep the full joist length within the span table. A joist that is fine for 10 ft may not be rated for 12 ft total length.
  • Do not use joist overhang numbers for a beam. A cantilevered beam carries the loads of many joists and needs its own check against the beam table in your code.

Frequently Asked Questions

How far can a deck cantilever past the beam?

Under the IRC, joists may overhang up to one-fourth of their actual backspan. A 12 ft backspan allows 3 ft; an 8 ft backspan allows 2 ft. Your local building department can set tighter limits.

Can a deck beam cantilever past the posts?

Yes. The IRC permits a beam to extend past each end post by up to one-fourth of the beam span, which is the distance between posts. Use the "Beam past the end posts" option above to check it.

What is a backspan?

The backspan is the length of joist (or beam) between its two supports, behind the overhang. For a ledger-attached deck it runs from the ledger to the beam. The overhang is the part that extends past the beam.

Can I cantilever more than one-fourth of the span?

Not under the prescriptive code path. Longer overhangs need an engineer's design and a permit. This tool never goes beyond the one-fourth limit.

Does a bigger joist allow a longer cantilever?

Not under the one-fourth rule. A 2×12 and a 2×8 have the same prescriptive limit. A larger joist stiffens the deck, but only an engineered design can justify a longer overhang.

Can I put a railing post or stairs on the cantilever?

Railing posts can be mounted on a cantilever when blocked and fastened with approved hardware. Stairs and heavy concentrated loads should not sit on an overhang unless an engineer has designed it.
