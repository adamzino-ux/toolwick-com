Why This Matters

A hopper gun is an air-hungry tool. Even a small one moves 5 to 12 cubic feet of air per minute while the trigger is open, which is more than most homeowner compressors can produce. A typical 6-gallon pancake unit delivers about 2.6 CFM at 90 PSI, so it falls behind within a minute or two. Then you wait for the tank to refill while the texture in the hopper stiffens and your pattern changes from one pass to the next. That is why finishers who texture a 300 sq ft bedroom usually bring a 30-gallon two-stage compressor instead of the small one from the garage.

Sizing it right saves money in both directions. It stops you from buying or renting a unit that is too small, and it stops you from paying for an 80-gallon machine when a 30-gallon one will do. It also helps crews decide whether one compressor can feed two guns on a 2,000 sq ft house or whether a second unit is needed. Elevation matters as well: at 6,000 ft a compressor delivers roughly 18% less air than its sea-level rating.

How It's Calculated

The tool estimates the average air your guns draw and adds allowances for hose loss and altitude. It then adds a safety margin so the pump is not running flat out. These are trade rules of thumb, not figures from a code or standard.

  • Base gun demand: typical CFM with the trigger fully open. Knockdown 8, orange peel 6, popcorn 12, splatter 10, smooth 5, multiplied by the number of guns.
  • Spray duty: the share of working time the trigger is actually open. A lower duty lowers the average demand, because the tank recovers between passes. At 100% the compressor must keep up continuously.
  • Hose factor: an allowance for pressure loss in ⅜″ hose: ×1.03 up to 25 ft, ×1.06 up to 50 ft, ×1.12 up to 100 ft and ×1.18 up to 150 ft. A ¼″ hose loses more, so use ⅜″ beyond 25 ft.
  • Altitude factor: delivered air drops about 3% per 1,000 ft. The tool uses the top of your elevation band: 1 ÷ (1 − 0.03 × thousands of ft).
  • Safety margin: the minimum is multiplied by 1.25 to get the recommended CFM. Both values are rounded up to 0.1 CFM.
  • Tank: 2.5 gallons per recommended CFM, never less than 20 gallons, rounded up to the next standard size (20, 30, 40, 60, 80, 120). Tank size does not shrink when spray duty is lowered.

Formula: Minimum CFM = Base CFM × Guns × Duty × Hose factor × Altitude factor, then Recommended = Minimum × 1.25. Compressor ratings in the comparison table are typical SCFM at 90 PSI. Texture guns run at 35–90 PSI, where a compressor delivers a little more air, so the comparison errs on the cautious side.

Worked Example

A 20 × 15 ft bedroom with knockdown texture, one gun, a 50 ft hose, 70% spray duty and a job site near sea level:

  1. Ceiling area: 20 × 15 = 300 sq ft.
  2. Base demand: 8 CFM × 1 gun = 8.0 CFM.
  3. Duty: 8.0 × 0.70 = 5.6 CFM average.
  4. Hose (50 ft): 5.6 × 1.06 = 5.936 CFM. Altitude factor 1.00 leaves it at 5.936, which rounds up to a minimum of 6.0 CFM.
  5. Margin: 5.936 × 1.25 = 7.42, which rounds up to a recommended 7.5 CFM at 90 PSI.
  6. Tank: 7.5 × 2.5 = 18.75 gal, raised to the 20-gallon minimum.
  7. Spray time: 300 ÷ 40 sq ft/min = 7.5 min with the trigger open, ÷ 0.70 = 10.7, so about 11 minutes. This excludes masking, mixing and hopper refills.

The tool shows a gun PSI range of 40–65. In the comparison table, a 30-gallon single-stage unit (7.0 CFM) is marginal. A 30-gallon two-stage unit (9.5 CFM) clears the 7.5 CFM recommendation.

Tips & Common Mistakes

  • Don't trust "max" CFM on the box. Compare the SCFM at 90 PSI on the nameplate. Inflated "displacement CFM" figures can be 30% higher than what the pump really delivers.
  • Use ⅜″ hose, and keep it short. Quarter-inch hose throttles a hopper gun on runs over 25 ft. Use the shortest hose that lets you reach the whole room from one spot.
  • Start at the low end of the PSI range. Set the regulator at the gun, test on cardboard, and raise pressure in small steps. Never exceed the lowest pressure rating in the system.
  • Drain the tank and water trap. Water in the line spits onto the ceiling and ruins adhesion. Drain before and after each job.
  • Check the electrical supply. Larger compressors usually need a dedicated 240 V circuit. Read the nameplate amps and ask a licensed electrician. Don't run big motors on long extension cords.

Frequently Asked Questions

What size compressor do I need for a hopper gun?

For a single gun spraying knockdown or orange peel, plan on about 6–10 CFM at 90 PSI and a tank of at least 20 gallons. For the default bedroom example above, the tool recommends 7.5 CFM, and a 30-gallon two-stage unit rated 9–10 CFM fits comfortably. A 6-gallon pancake compressor is too small for almost every hopper gun job.

Can I use a small compressor if I spray in short bursts?

Yes, to a degree. Lowering the spray duty lowers the average air demand, and the calculator reflects that. But at 30–40% duty you will pause constantly while the tank recovers, and the pattern can change between passes. For anything over about 100 sq ft, a properly sized compressor is worth the rental.

What PSI should I set for ceiling texture?

The tool uses these gun ranges: knockdown 40–65 PSI, orange peel 50–70, splatter or skip-trowel 45–75, smooth spray 35–60 and popcorn 60–90. Start at the low end and raise pressure gradually until the pattern looks right. Never go above the rating of your hose, gun or fittings.

Does hose length really change the compressor I need?

Yes. Longer hose loses more pressure, so the compressor has to supply more air to hold the same pressure at the gun. The tool adds 3% for up to 25 ft, 6% up to 50 ft, 12% up to 100 ft and 18% up to 150 ft, assuming ⅜″ hose. Moving the compressor closer to the room is often cheaper than buying a bigger one.

How does elevation affect my air compressor?

Thinner air means the pump moves less mass of air per stroke. A rule of thumb is a loss of about 3% per 1,000 ft. At 5,000 ft the tool raises your CFM requirement by about 18%.

Can two hopper guns run off one compressor?

Only if the compressor covers the combined demand. The tool multiplies demand by the number of guns. Two knockdown guns at 70% duty with a 50 ft hose need a minimum of 11.9 CFM and a recommended 14.9 CFM, with a tank of at least 40 gallons. A 60-gallon two-stage unit rated 14.0 CFM shows as marginal, and an 80-gallon two-stage unit rated 17.0 CFM is the first common size that clears the recommendation.
