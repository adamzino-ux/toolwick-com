How to Use This Ridge Vent Length Calculator

Enter your attic's floor area, either as length × width or as a single square-footage figure. Pick the ventilation ratio, type in the net free area (NFA) printed on your ridge vent's packaging, and set how much of the ventilation you want at the ridge. The calculator returns the ridge vent length to install, the number of vent pieces to buy, and the minimum soffit intake area that must balance it. If you enter your actual ridge length, it also checks whether the vent will fit.

Why This Matters

A poorly ventilated attic costs real money. In summer, an unvented attic can reach 130–150°F, which bakes shingles from below, shortens their life by years, and pushes heat into the rooms under it. In winter, warm moist air leaking from the living space condenses on cold roof sheathing, causing mold, rotted rafters, and ice dams at the eaves. Proper ventilation moves that air out before damage starts.

Ridge vents are popular because they run along the whole peak and exhaust air evenly from both roof slopes, with no spinning parts. But they only work when sized correctly and paired with enough intake at the soffits. A typical 1,600 sq ft attic needs roughly 768 square inches of total net free area at 1:300. A 40 ft ridge vent rated at 9 sq in per foot delivers 360 square inches, so a short ridge can leave you well under the target. Homeowners replacing a roof, roofers writing a bid, and inspectors checking a job all hit this question. Overestimating wastes little. Underestimating leaves the attic hot and damp.

How It's Calculated

The calculator follows the attic ventilation rule in the International Residential Code, section R806.2 (2021 edition). The required net free ventilating area is 1/150 of the vented attic floor area. That drops to 1/300 if either (1) a Class I or II vapor retarder is on the warm side of the ceiling in climate zones 6, 7 and 8, or (2) 40–50% of the required area is in upper vents at least 3 ft above the eave vents, with the rest at the eaves. Local codes vary, so confirm what your jurisdiction has adopted.

Total NFA (sq in) = attic area (sq ft) ÷ ratio × 144
Ridge NFA (sq in) = total NFA × ridge share
Ridge length (ft) = ridge NFA ÷ vent NFA per ft
Soffit intake (sq in) = larger of (total − ridge NFA) or installed ridge NFA

Rounding always goes to the safe side. Ridge length rounds up to the next half foot, vent pieces round up to whole units, and the soffit intake is never less than the exhaust you actually install, so the system stays balanced. The "length needed to fit" adds the unvented gap at each ridge end, because the slot is normally stopped short of the gable or hip ends.

Worked Example

Take a gable house with an attic 50 ft long by 32 ft wide, a 50 ft ridge, a vent rated at 9 sq in per linear foot, 6 in gaps at each end, and a 4 ft rigid product. The attic meets the balanced 1:300 conditions, and the ridge share is set to 50%.

  • Attic area: 50 × 32 = 1,600 sq ft.
  • Total NFA: 1,600 ÷ 300 = 5.333 sq ft × 144 = 768 sq in.
  • Ridge share at 50%: 384 sq in.
  • Exact length: 384 ÷ 9 = 42.67 ft, rounded up to 43 ft.
  • Pieces: 43 ÷ 4 = 10.75, so buy 11 sections (44 ft).
  • Soffit intake: the larger of 384 and 43 × 9 = 387, so at least 387 sq in.
  • Fit check: the usable ridge is 50 − 1 = 49 ft, which gives 441 sq in, enough.

Switch the ratio to 1:150 and the requirement doubles to about 85.5 ft. A 50 ft ridge can no longer do it alone, and you would need a higher-NFA vent or added exhaust.

Tips & Common Mistakes

  • Use the right area. Use the attic floor (ceiling) area under the vented space, not the roof surface area. Separate attic sections each need their own calculation.
  • Check per-side vs total NFA. Some packaging lists NFA per side. Doubling a per-side rating by mistake halves your real vent length.
  • Do not skip the intake. A ridge vent with blocked or missing soffit vents pulls air from the living space or through the roof. Keep baffles clear of insulation.
  • Do not mix vent types. Combining ridge vents with gable-end fans or powered vents can short-circuit airflow. Use one exhaust system.
  • Stop the slot short of the ends. Follow the manufacturer's instructions on slot width and end gaps. They affect weather resistance and shingle support.
  • Round up, not down. If your number lands close to a whole or half foot, take the longer option.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long should a ridge vent be?

It depends on attic size and the vent's rating. A 1,600 sq ft attic at 1:300 with a 9 sq in/ft vent needs about 43 ft. Higher-NFA products need less length. Enter your numbers above for an exact figure.

How much net free area does a ridge vent provide per foot?

Most ridge vents provide about 9 to 20 square inches of NFA per linear foot, depending on the product and slot width. Always use the manufacturer's published figure. If you cannot find it, assume 9 to stay conservative.

Do I need soffit vents if I have a ridge vent?

Yes. A ridge vent only exhausts air, so replacement air must enter at the eaves. Code balances the two, with the lower intake supplying roughly half of the required area or more.

Can I use 1:300 instead of 1:150?

Only if the code conditions are met, such as a balanced system with 40–50% at the upper vents, or a qualifying vapor retarder in climate zones 6–8. Otherwise use 1:150. Your local building department has the final say.

What if my roof has a short ridge, such as a hip roof?

Add up the length of every ridge and hip run that can take vent. If the total is too short, use a higher-NFA ridge product and add supplemental exhaust such as low-profile box vents. Keep the intake balanced with the total exhaust.

Should a ridge vent run the entire length of the ridge?

No. It stops a few inches short of each end, and you only need the length the calculation requires. If you install more, increase the soffit intake to match, since exhaust above 50% of the total can unbalance the system.
