Why This Matters

Epoxy is not cheap, and it is not forgiving. A 100% solids kit can cost $60 to $120 per gallon, so a typical 20×20 ft two-car garage easily runs several hundred dollars in material before flakes and topcoat. Once you mix part A and part B, you usually have 20 to 40 minutes of working time, which means you cannot pause the job to drive to the store. Come up one gallon short on a 400 sq ft floor and you end up with a visible lap line, a thin patch, or a second batch from a different lot that doesn't match.

Overbuying hurts too: opened epoxy can't be stored, so every spare gallon is money in the bin. Homeowners planning a weekend project, contractors writing a bid, house flippers pricing a garage refresh, and shop owners coating a 1,200 sq ft work bay all need the same answer: how much, to the gallon, before ordering. Porous or old concrete soaks up the first coat, and every pour wastes some material in the bucket and on the roller, so a simple area divided by coverage figure is often short. This calculator builds those real-world allowances in.

How It's Calculated

Everything starts with the net area you will coat. Each layer is then divided by its coverage rate, the first epoxy coat is increased for absorption, and a waste percentage is added.

Net area = (Length × Width) − Excluded area
Base epoxy (gal) = Net ÷ Rate × (Coats + Absorption) × (1 + Waste)
Topcoat (gal) = Net ÷ Topcoat rate × Topcoat coats × (1 + Waste)
Flakes (lb) = Net ÷ (sq ft per lb) × (1 + Waste)
Film thickness (mils) = 1,604 ÷ Coverage rate

The 1,604 figure comes from the physics of liquid coatings: one US gallon spread at a thickness of 1 mil (0.001 in.) covers 1,604 sq ft. Absorption applies to the first coat only (0%, 10% or 25%) because once the pores are filled, later coats spread at the printed rate. Purchase quantities are always rounded up to whole gallons or pounds.

No building code sets epoxy coverage. This tool follows the manufacturer-style rule of thumb above and typical published coverage ranges, which differ by product, so the manufacturer's data sheet always governs. Local VOC regulations may restrict some solvent-based products. The result is a material estimate, not engineering, inspection or warranty approval.

Worked Example

A 20 × 20 ft two-car garage with nothing excluded, 100% solids epoxy at 150 sq ft per gallon, one base coat, average ground concrete (+10%), full-broadcast flakes, one clear topcoat at 250 sq ft per gallon and 10% waste. These are the tool's default values.

  1. Net area: 20 × 20 = 400 sq ft.
  2. Base epoxy: 400 ÷ 150 = 2.667 gal per coat. Multiply by (1 + 0.10) for absorption = 2.933 gal. Add 10% waste: 3.227 gal, so buy 4 gallons (about 15.1 L).
  3. Topcoat: 400 ÷ 250 = 1.6 gal. Add 10% waste: 1.76 gal, so buy 2 gallons (about 7.6 L).
  4. Flakes: 400 ÷ 5 = 80 lb. Add 10% waste: 88 lb, so buy 88 lb.
  5. Film thickness: 1,604 ÷ 150 ≈ 10.7 mils for the epoxy and 1,604 ÷ 250 ≈ 6.4 mils for the topcoat, before absorption.

Switching the same floor to water-based epoxy at 250 sq ft per gallon needs 1.936 gal, so 2 gallons. That is cheaper per gallon, but it builds a much thinner film.

Tips & Common Mistakes

  • Use the data sheet rate. Coverage for 100% solids products ranges roughly from 100 to 200 sq ft per gallon. Type in your product's rate and pick "Custom" if it differs from the presets.
  • Test for moisture first. A common rule of thumb is to tape an 18-inch square of clear plastic to the slab for 16 to 24 hours (the idea behind ASTM D4263). Condensation or a dark patch means moisture is coming through, and epoxy applied over it can peel.
  • Grind, don't just etch. Diamond grinding gives a more consistent profile than acid etching, and the profile affects how much the first coat absorbs.
  • Split odd shapes. For an L-shaped garage, run each rectangle separately and add the totals, or enter the notch as excluded area.
  • Don't skip the topcoat on flakes. A full-broadcast floor needs a clear topcoat to lock in the flakes and make the surface smooth and cleanable. Always ventilate, wear a respirator rated for the product, and stay inside the temperature window on the label.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much epoxy do I need for a 2-car garage?

A typical two-car garage is 400 sq ft. With 100% solids epoxy, one coat, average concrete and 10% waste, you need about 3.2 gallons, so buy 4. Water-based epoxy needs less per coat but is thinner, so you may want two coats.

How many square feet does a gallon of epoxy cover?

It depends on the type. Water-based epoxy often covers 200 to 300 sq ft per gallon, solvent-based roughly 150 to 250, and 100% solids around 100 to 200. Use the exact figure printed on your product's data sheet.

Do I need two coats of epoxy on a garage floor?

Many 100% solids systems use one base coat plus flakes and a clear topcoat. Thinner water-based products generally need two coats for a durable film. Very porous concrete may also need an extra coat or a primer.

How much extra epoxy does porous concrete need?

New or smooth concrete absorbs little, while old, rough or poorly ground slabs can absorb 25% more on the first coat. This calculator lets you add 0%, 10% or 25% to the first coat only, which is a conservative rule of thumb.

How many pounds of flakes do I need?

For a full broadcast, plan on about 1 lb per 5 sq ft, so a 400 sq ft garage needs roughly 80 lb plus waste. Light scatter uses far less, around 1 lb per 20 sq ft. Coverage varies with flake size, so confirm with your supplier.

How long before I can drive on a new epoxy floor?

Most systems allow foot traffic after 12 to 24 hours and light use within 2 to 3 days. Many manufacturers recommend about 5 to 7 days before parking vehicles. Cool temperatures extend these times, so follow the product label.
