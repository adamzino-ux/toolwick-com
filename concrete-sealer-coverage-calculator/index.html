Choose your units and slab shape, then enter the dimensions. Pick the sealer type to load a typical label coverage rate, or select "Custom" and type the rate printed on your product's data sheet. You get the total volume with waste, a rounded-up list of containers to buy, an optional cost estimate, and a comparison showing how much more you would need on a rougher surface.

Why This Matters

Sealer is one of the few concrete products where running short mid-job causes visible damage. If you stop halfway across a driveway, you can leave a lap line, and a second batch may not match the sheen of the first. Overbuying hurts too: a 5-gallon pail of quality penetrating or epoxy sealer can easily cost $100 to $250, and opened pails of solvent-based products don't keep forever.

The trap is that coverage on the label assumes a smooth, tight surface. A 600 sq ft broom-finished driveway sealed twice with a product rated at 250 sq ft per gallon looks like 4.8 gallons on paper. At a realistic 212.5 sq ft per gallon it is closer to 5.65 gallons, which is the difference between one pail and two. Homeowners resealing a 20 × 20 ft patio, contractors bidding a pool deck, and property managers maintaining sidewalks and garage aprons all need the same thing: a number they can order with confidence, based on the actual surface and the number of coats.

How It's Calculated

The calculator follows the same rule-of-thumb method most sealer manufacturers and contractors use. Label coverage figures typically range from about 100 to 300 sq ft per gallon, depending on the product and the surface.

  • Area: rectangle = length × width; circle = π × (diameter ÷ 2)²; or the area you enter directly.
  • Effective rate = label rate × texture factor. The factors are 1.00 for smooth troweled concrete, 0.85 for a light broom finish, 0.70 for stamped or exposed aggregate, and 0.60 for rough, weathered or very porous concrete. These are practical rules of thumb, not published standards.
  • Per coat = area ÷ effective rate.
  • Total = per coat × number of coats. Later coats are assumed to cover no better than the first, which is the conservative choice.
  • With waste = total × (1 + waste %).

The result is then rounded up to real container sizes: 5-gallon pails and 1-gallon jugs in imperial, or 20 L pails and 5 L cans in metric. The calculator never rounds down. Metric conversion uses 1 sq ft per gallon ≈ 0.0245 m² per liter. This is an estimate only: your product's technical data sheet and local conditions always take priority.

Worked Example

A 24 ft × 20 ft broom-finished garage apron, sealed with an acrylic sealer rated at 250 sq ft per gallon, two coats, 10% waste. These are the tool's default inputs.

  1. Area = 24 × 20 = 480 sq ft.
  2. Effective rate = 250 × 0.85 = 212.5 sq ft per gallon.
  3. Per coat = 480 ÷ 212.5 = 2.26 gal.
  4. Two coats = 2.26 × 2 = 4.52 gal.
  5. Add 10% waste = 4.52 × 1.10 = 4.97 gal.
  6. Round up to containers: 4.97 gal is just under one pail, so buy 1 × 5-gallon pail. At $40 per gallon that is $200.00.

On smooth concrete the same job would need only 4.22 gal with waste, so the texture adjustment matters. If you sprayed rather than rolled, you might raise waste to 15–20%, which would push this job toward a second container.

Tips & Common Mistakes

  • Trust the data sheet over the front of the can. Use "Custom" and enter the rate for your exact product and surface. Marketing rates are often best case.
  • Measure everything you will coat. Include steps, borders and aprons, and don't subtract small gaps or control joints; edges and joints soak up extra sealer.
  • Two thin coats beat one heavy coat. Thick film-forming acrylics can turn cloudy or white and peel. Follow the label's recoat window.
  • Buy it all at once. Different batches can vary slightly in color and gloss. Check that the batch numbers match.
  • Add waste for sprayers and porous slabs. Sprayers lose material to overspray, and old concrete drinks the first coat. Use 15–20% in those cases.
  • Check cure time, weather and ventilation. Many products need new concrete to cure for about 28 days, a dry surface and moderate temperatures. Solvent-based sealers need good ventilation and no open flames.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much concrete sealer do I need for 1,000 square feet?

With an acrylic sealer at 250 sq ft per gallon, one coat needs 4 gallons and two coats need 8 gallons. Adding 10% waste brings that to 8.8 gallons, so plan on nine gallons or two 5-gallon pails. A rough surface will push this higher.

How many square feet does a gallon of concrete sealer cover?

Most products cover about 100 to 300 sq ft per gallon. Thin acrylics on smooth concrete sit at the high end, while epoxy and sealers on porous or stamped concrete sit at the low end. The label for your product is the best source.

Do I need two coats of concrete sealer?

It depends on the product. Many acrylic and polyurethane sealers call for two thin coats, while penetrating silane or siloxane sealers are often applied as a single saturating coat. Follow the manufacturer's instructions, and set the coats slider to match.

How much sealer do I need for a driveway?

A typical two-car driveway of 20 × 20 ft is 400 sq ft. At an effective rate of 200 sq ft per gallon, one coat needs 2 gallons, or 2.2 gallons with 10% waste. Measure your actual driveway, including any apron or walkway you plan to seal.

Does stamped or exposed aggregate concrete use more sealer?

Yes. Texture increases the real surface area and creates low spots that hold product, so coverage per gallon drops. The 0.70 factor for stamped or exposed aggregate and 0.60 for rough or porous concrete reflect that, and the comparison table shows the difference.

Is it okay to round down if I'm just a little short?

It's better not to. Running out partway through leaves lap marks, and a later batch may not match. The calculator rounds up to full containers so you have a small reserve for touch-ups.
